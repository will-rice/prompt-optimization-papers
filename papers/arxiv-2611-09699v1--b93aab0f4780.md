# Verifier Noise Is a Temperature: Testing Tilt Predictions in Finite-Budget LLM-Guided Search

Xiaomeng Li Affiliation: Link Heart Limited, Houston, TX, USA

Preprint, November 2026

###### Abstract

LLM-guided search loops propose a modified candidate, score it, and keep
it if it scores better. When the score is noisy and both candidates are
re-scored at every comparison, as in the minibatch acceptance gates of
prompt optimizers, keep-if-better is a reversible Markov chain if the
score difference is logistic and the mutation operator is reversible,
and its stationary law is the mutation operator’s equilibrium tilted by
$`e^{\beta f}`$. For any continuous symmetric noise difference the same
tilt is the stationary law of the small-step diffusion limit, with
$`\beta=4F^{\prime}(0)`$, where $`F`$ is the distribution of the noise
difference; for a minibatch gate $`\beta`$ grows with the square root of
the minibatch size. Caching the incumbent’s score instead inflates the
recorded score, and whether iteration beats independent best-of-$`N`$
depends on the noise and quality tails. We test the tilt as a prediction
for LLM-guided program search, committing each prediction from
calibration data alone before the search runs. The level after 150 steps
rises with the minibatch size in six of seven settings, but its
predicted value fails: on bin packing, search attains $`62`$–$`88\%`$ of
the predicted gain when the proposer edits the parent and
$`109`$–$`119\%`$ when it writes new heuristics. The attained fraction
falls with the parent–child quality correlation of unselected mutation;
a registered test on two new settings confirmed the direction, while the
quantitative agreement, for the small-edit setting, did not survive a
correction for proposals shared across conditions. Two further
registered tests found that runs started from a good program kept losing
quality over 300 steps and, in the setting tested, that the loss was
larger from starts that search had selected than from starts of the same
quality visited by unselected walks: after 300 steps the latter were
statistically compatible with the prediction and the former narrowly
below it, a difference that passed its registered rule by a small
margin. Within our budgets, runs from different starts had not reached a
common level, so whether the tilt describes the stationary law of LLM
editing remains open; on bin packing, for editing proposers, it was an
upper estimate of what search from the starting program reached. A
registered test in a second domain, prompt optimisation on frozen
task-model scores, failed: search exceeded the tilt, and the bin-packing
relation between correlation and attained fraction did not transfer.

## 1 Introduction

A growing family of systems improves an artifact by letting a language
model propose edits and keeping the edits that score better: program
search in FunSearch and AlphaEvolve (Romera-Paredes et al., 2024;
Novikov et al., 2025), prompt and pipeline optimisers such as GEPA and
MIPRO (Agrawal et al., 2025; Opsahl-Ong et al., 2024), and
self-refinement loops judged by another model. The score that decides
what is kept is usually noisy: a minibatch of tasks, a sampled workload,
or a stochastic LLM judge. Practitioners respond to this noise in two
ways. Some re-score the incumbent together with each candidate on a
fresh sample, as in GEPA’s minibatch acceptance gate. Others score each
candidate once and store the result, as in program databases.

This paper characterises the first choice exactly and contrasts it with
the second. Our starting observation is classical: when both candidates
are re-scored with independent Gumbel noise, keep-if-better is Barker’s
acceptance rule, and the search is a reversible Markov chain whose
stationary law is the mutation operator’s equilibrium tilted by
$`e^{f/b}`$. This is the logit dynamics of game theory and the
mutation–selection balance of population genetics with “noise scale” in
place of “population size”; that payoff noise raises the temperature of
selection is known for evolutionary games (Traulsen et al., 2007a). We
take it as a lemma and derive what it implies for LLM-guided search:

1.  1.  Any symmetric noise is a temperature in a small-step diffusion
        limit. The limiting diffusion has the tilted law as its stationary
        law, with inverse temperature $`\beta=4F^{\prime}(0)`$, four times
        the density of the score-noise difference at zero (Theorem 1); for
        Gaussian noise of standard deviation $`\sigma`$ per evaluation,
        $`\beta=2/(\sqrt{\pi}\,\sigma)`$, and for a minibatch gate
        $`\beta\propto\sqrt{m}`$.
2.  2.  Under these conditions, re-evaluated search has a computable
        stationary level, $`f^{\star}=\kappa^{\prime}(\beta)`$, the
        derivative of the cumulant generating function of quality under the
        mutation operator’s own equilibrium (Corollary 1). The verifier
        enters only through $`\beta`$ and the LLM only through the
        distribution of quality that unselected mutation produces, which can
        be measured. Whether a finite run reaches this level is a separate,
        empirical question.
3.  3.  Caching the score inflates the reported score and changes the
        dynamics; whether true quality keeps improving depends on the
        mutation operator (with state-independent mutation, caching is
        best-of-$`N`$ and also plateaus). In simulation, cached search is
        ahead of re-evaluation at every budget. In an exploratory LLM run it
        is not ahead within 400 evaluations, while its recorded score
        overstates true quality by up to three times the gain that a larger
        minibatch buys.
4.  4.  The noise tail, together with the quality tail, decides whether
        iterative search can beat independent best-of-$`N`$ at matched
        evaluations: with Gaussian noise and unbounded Gaussian quality,
        best-of-$`N`$ eventually wins and re-evaluated search leads only in
        a window; under Gumbel noise both converge to tilts of different
        base laws (Section 4).

We check these predictions in simulation, in LLM-guided search over
online bin-packing and travelling-salesman heuristics and, in one
registered test, over prompts, in all of which the noise is a random
minibatch of instances and the true quality is the score on all
instances (Section 5.2). Each prediction uses only a calibration run of
the mutation operator without selection, and is committed before the
search is launched. Across seven settings (two tasks; two proposer
models; three mutation instructions) the level after 150 steps rises
with the minibatch size in six, which any account of selection under
noise predicts; the predicted value is what discriminates, and it fails.
On bin packing it is too high by $`0.0008`$–$`0.0024`$ when the proposer
edits the parent, and too low when it writes new heuristics whose
quality barely depends on the parent. The error ordered with the
parent–child quality correlation of unselected mutation, and a
registered test on two new settings at the two ends of the correlation
range confirmed the direction; the quantitative agreement passed its
registered rule in both settings but, in the small-edit setting, not a
correction for proposals shared across minibatch sizes, and with the
uncertainty of the new settings’ calibration and of the fit
approximately propagated it is neither established nor refuted. A third
registered test continued four editing settings to 300 steps: with the
small-edit instruction, runs started from one high-quality program kept
losing quality and met or crossed the runs from below under the
prediction, while with the neutral instruction slow mixing was not
clearly rejected. Because that start had itself been selected by a
search, a fourth registered test started runs from twelve programs of
matched quality, half selected by search and half visited by unselected
walks. It was inconclusive on its primary window (the first 50 steps);
on its secondary window (steps 100–150) the selected starts lost
$`0.0019`$ and the walk-visited starts $`0.0003`$, in the one registered
setting, and a registered continuation found the two groups still apart
after 300 steps, by a margin that passed the registered rule narrowly
and does not survive leaving out most single starts, with the
walk-visited starts statistically compatible with the prediction and the
selected ones just below it. Among the tested starts, whose origin was
not randomised, the quality loss of runs from above is thus associated
with where the start came from, while single-step improvement rates show
no robust difference once a sampling bias in their estimate is removed
and errors are clustered by run. A fifth registered test took the
inheritance relation to a second domain, minibatch-gated prompt
optimisation on frozen task-model scores, and it failed there: in both
settings search exceeded the tilt, the setting with the higher
correlation by more, opposite to bin packing; one of the two settings
met the transferred line, which, with calibration uncertainty included,
means only that the line was not rejected there. Table 4 lists every
registered hypothesis, its verdict and the robustness checks. Within our
budgets runs from different starts had not reached a common level, so we
cannot say whether the tilt is the stationary law of LLM editing; what
the data support is narrower: an effective-temperature proxy for the
gate can be computed from calibration, on bin packing the tilt is an
upper estimate of the level that editing proposers reached from the
starting program in a few hundred steps, and there the inheritance
correlation, measured without selection, indicated how far below or
above it the runs ended; neither statement carried over to the prompt
domain.

## 2 Setting

Let $`\mathcal{X}`$ be a space of candidates (programs, prompts, plans)
with true quality $`f:\mathcal{X}\to\mathbb{R}`$, the quantity one would
measure with unlimited evaluation. A _mutation operator_—in practice an
LLM prompted with the incumbent—is a Markov kernel $`K(x,\mathrm{d}y)`$.
We write $`\pi_{0}`$ for its equilibrium: the distribution that repeated
mutation _without selection_ converges to.

###### Definition 1 (Re-evaluated keep-if-better search, regime R).

Given incumbent $`x_{t}`$, draw $`y\sim K(x_{t},\cdot)`$, score both on
fresh noise, $`\hat{s}(x_{t})=f(x_{t})+\varepsilon`$,
$`\hat{s}(y)=f(y)+\varepsilon^{\prime}`$, and set $`x_{t+1}=y`$ if
$`\hat{s}(y)>\hat{s}(x_{t})`$, else $`x_{t+1}=x_{t}`$.

With $`D=\varepsilon-\varepsilon^{\prime}`$ and $`\Delta=f(y)-f(x)`$,
the acceptance probability is
$`a(x,y)=\Pr(D<\Delta)=F_{xy}(\Delta^{-})`$, the left limit at
$`\Delta`$ of the distribution function $`F_{xy}`$ of $`D`$; we write
$`F_{xy}(\Delta)`$ where $`F_{xy}`$ is continuous. If the two noise
draws are exchangeable and $`D`$ has a continuous law,
$`F_{xy}(\Delta)+F_{xy}(-\Delta)=1`$. This covers i.i.d. continuous
noise. It does not cover paired minibatch gates exactly, where parent
and child are scored on the same freshly sampled minibatch of $`m`$
tasks: there $`-D`$ is the minibatch mean of the per-task score
differences minus its population mean, whose law is that of the actual
differences; it need not be symmetric for small $`m`$ (with
child-minus-parent differences $`-1,-1,2`$ on three tasks and $`m=1`$,
the child wins with probability $`1/3`$ although $`\Delta=0`$), and it
has atoms when scores are discrete. For large $`m`$ it is approximately
Gaussian with standard deviation $`\tau_{xy}/\sqrt{m}`$, and the
symmetric formulas below apply to it as an approximation (Section 3.2).

#### Regime C (cached).

The incumbent keeps the score it was accepted with, and a proposal is
accepted if its single fresh score exceeds that cached value. This is
how program databases in FunSearch-style systems operate. The comparison
is no longer symmetric: the cached score is the maximum of the noisy
scores the chain has accepted, so it is biased upward (the winner’s
curse).

#### Independent sampling (IID).

Draw $`N`$ candidates from a fixed generator $`\pi_{\mathrm{root}}`$
(fresh samples from the seed prompt), score each once, and return the
arg-max of the noisy score.

#### Budget.

We compare methods at a matched number of _evaluations_: IID uses one
per candidate, regime C one per step, regime R two per step.

#### Assumption (reversibility).

For the results of Section 3 we assume $`K`$ is reversible with respect
to $`\pi_{0}`$. This holds for the Gaussian autoregressive kernels used
in our simulations, and is the standard modelling assumption under which
mutation-selection balance is analysed in population genetics. LLM
mutation operators are not reversible in general; Section 5 tests how
much the predictions depend on this assumption.

## 3 Stationary law of re-evaluated search

### 3.1 Exact tilt under Gumbel noise

###### Lemma 1 (Barker acceptance gives an exponential tilt).

If $`a(x,y)=\sigma\big(\beta(f(y)-f(x))\big)`$ with
$`\sigma(u)=1/(1+e^{-u})`$, $`K`$ is $`\pi_{0}`$-reversible, and
$`Z(\beta)=\int e^{\beta f}\,\mathrm{d}\pi_{0}<\infty`$, then regime R
is reversible with respect to

```math
\pi_{\beta}(\mathrm{d}x)=Z(\beta)^{-1}e^{\beta f(x)}\,\pi_{0}(\mathrm{d}x).
```

###### Proof.

For $`x\neq y`$,
$`\pi_{\beta}(x)K(x,y)a(x,y)=Z^{-1}\pi_{0}(x)K(x,y)\,e^{\beta f(x)}\sigma(\beta\Delta)`$
with $`\Delta=f(y)-f(x)`$. Since
$`\sigma(\beta\Delta)=e^{\beta\Delta}\sigma(-\beta\Delta)`$ and
$`\pi_{0}(x)K(x,y)=\pi_{0}(y)K(y,x)`$, this equals
$`\pi_{\beta}(y)K(y,x)a(y,x)`$. ∎

The difference of two independent Gumbel variables of scale $`b`$ is
logistic of scale $`b`$ (McFadden, 1974; Yellott, 1977), so i.i.d.
Gumbel score noise gives exactly Barker’s rule (Barker, 1965) with
$`\beta=1/b`$. The same composition—noise re-drawn at each pairwise
comparison, logistic choice, Gibbs stationary law—is classical in logit
dynamics (Blume, 1993; Alós-Ferrer and Netzer, 2010) and, with
population size in place of $`1/b`$, in population genetics (Iwasa,
1988; Sella and Hirsh, 2005). We state it as a lemma; the contribution
of this section is what it implies for LLM-guided search and how far it
extends beyond the logistic case.

An exact tilt for every reversible $`K`$ requires
$`a(x,y)/a(y,x)=e^{\beta\Delta}`$, i.e. a logistic noise difference. For
other noise, regime R is not in general reversible with respect to any
tilt of $`\pi_{0}`$, and we need a limit.

### 3.2 Small steps: only the slope at zero matters

###### Theorem 1 (Small-step tilt).

Let $`\mathcal{X}=\mathbb{R}^{n}`$, let $`K_{h}`$ be
$`\pi_{0}`$-reversible kernels with step covariance $`h^{2}I+o(h^{2})`$
and $`\mathbb{E}|y-x|^{3}=O(h^{3})`$ uniformly for $`x`$ in compact
sets, whose generators satisfy
$`h^{-2}(K_{h}-I)g\to\frac{1}{2}(\Delta g+\nabla\log\pi_{0}\cdot\nabla g)`$
for smooth $`g`$, let $`f`$ be smooth with bounded first and second
derivatives, and let $`a(x,y)=F(f(y)-f(x))`$ with $`F`$ differentiable
at $`0`$ and $`F(\Delta)+F(-\Delta)=1`$. Then the generator of regime R,
rescaled by $`h^{-2}`$, converges to

```math
\mathcal{L}g=\tfrac{1}{4}\Big(\Delta g+\nabla\big(\log\pi_{0}+4F^{\prime}(0)f\big)\cdot\nabla g\Big),
```

a Langevin diffusion with stationary law
$`\pi_{\beta}\propto e^{\beta f}\pi_{0}`$, $`\beta=4F^{\prime}(0)`$,
whenever $`\pi_{\beta}`$ is normalisable. Convergence is of generators
on smooth compactly supported $`g`$; the Gaussian autoregressive kernels
of Example 1 satisfy the moment conditions locally uniformly, not
uniformly on $`\mathbb{R}^{n}`$, since their mean step grows linearly in
$`x`$.

###### Proof sketch.

Write $`F(\Delta)=\tfrac{1}{2}+F^{\prime}(0)\Delta+o(\Delta)`$. Then
$`\mathbb{E}[(g(y)-g(x))a(x,y)]=\tfrac{1}{2}\mathbb{E}[g(y)-g(x)]+F^{\prime}(0)\mathbb{E}[(g(y)-g(x))(f(y)-f(x))]+o(h^{2})`$.
The first term is $`\tfrac{1}{2}(K_{h}-I)g`$; the second is
$`F^{\prime}(0)\,h^{2}\,\nabla g\cdot\nabla f+o(h^{2})`$ because the
steps have identity covariance at leading order. Dividing by $`h^{2}`$
gives $`\mathcal{L}`$. Details are in Appendix A. ∎

For Gaussian score noise with standard deviation $`\sigma`$ on each
evaluation, $`F(\Delta)=\Phi\big(\Delta/(\sqrt{2}\,\sigma)\big)`$ and

```math
\beta=\frac{2}{\sqrt{\pi}\,\sigma}\approx 1.128/\sigma.
```

For logistic noise, Theorem 1 recovers Lemma 1, and the two uses of
$`\beta`$ coincide. The theorem is the stationary-law counterpart of the
_universality of weak selection_ of Wu et al. (2010), who show that
fixation probabilities of pairwise-comparison processes depend on the
comparison function only through its slope at zero. The often-used
probit-to-logit constant ($`1.702/\sqrt{2}\approx 1.2035`$ per unit SNR)
is the best global logistic fit, not the small-step limit; the two
differ by about 7%.

#### Minibatch gates.

If the noise comes from scoring parent and child on a fresh minibatch of
$`m`$ tasks with per-task difference standard deviation $`\tau`$, then
$`\sigma_{\text{diff}}=\tau/\sqrt{m}`$ and, since $`F`$ now refers to
the _difference_,

```math
\beta(m)=\frac{4}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\cdot\frac{\sqrt{m}}{\tau}\approx 1.596\,\frac{\sqrt{m}}{\tau}.
```

The minibatch size is a temperature knob: $`\beta\propto\sqrt{m}`$. If
the minibatch is drawn without replacement from a fixed pool of $`N`$
tasks, the difference has s.d. $`(\tau/\sqrt{m})\sqrt{(N-m)/(N-1)}`$,
and $`\beta(m)`$ is multiplied by $`\sqrt{(N-1)/(N-m)}`$. With discrete
scores, a strict gate rejects ties in both directions, so
$`a(x,y)+a(y,x)=1-p_{xy}`$ with $`p_{xy}`$ the tie probability. Because
$`p_{xy}=p_{yx}`$, ties cancel in the ratio $`a(x,y)/a(y,x)`$ that
enters detailed balance. If $`p_{xy}`$ is roughly constant over the
pairs the chain visits, they only slow the chain, and $`\beta`$ is
$`4G^{\prime}(0)`$ for the law $`G`$ of the difference conditioned on no
tie.

#### The minibatch formula is an approximation.

It is not a corollary of Theorem 1 without further assumptions. The
theorem uses one acceptance function $`F`$ for all pairs. A paired gate
has a pair-dependent $`F_{xy}`$: its scale $`\tau_{xy}`$ varies across
parent–child pairs, the minibatch mean of per-task differences need not
be symmetric about its mean for small $`m`$, and discrete scores create
ties. Edges with different log-odds slopes are not described by one
$`\beta`$, and averaging $`\beta_{xy}`$ does not restore a Gibbs
stationary law. There is also a scaling issue. If per-task scores vary
smoothly with the candidate, both the mean and the s.d. of the paired
difference are $`O(h)`$ for steps of size $`h`$, so the signal-to-noise
ratio of a comparison need not vanish as $`h\to 0`$, and the fixed-$`F`$
expansion does not literally apply. We therefore treat $`\beta(m)`$ as
an approximation whose quality we measure (Section 5.2): it requires
roughly Gaussian minibatch means and a $`\tau_{xy}`$ that varies little
over the pairs the chain visits.

### 3.3 The plateau

###### Corollary 1 (Plateau).

Under the conditions of Lemma 1 or Theorem 1, and if
$`\mathbb{E}_{\pi_{0}}\big[|f|e^{\beta f}\big]<\infty`$, the stationary
mean quality of regime R (under Theorem 1, of its small-step diffusion
limit) is

```math
f^{\star}(\beta)=\mathbb{E}_{\pi_{\beta}}[f]=\kappa^{\prime}(\beta),\qquad\kappa(\beta)=\log\mathbb{E}_{\pi_{0}}\big[e^{\beta f}\big],
```

the derivative of the cumulant generating function of quality under the
mutation equilibrium. If $`\mathbb{E}_{\pi_{0}}e^{tf}<\infty`$ for $`t`$
in a neighbourhood of $`[0,\beta]`$, $`\kappa`$ is analytic there and
$`f^{\star}(\beta)=\mu_{0}+\beta\sigma_{0}^{2}+\tfrac{\beta^{2}}{2}\kappa_{3}+\dots`$
for small $`\beta`$, with $`\mu_{0},\sigma_{0}^{2},\kappa_{3}`$ the
mean, variance and third cumulant of $`f`$ under $`\pi_{0}`$. If $`f`$
is Gaussian under $`\pi_{0}`$, $`f^{\star}=\mu_{0}+\beta\sigma_{0}^{2}`$
exactly.

###### Proof.

$`\mathbb{E}_{\pi_{\beta}}[f]=\mathbb{E}_{\pi_{0}}[fe^{\beta f}]/\mathbb{E}_{\pi_{0}}[e^{\beta f}]=\kappa^{\prime}(\beta)`$.
∎

Two practical readings follow. First, the plateau depends on the
mutation operator only through the distribution of quality under its own
equilibrium, which can be estimated by running the operator _without
selection_. To first order a stronger LLM raises the plateau by raising
$`\mu_{0}`$ (it drifts toward better candidates on its own) or
$`\sigma_{0}^{2}`$ (it explores a wider range), and a better verifier
raises it through $`\beta`$. The cumulant expansion is useful only when
$`\beta\sigma_{0}`$ is small; in our LLM experiments $`\beta\sigma_{0}`$
is between $`1.4`$ and $`27`$, on bin packing the Gaussian form
$`\mu_{0}+\beta\sigma_{0}^{2}`$ exceeds the maximum possible score of
$`1`$ at $`m=64`$ in every setting and from $`m=16`$ on in four of six,
and we test the full tilt $`\mathbb{E}_{\pi_{0}e^{\beta f}}f`$ instead.
Second, Lemma 1 shows that $`\pi_{\beta}`$ is invariant, not that the
chain converges to it from a given start; that needs more, for instance
irreducibility and aperiodicity on a finite space. A proposal that never
moves between two classes of candidates keeps the chain in the class it
starts in, whose own tilt can lie far below the global one. Once the
chain has mixed (which our LLM experiments could not establish within
their budgets; Section 5.2), the marginal mean quality of the _current
incumbent_ no longer increases with the number of steps: extra steps
only resample from $`\pi_{\beta}`$. This is not an upper bound from
every initial condition (a chain started above $`f^{\star}`$ drifts down
to it), and it does not constrain an output rule that keeps the best
candidate ever visited, whose selection would itself need evaluations.

###### Example 1 (Autoregressive mutation with drift).

Let quality itself be the state,
$`y=\rho x+\delta+\sqrt{1-\rho^{2}}\,z`$ with $`z\sim N(0,1)`$. This
kernel is reversible with respect to $`\pi_{0}=N(\delta/(1-\rho),1)`$,
so under Gumbel noise $`\pi_{\beta}=N(\delta/(1-\rho)+1/b,\,1)`$
exactly, and $`f^{\star}=1/b+\delta/(1-\rho)`$: a verifier term plus a
mutation term. The correlation $`\rho`$ of the mutation affects the
plateau only through the drift term; it mainly sets how fast the plateau
is reached.

### 3.4 Large steps: slow mixing

For non-logistic noise and large steps the small-step constant no longer
applies; the stationary law of regime R, where it exists, is then not in
general a tilt of $`\pi_{0}`$, and in the Gaussian autoregressive
settings of Table 1 its mean lies above
$`\kappa^{\prime}(4F^{\prime}(0))`$. Convergence to it can also become
very slow. In the autoregressive example with $`\rho=0.5`$ and Gaussian
noise of s.d. $`1/3`$, the stationary mean computed on a grid is
$`3.996`$ ($`=1.332\,d`$) for grids reaching $`x=20`$, $`30`$ and
$`40`$. Simulated search reaches it only slowly: divided by $`d`$, the
mean of 20,000 chains is $`1.25`$, $`1.32`$, $`1.33`$ and $`1.33`$ after
$`600`$, $`2{,}500`$, $`10{,}000`$ and $`40{,}000`$ steps. At
$`\rho=0.9`$ and the same noise it is flat at $`1.22`$ from 600 steps
on. A numerical warning applies to the grid computation. At high quality
the probability of leaving a grid state underflows, so many eigenvalues
of the transition matrix are numerically equal to one. An eigen-solver
can then return a vector concentrated on these numerically absorbing
states and suggest that no stationary law exists. We use power iteration
started from the bulk, which avoided this in our computations (its
results agree across grid ranges and with simulation); it does not
exclude truncation effects or very slow mixing in general. Whether
regime R has a stationary law in every large-step, non-logistic setting
remains open. Corollary 1 is stated where it exists.

## 4 Caching, best-of-$`N`$, and the role of the noise tail

### 4.1 Caching changes the dynamics and inflates the reported score

In regime C the acceptance threshold is the incumbent’s _recorded_ score
$`s_{t}`$, which only ever increases. The incumbent $`x_{t}`$ alone is
no longer a Markov chain. The pair $`(x_{t},s_{t})`$ is a
time-homogeneous Markov chain whose score component is non-decreasing.
With noise unbounded above, such as Gumbel or Gaussian noise, and
proposals whose true quality stays above some fixed level, the recorded
score grows without bound and the pair has no stationary law; with
bounded scores it can be absorbed at the largest attainable score. In
either case Corollary 1 does not apply. This does _not_ imply that true
quality keeps improving. If the mutation ignores the current state,
$`K(x,\cdot)=\pi_{0}`$, regime C is exactly best-of-$`N`$ over
independent draws from $`\pi_{0}`$. Under Gumbel noise, and for bounded
or Gaussian quality, its expected true quality then converges to the
tilt mean $`\kappa_{0}^{\prime}(1/b)`$ (Proposition 1a), a plateau,
while the recorded score grows without bound. Whether true quality under
caching keeps rising therefore depends on the mutation operator and the
noise; what is general is the inflation of the recorded score. Two
effects compete. Selection keeps acting, because every accepted proposal
has to beat a threshold that rises with the best score seen so far. The
threshold, however, is inflated by the noise of the lucky draw that set
it (Arnold and Beyer, 2002; Smith and Winkler, 2006), so the gap between
recorded score and true quality grows and accepted moves become rarer.
In the autoregressive model with $`d=1`$, the recorded score exceeds the
incumbent’s true quality by $`1.98`$, $`2.89`$ and $`3.62`$ after
$`32`$, $`512`$ and $`8{,}192`$ evaluations. With $`d=3`$ the gaps are
$`0.47`$, $`0.74`$ and $`0.96`$ (sim_inflation.py). In the
autoregressive simulation (Section 5.1), and within the budgets we test,
the true quality of regime C keeps rising, leads regime R throughout and
overtakes IID. This is an observation for that model, not a general law;
the reported score overstates true quality by an amount that grows with
the noise and with the budget. Hu and Tang (2026) report related
evidence for prompt optimisation (Section 6). A practical asymmetry
matters as much as caching itself: a re-evaluated gate can score parent
and child on the same tasks, which cancels shared task difficulty, while
a cached comparison cannot (Section 5.4). We do not have a closed-form
growth law for regime C and present it as an empirical finding.

### 4.2 Independent sampling and the noise tail

For IID best-of-$`N`$, the returned quality depends on how the noise
tail compares with the quality tail. We state two exact cases and one
heuristic one.

###### Proposition 1 (Best-of-$`N`$ under two noise tails).

Let $`f\sim\pi_{\mathrm{root}}`$ and independent noise $`\varepsilon`$.
(a) _Gumbel noise of scale $`b`$:_ if
$`0<Z=\mathbb{E}_{\pi_{\mathrm{root}}}e^{f/b}<\infty`$, the returned
candidate converges in law, as $`N\to\infty`$, to
$`e^{f/b}\pi_{\mathrm{root}}/Z`$ (Gumbel-max). If moreover $`f`$ is
bounded, or $`f`$ is Gaussian under $`\pi_{\mathrm{root}}`$, the
expected returned quality converges,
$`\mathbb{E}[f]\to\kappa_{\mathrm{root}}^{\prime}(1/b)`$. (b) _Gaussian
noise and Gaussian quality_ ($`f\sim N(\mu,s^{2})`$, noise s.d.
$`\sigma`$): $`\mathbb{E}[f]=\mu+e_{N}\,s^{2}/\sqrt{s^{2}+\sigma^{2}}`$,
with $`e_{N}`$ the expected maximum of $`N`$ standard normals; this
grows without bound like $`\sqrt{2\log N}`$.

###### Remark 1 (Heavy-tailed noise).

Kwa et al. (2024, Thm. 5) show that if the error is subexponential (in
the heavy-tailed sense: the class of distributions whose sum-tail
behaves like the maximum’s) and quality has a lighter tail, then
conditioning on a high noisy score gives no gain:
$`\mathbb{E}[f\mid f+\varepsilon\geq t]\to\mathbb{E}[f]`$ as
$`t\to\infty`$. This is a threshold statement, not a statement about the
arg-max of $`N`$ draws. We expect the analogous best-of-$`N`$ conclusion
to hold, but we do not prove the link, and we do not use it below. Their
positive result (Thm. 6) requires a tail-ratio condition on the noise
and unbounded quality.

Part (a) is the Gumbel-max trick (Verdun et al., 2025). Given the
qualities, the returned one has mean
$`\sum_{i}f_{i}e^{f_{i}/b}/\sum_{j}e^{f_{j}/b}`$, which converges almost
surely to $`\kappa_{\mathrm{root}}^{\prime}(1/b)`$ by the law of large
numbers; for bounded $`f`$, dominated convergence gives the mean. For
Gaussian quality $`N(\mu,s^{2})`$, Gaussian integration by parts gives
the exact identity
$`\mathbb{E}[f_{I_{N}}]=\mu+\beta s^{2}\big(1-\mathbb{E}\sum_{i}p_{i}^{2}\big)`$
with $`\beta=1/b`$ and
$`p_{i}=e^{\beta f_{i}}/\sum_{j}e^{\beta f_{j}}`$, and
$`\sum_{i}p_{i}^{2}\to 0`$ almost surely and in mean, so
$`\mathbb{E}[f_{I_{N}}]\to\mu+\beta s^{2}=\kappa_{\mathrm{root}}^{\prime}(\beta)`$
(Appendix A). A condition on the upper tail alone does not suffice: with
$`f=-e^{1/U}`$, $`U`$ uniform on $`(0,1)`$, every exponential moment
$`\mathbb{E}e^{cf}`$, $`c>0`$, is finite and the tilt has a finite mean,
but the returned quality is at most $`\max_{i}f_{i}`$, whose expectation
is $`-\infty`$ for every $`N`$. Part (b) is the breeder’s equation with
a reliability factor.

#### Consequences for iterative versus independent search.

Combine Proposition 1 with Corollary 1 at matched evaluations. The
comparisons are asymptotic and concern expected quality, so both
procedures must converge in mean. For IID this holds under the
conditions of Proposition 1 (bounded or Gaussian quality in part (a)).
For regime R we assume that the chain converges in law to its stationary
law from the start considered (Lemma 1 gives invariance only; on a
finite space irreducibility and aperiodicity suffice) and that $`f`$ is
bounded or otherwise uniformly integrable along the chain. The outcome
depends jointly on the noise tail, the quality tail and the mutation
equilibrium, not on the noise tail alone.

- •
  _Gumbel noise._ IID and regime R converge to the same kind of limit, a
  tilt at the same $`\beta=1/b`$, but of different base laws:
  $`\pi_{\mathrm{root}}`$ for IID and the mutation equilibrium
  $`\pi_{0}`$ for R. Iteration wins asymptotically if and only if
  $`\kappa_{0}^{\prime}(1/b)>\kappa_{\mathrm{root}}^{\prime}(1/b)`$,
  that is, if the tilted mutation equilibrium beats tilted fresh
  samples.
- •
  _Gaussian noise and unbounded Gaussian quality under
  $`\pi_{\mathrm{root}}`$._ IID grows without bound. If R has a finite
  stationary mean, IID eventually overtakes it at matched evaluations.
  When quality is bounded, as in our bin-packing task, this conclusion
  does not carry over. R can lead only in an intermediate window, and
  only if the plateau $`f^{\star}(\beta)`$ exceeds the IID curve before
  IID catches up. The late crossover is predicted by solving
  $`\mu+e_{E}s^{2}/\sqrt{s^{2}+\sigma^{2}}=f^{\star}(\beta)`$ for $`E`$;
  this gives a _double crossover_ (IID $`\to`$ R $`\to`$ IID) when SNR
  is high.

In practice the late crossover can lie beyond any realistic budget when
the mutation drift is large ($`\mu_{0}\gg\mu`$), which is the regime in
which LLM-guided evolution is used.

### 5.2 LLM-guided program search: pre-registered tests

We test the tilt as a prediction for real LLM search loops. In each
setting the prediction is computed from calibration data alone and
committed to version control before the search is launched; the search
then runs on independent seeds. Five registered tests follow: the level
of the prediction (test 1), a relation between its error and inheritance
found after the first five settings (test 2), slow mixing as an account
of the error (test 3), the role of the start program (test 4), and a
second domain, prompt optimisation, in which the bin-packing relation
was tested out of sample (test 5, Section 5.3). Table 4 lists every
registered hypothesis, its rule and its verdict, together with the
robustness checks of this section. All levels we report are levels over
a fixed window of a finite run; we call them plateaus only where runs
from different starts agree.

#### Tasks.

_Bin packing (BP)._ A program is a Python priority function for online
bin packing (capacity 100; 300 items per instance drawn as
$`45\cdot\mathrm{Weibull}(3)`$). Its score on an instance is
$`L_{1}/\text{bins}`$, where $`L_{1}`$ is the volume lower bound, and
its true quality $`f`$ is the mean over a fixed set of 200 instances.
Search starts from first fit ($`f=0.9430`$). _Travelling salesman
(TSP)._ A program is a priority function that picks the next city of a
tour on 60 uniform random cities. Its score on an instance is the
nearest-neighbour tour length divided by its own tour length, so nearest
neighbour, the starting program, has $`f=1`$; $`f`$ is the mean over 200
fixed instances. TSP scores are real-valued, without the integer bin
counts that make BP scores discrete, but ties still occur: on
calibration pairs $`3.3\%`$ of per-instance differences are exactly zero
and $`0.3\%`$ of pairs have identical score vectors (ties_diag.py). True
quality is measured on the same 200 instances that minibatches are drawn
from: we test the search dynamics on a finite task pool, not
generalisation to new instances.

#### Proposers.

A mutation sends the current program to an LLM with a fixed instruction
and takes the returned program as the child; every proposal is a fresh
sample. Seven settings vary the proposer and the instruction: on BP,
Claude Haiku 5.5 and Claude Sonnet 5.5, each with a neutral instruction
(“write an improved or alternative version”), a small-edit instruction
and a rewrite instruction; on TSP, Haiku with the neutral instruction.
The Sonnet settings with the small-edit and rewrite instructions were
added for test 2. Prompts, sampling and failure handling are in
Appendix C. A child that fails to run is never accepted.

#### Protocol.

For each setting: (1) _Calibration._ 24 no-selection walks of 100 steps,
in which every proposal is accepted, and 600 fresh children of the
starting program. The second halves of the walks (1,224 programs)
estimate the mutation equilibrium $`\pi_{0}`$. The difference scale
$`\tau`$ is the median, over walk parent–child pairs, of the standard
deviation of per-instance score differences. (2) _Prediction._
$`\beta(m)=1.596\sqrt{m}/\tau\cdot\sqrt{(N-1)/(N-m)}`$, where the last
factor corrects for minibatches drawn without replacement from $`N=200`$
instances, and the tilt prediction $`\mathbb{E}_{\pi_{0}e^{\beta f}}f`$
is estimated by reweighting the walk sample. We also record the
effective sample size of the weights,
$`\mathrm{ESS}=(\sum w_{i})^{2}/\sum w_{i}^{2}`$, and a bootstrap error
over walks. The prediction is committed before the search starts. (3)
_Search._ Regime R with $`m\in\{1,4,16,64\}`$, 6 seeds each, 150 steps
(300 for four settings in test 3). The observed level is the mean true
quality of the current incumbent over steps 100–150, with the standard
error over seeds. A step costs two minibatch evaluations, i.e. $`2m`$
instance evaluations, so comparisons across $`m`$ at equal steps are not
at equal cost; we compare levels across $`m`$ and costs only within each
$`m`$. (4) _From above._ For BP with the neutral and small-edit
instructions (Haiku and Sonnet), regime R is also started from one
program of quality $`0.9559`$, above every prediction, which the
exploratory search of Section 5.4 had selected ($`m\in\{1,4,16\}`$, 6
seeds, 150 steps; 300 for the small-edit instruction). (5) _Independent
sampling._ Best of $`E`$ fresh children of the starting program,
selected by a size-$`m`$ minibatch, with $`E=300`$ equal to the
evaluations used by R; the $`E`$ children are drawn without replacement
from the 600 calibration children (failed children, which are never
accepted, excluded), so the estimate is unbiased for $`E`$ fresh valid
samples given that pool. The pool itself is a sample, and the reported
error does not include its variability.

#### How sharp is test 1?

Without selection ($`\beta=0`$) the prediction is the walk mean,
$`0.944`$–$`0.947`$ on BP; a chain that reached the best walk sample
would be at $`0.9554`$–$`0.9556`$. The observed levels lie between
these; with the editing instructions they fall short of the tilt by
$`8`$–$`58\%`$ of the lift, and with the rewrite instruction they exceed
it in seven of eight cells. Doubling $`\beta`$ raises the BP prediction
by $`0.0006`$–$`0.0019`$, so the test can resolve a factor-two error in
$`\beta`$. Two accounts of the shortfall are consistent with the levels
alone: a correct temperature applied to a base law worse than
$`\pi_{0}`$ near the top, or a lower effective temperature. A check of
the acceptance rule on walk pairs, simulating the paired gate with
minibatches drawn without replacement, finds the exact acceptance
log-odds at or above the slope of the Gaussian approximation, within
bootstrap error, at every $`m`$ and in every BP setting (slope ratios
$`0.98`$–$`2.11`$; Appendix D). This removes a flatter acceptance
function as an explanation, on the pairs a walk visits; it does not
address heterogeneity of $`\tau_{xy}`$ over the pairs a search visits.

#### Test 2: inheritance.

After the first five settings, post-hoc checks (Appendix D) found that
neither these re-estimates of $`\tau`$ nor a projected kernel that drops
reversibility removed the error, and showed that the attained fraction
of the lift ordered with the parent–child quality correlation of the
calibration walks: $`0.13`$ (Haiku rewrite), $`0.61`$ (Haiku neutral),
$`0.84`$ (Sonnet neutral), $`0.91`$ (Haiku small edit), against
$`105`$–$`111\%`$, $`86`$–$`92\%`$, $`73`$–$`87\%`$ and $`71`$–$`76\%`$.
Before calibrating two new settings, Sonnet with the rewrite and with
the small-edit instruction, we committed four hypotheses, their decision
rules and the evaluation script. Let $`L`$ be the attained fraction of
the predicted lift, averaged over the cells with ESS at least $`30`$,
and $`\rho`$ the parent–child quality correlation on calibration walk
pairs. On the four BP settings, $`L=1.148-0.434\,\rho`$ with residual
s.d. $`0.020`$. _H1a_: with the rewrite instruction, $`L\geq 0.95`$
(fail if $`L+2\,\mathrm{se}<0.95`$; void if $`\rho>0.3`$). _H1b_: with
the small-edit instruction, $`L\leq 0.85`$ (fail if
$`L-2\,\mathrm{se}>0.85`$; void if $`\rho<0.7`$). _H1q_:
$`|L-\hat{L}(\rho)|\leq 2\sqrt{0.020^{2}+\mathrm{se}^{2}}`$, where
$`\hat{L}(\rho)`$ is computed from the calibration $`\rho`$ and
committed with the tilt prediction, before the search. _H2_: for parents
of quality in $`[0.952,0.957)`$, the probability that the child is
better is at least $`0.05`$ lower for search parents than for walk
parents if $`\rho>0.7`$, and within $`0.05`$ if $`\rho<0.3`$.

Calibration gave $`\rho=-0.01`$ (rewrite) and $`0.88`$ (small edit),
hence $`\hat{L}=1.15`$ and $`0.77`$. Search gave $`L=1.19`$ and
$`0.62`$. Under the registered rules all four hypotheses pass: H1a and
H1b by wide margins, H1q with differences of $`0.04`$ (tolerance
$`0.12`$) and $`0.15`$ (tolerance $`0.17`$), and H2 with $`0.17`$ vs.
$`0.28`$ for the small-edit setting and $`0.02`$ vs. $`0.04`$ for the
rewrite setting. Two of these passes do not survive a correction of the
registered analysis. _H1q._ The registered standard error of $`L`$
treats the cells $`m=1,\dots,64`$ as independent, but within a seed they
share proposals. Averaging the lift over cells within each seed and
taking the standard error over seeds gives $`\mathrm{se}=0.070`$ and
$`0.045`$ instead of $`0.058`$ and $`0.084`$; with the registered rule
the tolerance becomes $`0.15`$ and $`0.10`$, and the small-edit setting
fails ($`0.15>0.10`$). Propagating, approximately, also the uncertainty
of the two settings’ calibration (block bootstrap over walks, through
$`\pi_{0}`$, $`\tau`$ and $`\rho`$) and of the four-point fit (normal
draws from its fixed-design least-squares covariance, which has two
residual degrees of freedom; the four training settings are not
resampled) gives 95% intervals for $`L-\hat{L}`$ of $`[-0.11,+0.21]`$
(rewrite) and $`[-0.36,+0.07]`$ (small edit), dominated by the
calibration walks in the small-edit setting. The quantitative agreement
is therefore neither established nor refuted; the directional hypotheses
H1a and H1b are unaffected. _H2._ The registered statistic pools all
proposals from a parent. A search incumbent receives proposals until one
is accepted, so parents that are hard to improve receive more proposals
and are over-represented; walk parents receive one proposal per visit.
In a toy model in which search and walk parents have the same
distribution of improvement probabilities, the pooled rate is $`0.21`$
for search parents against $`0.25`$ for walk parents
(toy_sampling_bias.py). Using only the first proposal from each parent
in each run, which is drawn before any stopping decision, and excluding
the chains from above (which the registered statistic included), the
small-edit rates are $`0.22`$ vs. $`0.26`$ ($`n=227`$ and $`413`$; a
difference of $`0.046`$, short of the registered $`0.05`$) and the
rewrite rates $`0.02`$ vs. $`0.04`$. Table 4 reports both versions.
_Post-hoc six-setting fit._ Refitting on all six BP settings, after the
test, gives $`L=1.18\,(\pm 0.05)-0.52\,(\pm 0.07)\,\rho`$, with residual
s.d. $`0.065`$ and correlation $`-0.96`$; the line crosses $`L=1`$ at
$`\rho\approx 0.34`$. With six settings, two models and one task, we
read it as an empirical regularity about finite-budget runs: the less a
child inherits, the closer the tilt comes to the observed level after
150 steps and, for children nearly independent of the parent, the more
it underestimates it.

#### What the regularity does and does not say.

Under the Barker acceptance condition of Lemma 1, the tilt of
$`\pi_{0}`$ is the stationary law of every proposal kernel that is
reversible with respect to $`\pi_{0}`$ on program space, whatever the
program’s internal structure, so, if the chain is also irreducible and
the tilt is therefore its unique stationary law, inheritance alone
cannot move a stationary level away from the tilt; it can lengthen the
time to reach it. The actual minibatch gates meet that condition only
approximately (Section 3.2), so a deviation can also come from
finite-step, non-logistic or pair-dependent acceptance; and our
predictors read the kernel through scalar quality only, from unselected
programs. Three post-hoc analyses (Appendix D) bear on which of these
applies. (a) With the rewrite instruction, children depend on the
parent’s lineage depth, and the calibration walks are deeper than the
search incumbents, so $`\pi_{0}`$ estimated from the walks is worse than
the proposal law the search samples; tilting early walk samples instead
removes the underestimate, but that correction was found on the same
data. (b) The evidence that search-selected parents are harder to
improve than walk parents of the same quality is weaker than the pooled
rates suggested: with first proposals only, the odds ratio of
improvement (search vs. walk, adjusted for parent quality, parents in
$`[0.952,0.957)`$) is $`1.05`$ (Haiku neutral), $`0.51`$ (Haiku small
edit, 95% interval $`[0.28,0.92]`$), $`0.74`$ (Sonnet neutral,
$`[0.54,1.01]`$) and $`0.80`$ (Sonnet small edit, $`[0.54,1.19]`$), with
intervals clustered by parent. First proposals of different parents
along one run are not independent, and with errors clustered by run
instead the Haiku small-edit interval is $`[0.16,1.59]`$ (6 search and
13 walk runs) and no setting is significant at 5%; with this few
clusters the normal approximation is itself rough. We read (b) as
exploratory. (c) A reversible null model with the walks’ autocorrelation
reproduces the size of the shortfall for small edits after 150 steps but
not its time course, not the shortfall for neutral edits, and not the
runs from above, which it keeps at the prediction. Tests 3 and 4 were
registered to look at the dynamics directly.

#### Test 3: slow mixing (300 steps).

Under the null model of (c) more steps raise the attained fraction and
keep runs from above near the prediction. After (c), and before any step
beyond 150 was generated, we committed a third test with its decision
rules and evaluation script: the runs of four settings (Haiku and
Sonnet, neutral and small-edit instructions) and the small-edit runs
from above are continued to 300 steps with the same seeds, and steps
250–300 ($`W_{300}`$) are compared with steps 100–150 ($`W_{150}`$).
_H3_ (small edit): with $`\Delta=L(W_{300})-L(W_{150})`$ per seed, slow
mixing is rejected if $`\Delta+2\,\mathrm{se}<\Delta_{\rm null}/2`$ and
persistence if $`\Delta-2\,\mathrm{se}>\Delta_{\rm null}/2`$, where
$`\Delta_{\rm null}=0.21`$ (Haiku) and $`0.19`$ (Sonnet) is the null
model’s rise. _H4_ (neutral; null model $`L(W_{300})=1.00`$): slow
mixing is rejected if $`L(W_{300})+2\,\mathrm{se}<0.95`$. _H5_ (from
above, $`m=4,16`$): slow mixing is rejected if the level over
$`W_{300}`$ plus $`2\,\mathrm{se}`$ is below the prediction minus
$`0.0005`$; the null model’s runs from above are within $`0.0002`$ of
the predictions there. A power calculation, committed after launch and
before any result, found that H3 was unlikely to decide with six seeds
and that H5 carried the test.

In every continued run the first 151 steps reproduce the stored run
(Figure 3). _H5 rejects the null model in all four cells._ Over
$`W_{300}`$ the runs from above are at $`0.9505\pm 0.0005`$ and
$`0.9505\pm 0.0008`$ (Haiku, $`m=4,16`$; predictions $`0.9531`$,
$`0.9548`$) and $`0.9508\pm 0.0004`$ and $`0.9519\pm 0.0007`$ (Sonnet;
$`0.9535`$, $`0.9543`$), each lower than over $`W_{150}`$ ($`0.9508`$,
$`0.9509`$, $`0.9519`$, $`0.9523`$). This verdict was largely settled
before the continuation: the $`W_{150}`$ levels were already below the
H5 threshold in three cells and at it in the fourth. What the
continuation adds is that these runs do not return within another 150
steps, and that runs from above and from below meet or cross below the
prediction: for Haiku at $`m=4`$ they meet ($`0.9505`$ and $`0.9509`$);
for Sonnet at $`m=16`$ they are within $`0.0004`$ of each other,
$`0.0024`$ below the prediction; for Sonnet at $`m=4`$ the run from
above is below the mean of the runs from below in five of six seeds. Not
every cell behaves this way: for Sonnet at $`m=1`$ the two runs meet at
the prediction ($`0.9519`$ and $`0.9513`$ against $`0.9521`$), and one
of the six Haiku $`m=16`$ runs from above ends at $`0.9542`$. _H4_
rejects the null model for Sonnet only at the boundary
($`L(W_{300})=0.82\pm 0.06`$) and not for Haiku, whose attained fraction
rose from $`0.88`$ to $`0.93`$. _H3_ is inconclusive in both small-edit
settings, as the power calculation expected ($`\Delta=-0.06\pm 0.12`$
and $`+0.11\pm 0.10`$); the attained fractions over $`W_{300}`$ are
$`0.67\pm 0.12`$ and $`0.73\pm 0.07`$, against $`0.93`$ and $`0.98`$ for
the null model. In three of the four settings the runs from below moved
toward the prediction between the two windows. H5 rejects one reversible
model, unimodal in a latent inherited component, started from one
program. It does not reject reversibility on program space. Under the
Barker condition a proposal kernel reversible with respect to
$`\pi_{0}`$ has the tilt as an exact invariant law. If the chain is
moreover irreducible, a level below the tilt reached within 300 steps is
either not yet stationary (for instance, metastable) or reflects a
$`\hat{\pi}_{0}`$ that misestimates the kernel’s equilibrium near the
top; a reducible chain can also be stationary within a class of programs
it cannot leave; for the actual minibatch gates it may also reflect
finite-step, non-logistic or pair-dependent acceptance (Section 3.2).

#### Test 4: the start program.

Every run from above starts from one program that a search had selected,
so tests 1 and 3 cannot tell whether it loses quality because it was
selected or because of its quality. After test 3, and before any step of
the new runs was generated, we committed a fourth test. For Sonnet with
the small-edit instruction we took twelve start programs of matched true
quality (mean $`0.9544`$, range $`0.9541`$–$`0.9547`$; prediction at
$`m=4`$: $`0.9535`$): six visited by unselected calibration walks (one
per walk) and six that had been incumbents of the root searches (one per
seed). True quality is the score on all instances, so choosing a program
inside the band involves no winner’s curse in either group. The selected
starts had been incumbents of $`m=64`$ chains (five) and an $`m=16`$
chain (one), i.e. selected by gates with $`2`$–$`5`$ times the inverse
temperature of the test gate; the registered contrast is selection as
such, not selection at $`m=4`$ (origins in Appendix C). Each start was
run in regime R at $`m=4`$ for 150 steps with seeds 60 and 61. Within a
seed all twelve starts draw the same sequence of minibatch indices until
their chains desynchronise, which helps the paired contrast but means
the 24 runs are not independent; the unit of analysis is the start. For
each start, the drop over a window is the mean true quality of the
incumbent minus the start’s quality, averaged over the two seeds; $`D`$
is the mean drop of the walk-visited starts minus that of the
search-selected starts, with the standard error across starts. _H6_
(primary, steps 1–50): selection predicts $`D>0`$; pass if
$`D-2\,\mathrm{se}>0`$, fail if $`D+2\,\mathrm{se}<0`$, inconclusive
otherwise. The same rule over steps 100–150 was registered as secondary,
and a regression of the drop on start type and lineage depth without a
rule. The walk-visited starts are deeper in their lineage (mean $`43`$
generations against $`16`$), which by (a) would work against the
hypothesis if the depth effect of the rewrite setting carried over. The
same design with Haiku and the small-edit instruction, where only two
walks reach the band, was registered as descriptive.

_H6 is inconclusive on its primary window._ Over steps 1–50 the
walk-visited starts lose $`0.0005`$ and the search-selected starts
$`0.0008`$ ($`D=0.0003\pm 0.0004`$). _The secondary window passes._ Over
steps 100–150 they lose $`0.0003`$ and $`0.0019`$
($`D=0.0016\pm 0.0004`$); in 35 of the 36 cross-group pairs of starts
the search-selected start loses more, and the regression gives
$`0.0019\pm 0.0006`$ for start type and $`-0.000008\pm 0.000014`$ per
generation of depth (Figure 4). On the 10-step moving average of the
drops the gap between the groups exceeds two standard errors
intermittently from step 76 and at every step from step 102. Over steps
100–150 the walk-visited starts are at $`0.9541`$ ($`\pm 0.0001`$ across
starts), $`0.0006`$ above the prediction (about $`1.8`$ standard errors
when the prediction’s bootstrap error is included), and the
search-selected starts at $`0.9525`$, near the level of the runs from
the root over steps 250–300 ($`0.9523`$). In the descriptive Haiku arm
both groups lose quality: the walk-visited starts fall from $`0.9549`$
to $`0.9528`$ and the search-selected starts from $`0.9553`$ to
$`0.9519`$ over steps 100–150 (prediction $`0.9531`$; drops $`-0.0023`$,
$`-0.0020`$ and $`-0.0036`$, $`-0.0031`$ for the four starts), so only
the sign of the difference matches the Sonnet result; the Haiku starts
sit further above their prediction ($`0.0017`$–$`0.0022`$) than the
Sonnet starts ($`0.0009`$). In the registered setting, then, among the
tested starts the subsequent loss was associated with origin even after
matching their initial measured quality: programs of the same quality
that a walk visited kept it for 150 steps. The single-step analysis (b)
shows a weaker and less consistent selection effect, so the quantity
that separates the groups over a run need not be the probability that
the next child is better. The test does not settle the stationary level.
Chains of one kernel at one $`m`$ stand at different levels after 150
steps depending on the start, so the chains have not been shown to mix
to a common invariant law within the budget (either at least one group
is not yet stationary, or the chain is reducible on the region it
visits), and every incumbent of a run from the root is itself a selected
program, so the common level below the tilt in test 3 was reached only
by chains of selected programs.

_Continuation to 300 steps._ Before any step beyond 150 was generated,
we registered two further hypotheses for the same chains, with the
readings of each outcome fixed in advance. _H6-300_: over steps 250–300,
the groups are “apart” if $`D-2\,\mathrm{se}>0`$, “reversed” if
$`D+2\,\mathrm{se}<0`$, “converged” if $`|D|+2\,\mathrm{se}<0.0005`$,
and inconclusive otherwise. _H10_: each group’s level over steps 250–300
is “above”, “at” or “below” the prediction ($`0.9535`$) by a
two-standard-error rule, with the prediction’s bootstrap error
($`0.0003`$) added in quadrature. Every continued run reproduces its
first 151 steps. (The numbers below are from a rerun: in the first run
of the continuation the evaluation harness failed on the machine it ran
on, so no new child was scored and the chains stayed at their
incumbents; we repaired the environment, re-scored the stored children
and reran with the same command, seeds and rules; Appendix C.) Over
steps 250–300 the walk-visited starts are at $`0.9535`$ (drops of
$`0.0003`$–$`0.0026`$ from their start) and the search-selected starts
at $`0.9525`$ (drops of $`0.0009`$–$`0.0030`$): $`D=0.0011\pm 0.0005`$,
so the groups are _apart_, narrowly ($`D-2\,\mathrm{se}=0.0001`$). The
walk-visited group is _at_ the prediction in the registered sense
($`0.9535\pm 0.0004`$: within two standard errors of it, which is
statistical compatibility, not equivalence; the $`\pm 2`$ se interval of
its difference from the prediction is $`[-0.0008,+0.0009]`$) and the
search-selected group _below_ it ($`0.9525\pm 0.0004`$, an upper bound
$`0.0001`$ under the prediction), near the runs from the root over the
same window ($`0.9523`$). The registered reading of this combination is
that the start’s history still determines the level after 300 steps. We
state it more narrowly: among these twelve starts, whose origin was not
randomised, origin and level after 300 steps remain associated, and the
two groups have not reached a common level. The association is fragile.
With starts as units, a post-hoc Welch interval for $`D`$ is
$`[-0.00001,+0.0022]`$ ($`p=0.053`$ two-sided, $`0.026`$ one-sided); per
seed, $`D=0.0016\pm 0.0006`$ (seed 60) and $`0.0005\pm 0.0009`$ (seed
61); leaving out one start at a time keeps $`D`$ positive in all twelve
cases but $`D-2\,\mathrm{se}>0`$ in only four (ms300_sensitivity.py).
Two seeds shared across starts are not 24 independent replications. The
walk-visited group’s compatibility with the tilt does not show that the
tilt is the stationary level, since chains of the same kernel from
selected starts stand $`0.0010`$ lower. In the descriptive Haiku arm the
walk-visited starts end at $`0.9528\pm 0.0020`$ and the search-selected
starts at $`0.9535\pm 0.0010`$, both within two standard errors of the
prediction ($`0.9531`$), with $`D=-0.0003\pm 0.0020`$.

### 5.3 Second domain: minibatch-gated prompt optimisation (test 5)

Tests 2–4 use bin packing only. To check whether the inheritance
relation of test 2 transfers, we registered a test in a second domain
before its calibration began, with the decision rules, the evaluation
script and the transferred line $`\hat{L}(\rho)=1.18-0.52\,\rho`$ (the
six-setting bin-packing fit, residual s.d. $`0.065`$) fixed in advance.

#### Design.

The candidates are system prompts for a synthetic counting task: each
instance asks how many times two given letters occur in total in a list
of 8–12 words, and the answer is scored by exact match. A fixed set of
$`N=100`$ instances defines the score vector, the 0/1 correctness of
each instance, so true quality is the accuracy on all 100. Each prompt
is evaluated once: the task model’s answers are sampled once and the
score vector is stored and reused, so true quality here is the _frozen_
empirical accuracy of that evaluation, not the expected accuracy of the
prompt under re-sampling of the task model. The gates re-draw
minibatches from the frozen vector (this is regime R, not C), but a
search can select lucky evaluations as well as better prompts, and the
design cannot separate the two. We did not re-evaluate prompts; the
levels below are frozen-score levels, not reproducible model accuracies.
The task model is Claude Haiku 5.5, answering 20 instances per call with
the candidate as its system prompt. The proposer is also Haiku 5.5,
starting from the prompt _“Answer the questions.”_ (frozen quality
$`0.50`$ and $`0.48`$ in its two independent evaluations, one per
setting). Two settings differ only in the instruction: _p_small_, the
small-edit instruction of the bin-packing settings, and _p_bold_,
_“Write a substantially different prompt built on a new idea.”_ The gate
is regime R as before, with minibatches drawn without replacement from
the 100 instances, and ties reject. Calibration used 6 no-selection
walks of 60 steps per setting, with $`\pi_{0}`$ estimated from the
pooled second halves (186 prompts). The prediction, $`\tau`$ and
$`\rho`$ were computed by the bin-packing scripts and committed before
search. Search used $`m\in\{4,16,64\}`$, 6 seeds and 100 steps; the
observed level is the mean over steps 60–100. _H7_ (ordering, $`m=64`$
above $`m=4`$) is a sanity check and is not counted. _H8_ (direction):
if $`\rho`$(p*small) exceeds $`\rho`$(p_bold) by at least $`0.2`$, then
$`L`$(p_bold) $`>`$ $`L`$(p_small): pass if the difference minus two
standard errors is positive, fail if the difference plus two standard
errors is negative, inconclusive otherwise. *H9* (transfer), per
setting: pass if
$`|L-\hat{L}(\rho)|\leq 2\sqrt{0.065^{2}+\mathrm{se}^{2}}`$. As in
test 2, $`L`$ averages the cells with ESS at least $`30`$. An addendum,
committed while the searches ran and before any search result existed,
fixed four points: the standard error of $`L`$ is taken over seeds; only
$`m=4`$ qualifies in either setting (ESS $`129`$, $`29`$, $`3`$ for
p_small and $`81`$, $`18`$, $`3`$ for p_bold), so H8 and H9 rest on one
cell per setting; a secondary prediction that rescales $`\beta`$ by the
measured acceptance slope ($`1.46`$ and $`1.45`$ at $`m=4`$) is reported
without a rule; and a task-model call that times out twice is scored as
unparseable. The registered void conditions (spread of $`\pi*{0}`$ below
$`0.01`$, or more than 10% of evaluated prompts with an unparseable
chunk) are not met: $`\pi\_{0}`$ has s.d. $`0.098`$ and $`0.134`$, and
$`6.1\%`$ and $`2.8\%`$ of prompts have an unparseable chunk.

#### Results.

Calibration gave $`\rho=0.73`$ (p*small) and $`0.29`$ (p_bold), a gap of
$`0.43`$, so H8 is not void, and $`\hat{L}=0.80`$ and $`1.02`$. At
$`m=4`$ the predictions are $`0.474`$ and $`0.674`$; search reached
$`0.756\pm 0.075`$ ($`z=+3.5`$) and $`0.781\pm 0.058`$ ($`z=+1.7`$), so
$`L=5.98\pm 1.32`$ and $`1.78\pm 0.42`$. These registered standard
errors cover the search seeds only. A post-hoc bootstrap that also
resamples the six calibration walks (through $`\pi*{0}`$, $`\tau`$,
$`\beta`$ and $`\rho`$) gives 95% intervals of $`[3.0,20.6]`$ and
$`[0.96,2.98]`$ for $`L`$, and $`[2.2,19.7]`$ and $`[-0.05,1.90]`$ for
$`L-\hat{L}`$ (prompt_L_bootstrap.py); it is conditional on the frozen
scores and the fixed line, and six walks give no exact coverage. *H8
fails*, in the opposite direction: $`L`$(p_bold) $`-`$ $`L`$(p_small)
$`=-4.20\pm 1.38`$ (bootstrap interval $`[-18.6,-0.9]`$). The setting
with the higher parent–child correlation exceeds its prediction by more,
whereas on bin packing the higher-correlation settings fell short. *H9
fails for p_small* ($`|L-\hat{L}|=5.18`$, tolerance $`2.64`$) *and
passes for p_bold* ($`0.75`$, tolerance $`0.85`$); with the calibration
uncertainty above, the pass means that the transferred line is not
rejected there, not that it predicts accurately. H7 holds in both
settings ($`0.963`$ against $`0.756`$; $`0.903`$ against $`0.781`$),
although p_bold is not monotone in $`m`$ ($`0.916`$ at $`m=16`$). The
secondary, slope-corrected prediction is still exceeded ($`L=3.85`$ and
$`1.19`$). In five of the six cells, including the four with ESS below
$`30`$, search lies above the prediction. In this domain, therefore, the
tilt computed from the short calibration walks was a _lower_ estimate of
what search reached in 100 steps on the frozen scores, and the
finite-step calibration and prediction procedure that worked
directionally on bin packing did not transfer; we do not read this as
isolating an inheritance mechanism that reverses across domains.

#### A post-hoc observation.

The p*small calibration walks are not stationary. Their mean quality
falls from $`0.61`$ over the first ten steps to $`0.41`$ by step 40 and
is $`0.45`$ over the last ten, so unselected small edits degrade the
prompt, and the second-half sample, which estimates $`\pi*{0}`$, has
maximum $`0.73`$. A reweighting of that sample cannot exceed its
maximum, and the search level at $`m=4`$ ($`0.756`$) is above it: the
prediction is capped by the region the walks occupied when the sample
was taken, not by the gate. Earlier steps of the same walks reached
$`0.96`$. On bin packing the walks were longer (100 steps) and the
search started near the walk mean. That short calibration walks cause
the underestimate here is a reading after the result. We did not test
it, and it is consistent with the general caution of Section 5.2 that a
calibration walk may not sample the kernel’s equilibrium near the top.

### 5.4 Exploratory run: cached versus re-evaluated search

Before the pre-registered tests we ran a single exploratory setting (BP,
Haiku, neutral instruction) with both regimes. Its prediction is not
pre-registered: $`\tau`$ was estimated mostly from search pairs, without
the finite-population factor, and the walk sample was small (6 walks,
306 programs). We report it for the comparison of regimes, which the
pre-registered runs do not cover.

#### Design.

Regime R as above. Regime C compares the child’s minibatch score with
the score cached when the incumbent was accepted. Both regimes run
$`m\in\{1,4,16,64\}`$ with 6 seeds, for 100 steps ($`m\leq 4`$) or 200
steps ($`m\geq 16`$); within a seed, the $`k`$-th proposal from a given
parent is shared across regimes and minibatch sizes (common random
numbers). In total 8,927 programs were generated; 7 failed to run.

#### Inputs and predictors.

The mutation equilibrium $`\pi_{0}`$ comes from 6 no-selection walks of
100 steps, second halves pooled, 306 points with mean $`0.9423`$, s.d.
$`0.0077`$ and maximum $`0.9550`$. The per-instance difference scale
$`\tau=0.0061`$ is the median, over 8,607 parent–child pairs, of the
standard deviation of per-instance score differences; most of those
pairs come from the search runs, and using only the 547 walk pairs gives
$`\tau=0.0054`$ and moves every tilt prediction by at most $`0.0004`$.
The _tilt prediction_ plugs $`\beta(m)=1.596\sqrt{m}/\tau`$ into
Corollary 1. The _projected-kernel predictor_ drops reversibility: it
estimates the conditional law of child quality given parent quality from
all generated pairs, including the search’s own, and evaluates the
one-dimensional chain with acceptance $`\Phi(\Delta\sqrt{m}/\tau)`$; it
is a consistency check rather than an independent prediction.

#### Cached search inflates its own score.

In regime C we replay each chain and compare the recorded score of the
final incumbent with its true quality. The recorded score is too high by
$`0.0151\pm 0.0015`$ ($`m=1`$), $`0.0099\pm 0.0012`$ ($`m=4`$),
$`0.0033\pm 0.0007`$ ($`m=16`$) and $`0.0015\pm 0.0001`$ ($`m=64`$); at
$`m=1`$ the overstatement is three times the entire difference in true
quality between $`m=1`$ and $`m=64`$. At matched _evaluations_ (C after
$`2t`$ steps against R after $`t`$ steps), C is not ahead within our
budget: $`C-R=-0.0008\pm 0.0017`$, $`-0.0003\pm 0.0014`$,
$`-0.0012\pm 0.0007`$, $`-0.0003\pm 0.0004`$ for $`m=1,4,16,64`$. In the
simulations of Section 5.1 C is ahead of R at every budget, and neither
bounded quality nor larger steps changes that (sim_cache_bounded.py).
Pairing is a plausible reason for the difference. R scores parent and
child on the same minibatch, so the difficulty of the sampled instances
cancels; C compares against a score from a different minibatch. In our
data the unpaired comparison has s.d. $`\sqrt{2}\times 0.0082=0.0116`$
against $`\tau=0.0062`$ for the paired one. Giving C $`1.87`$ times R’s
per-evaluation noise in the simulation makes C level with or behind R up
to about 500 evaluations in most settings, and ahead only at larger
budgets (sim_cache_paired.py); our LLM runs use at most 400. We have not
run the direct contrast (R with unpaired minibatches). Once the recorded
score is inflated, almost no proposal clears it: in the second half of
the run, C chains accept $`0.0`$, $`0.3`$, $`1.5`$ and $`1.3`$ proposals
per seed for $`m=1,4,16,64`$, against $`7.8`$, $`10.3`$, $`13.0`$ and
$`5.5`$ for R.

#### Caveats.

The LLM mutation is not known to be reversible and its steps are large,
so Theorem 1 does not formally apply; the experiments measure how well
the small-step temperature transfers rather than assume it. A pilot
reused a fixed pool of 8 children per parent. It stalled at parents with
no better child and gave predictions $`0.002`$–$`0.004`$ too high. Fresh
proposals removed most of that gap, which suggests the prediction
applies when the mutation kernel is sampled, not when it is enumerated
from a small cache. All programs, scores and scripts are archived with
the paper (Appendix C).

## 6 Related work

#### Noisy acceptance and tilted stationary laws.

Logistic choice from Gumbel utility shocks is due to McFadden (1974) and
Yellott (1977); Barker’s rule to Barker (1965); its equivalence to
“accept iff $`\Delta`$ plus logistic noise is positive” is used by Seita
et al. (2017). Logit dynamics yield Gibbs stationary laws in potential
games (Blume, 1993; Alós-Ferrer and Netzer, 2010). In population
genetics, the stationary law under weak mutation is the mutation
equilibrium tilted by fitness with population size as inverse
temperature (Iwasa, 1988; Sella and Hirsh, 2005); Wu et al. (2010) show
that, under weak selection, fixation probabilities and fixation times of
pairwise-comparison processes depend on the comparison function only
through its slope at zero. Theorem 1 is a stationary-law analogue in a
diffusion limit; we are not aware of a prior statement of
$`\beta=4F^{\prime}(0)`$ for the stationary law of keep-if-better
search, but the computation is elementary. Closest in spirit, Traulsen
et al. (2007a) show for evolutionary games that stochastic payoff
evaluation lowers the intensity of selection, i.e. raises the
temperature of a Fermi pairwise-comparison process (Traulsen et al.,
2007b), via a mean-field effective temperature, and study fixation. Our
title restates their observation for LLM-guided search; what we add is
the stationary law of keep-if-better search with a mutation operator,
the constant $`4F^{\prime}(0)`$ for general symmetric noise, the
paired-minibatch form, and a stationary level that, under the stated
conditions, can be computed from unselected mutation before search
(whether finite runs reach it is tested in Section 5.2). In evolutionary
computation, Boltzmann tournament selection builds a Boltzmann
distribution from pairwise competitions (Goldberg, 1990), and Rattray
and Shapiro (1997) analyse Boltzmann selection under additive Gaussian
fitness noise, where in the weak-selection limit noise can be offset by
a larger population. Noisy MCMC treats evaluation noise as bias to be
corrected (Ceperley and Dewing, 1999; Alquier et al., 2016); we treat it
as the temperature the search actually runs at.

#### Noisy evolutionary optimisation.

The role of re-evaluating the parent is analysed for evolution
strategies on the noisy sphere by Arnold and Beyer (2002), with
closed-form residual distances in the steady state (Beyer, 2001);
runtime theory for discrete noisy optimisation is surveyed by Jin and
Branke (2005). These results concern specific landscapes and step-size
adaptation rather than the stationary law of a generic mutation
operator.

#### LLM-guided search and verifiers.

SMCEvolve (Jiang et al., 2026) targets the same tilted law
$`p_{0}e^{\beta R}`$, with the terminal $`\beta`$ set as a parameter, an
adaptive (ESS-based) schedule of intermediate temperatures, and an exact
evaluator. Here the temperature is not chosen but induced by the
verifier noise. Hu and Tang (2026) study the winner’s curse of
keep-if-better prompt optimisation empirically. In one exploratory
setting with three seeds, re-drawing the selection set lowered the final
held-out gain; they note that this comparison is confounded with
feedback and with the effective number of candidates, so we cite it as
related evidence rather than as a test of our results. For independent
sampling with imperfect verifiers, Dorner et al. (2026) and Stroebl
et al. (2024) derive ceilings from false-positive rates and ROC slopes,
and Kwa et al. (2024) characterise heavy-tailed reward error. Empirical
comparisons of evolutionary and independent search at matched budgets
(Oved et al., 2026a; Oved et al., 2026b) motivate the question but do
not model verifier noise; most of their evaluators are deterministic.

## 7 Discussion and limitations

#### What to do with a noisy verifier.

The results suggest three measurements for a re-evaluated loop, all of
which need no search: estimate $`\beta`$ from the per-task score
differences of a sample of parent–child pairs, checking the acceptance
rule directly when $`m`$ is small or scores are discrete; estimate the
quality distribution of unselected mutation; and compute the tilt
$`\kappa^{\prime}(\beta)`$ (strictly, the stationary mean of the tilted
law, which Theorem 1 establishes only for the small-step limit). A
larger minibatch raises $`\beta\propto\sqrt{m}`$ and, in six of our
seven settings, raised the level the search reached. Whether more steps
will help is a different question, which the tilt does not answer for
LLM editing: in our runs, chains from different starts had not met after
150–300 steps, so a single run that has flattened is not evidence that
the loop is at its stationary level. In our bin-packing experiments
search attained $`62`$–$`88\%`$ of the predicted gain over unselected
mutation after 150 steps with proposers that edit the parent
($`67`$–$`93\%`$ after 300 steps in the four settings continued) and
$`109`$–$`119\%`$ with proposers that write new heuristics. On bin
packing the parent–child quality correlation of the calibration walk,
which costs nothing extra, indicated which case applied: for an editing
proposer the prediction was an upper estimate of what search from the
root reached within 300 steps, and the fitted line of Section 5.2 gives
a rough correction, whose quantitative accuracy we could not establish.
Neither held in the prompt domain (Section 5.3): search exceeded the
tilt in both settings, more so with the small-edit instruction, and the
calibration walks there were still drifting. The correction should not
be used outside the task it was fitted on, and a calibration walk should
be checked for drift before its tilt is trusted as an estimate in either
direction.

#### Limitations.

The theory assumes a mutation kernel reversible with respect to its
equilibrium; whether LLM mutation is reversible is unknown, and our
experiments measure rather than assume how well the prediction
transfers. For non-logistic noise the result is a small-step diffusion
approximation, and we do not prove convergence of the stationary laws;
we document a large-step regime where mixing becomes very slow, and
leave existence in general open. We give no growth law for cached search
and no mixing-time bound, so the _early_ crossover between independent
and re-evaluated search is measured, not predicted. The program-search
study covers two tasks, two proposer models of one family and three
mutation instructions, each with six seeds and 150 steps (300 for four
settings), and the prompt test two settings with one model, 100 steps
and calibration walks of only 60 steps; on the travelling-salesman task
the seed-to-seed spread is too large for a sharp test, and at the
largest minibatch sizes the walk samples are too small to estimate the
tilt (effective sample sizes in single digits). All reported levels are
levels of finite runs; none of our tests establishes a stationary level.
The calibration walks themselves may not be stationary: in one setting
their mean is still drifting over the second half, by an amount that
moves the prediction as much as the shortfall (Appendix D). The
inheritance account is an empirical regularity on one task with one
model family: the linear relation was found on four settings, its
direction confirmed on two, its quantitative form is not robust to how
the uncertainty of the registered test is computed, and in a second
domain its registered direction failed and its quantitative form was not
rejected in one of two settings, with wide calibration uncertainty. Our
post-hoc reading of that failure (short calibration walks that cap the
prediction) was not tested. Two registered analyses had flaws that we
found after the results: the standard error of the attained fraction
ignored proposals shared across minibatch sizes, and the
selection-effect statistic pooled proposals in a way that over-weights
hard-to-improve search parents. We report the registered verdicts and
the corrected ones side by side. The explanations of the residual errors
are post-hoc. For proposers whose children are nearly independent of the
parent’s quality, the tilt is too low because quality depends on lineage
depth and the calibration walks are deeper than the search; a
calibration-only correction removes the error but was found on the same
data, and the overshoot should shrink with run length, which we did not
test. For editing proposers, a reversible null model with the walks’
correlation structure does not reproduce the runs from above, and a
registered 300-step test confirms that these runs do not recover. A
fourth registered test associates this with the start’s origin, in one
setting at $`m=4`$ and among twelve starts whose origin was not
randomised: from programs of the same quality that unselected walks
visited, the runs kept their level for 150 steps (secondary window; the
primary window was inconclusive) and were statistically compatible with
the prediction after 300 steps, while runs from programs that searches
at $`m=16`$ and $`64`$ had selected lost it and stayed below the
prediction, apart from the first group by a margin that passed the
registered rule narrowly and is sensitive to leaving out single starts.
In the descriptive second setting both groups lost quality. Our data
therefore do not determine whether the stationary level of the
small-edit kernel lies below the tilt, at it, or whether a common level
exists within practical run lengths; a kernel that is reversible with
respect to $`\pi_{0}`$ but metastable is not excluded. In one cell
(Sonnet, rewrite, $`m=4`$) the incumbent is still falling at 150 steps.
The Gaussian approximation to the acceptance rule is too flat at the
smallest minibatch, where scores are discrete and ties are frequent, and
the per-pair noise scale is heterogeneous, so $`\beta`$ is a summary
rather than a constant.

## Appendix A Proofs

#### Theorem 1.

Let $`g`$ be smooth with compact support and write
$`\Delta_{h}=f(y)-f(x)`$, $`y\sim K_{h}(x,\cdot)`$. The generator of
regime R is $`L_{h}g(x)=\mathbb{E}\big[(g(y)-g(x))F(\Delta_{h})\big]`$.
Since $`F(\Delta)+F(-\Delta)=1`$ and $`F`$ is differentiable at $`0`$,
$`F(\Delta)=\tfrac{1}{2}+F^{\prime}(0)\Delta+r(\Delta)`$ with
$`r(\Delta)=o(\Delta)`$. Then

```math
L_{h}g=\tfrac{1}{2}(K_{h}-I)g+F^{\prime}(0)\,\mathbb{E}[(g(y)-g(x))(f(y)-f(x))]+\mathbb{E}[(g(y)-g(x))\,r(\Delta_{h})].
```

By assumption
$`h^{-2}\tfrac{1}{2}(K_{h}-I)g\to\tfrac{1}{4}(\Delta g+\nabla\log\pi_{0}\cdot\nabla g)`$.
For $`x`$ in a compact set, where the moment bounds hold uniformly,
Taylor expansion with the assumed step covariance $`h^{2}I+o(h^{2})`$
and third moment $`O(h^{3})`$ gives
$`\mathbb{E}[(g(y)-g(x))(f(y)-f(x))]=h^{2}\nabla g\cdot\nabla f+o(h^{2})`$.
For the remainder, write $`\xi=y-x`$ and use $`|\Delta_{h}|\leq L|\xi|`$
and $`|g(y)-g(x)|\leq L|\xi|`$. Given $`\eta>0`$ choose $`\epsilon`$
with $`|r(\Delta)|\leq\eta|\Delta|`$ for $`|\Delta|<\epsilon`$. On that
event the remainder is at most
$`\eta L^{2}\mathbb{E}|\xi|^{2}=O(\eta h^{2})`$. On the complement,
$`|r(\Delta)|\leq 1+F^{\prime}(0)|\Delta|`$ and Markov’s inequality give
$`O(\mathbb{E}|\xi|^{3}/\epsilon^{2})=O(h^{3})`$. Hence the remainder is
$`o(h^{2})`$. Hence $`h^{-2}L_{h}g\to\mathcal{L}g`$ uniformly on compact
sets. The operator
$`\mathcal{L}=\tfrac{1}{4}(\Delta+\nabla U\cdot\nabla)`$ with
$`U=\log\pi_{0}+\beta f`$ is the generator of the Langevin diffusion
$`\mathrm{d}X=\tfrac{1}{4}\nabla U\,\mathrm{d}t+\tfrac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\mathrm{d}W`$,
which is reversible with respect to $`e^{U}`$ whenever $`e^{U}`$ is
integrable. Convergence of the rescaled chains to this diffusion on
compact time intervals follows from standard generator-convergence
results for Markov chains \[Ethier and Kurtz, 1986, Ch. 4, Sec. 8\]
under the usual tightness conditions; we do not claim convergence of the
stationary laws themselves. That would need, for example, tightness of
the chains’ invariant laws, uniqueness of the invariant law of the
limit, and uniform integrability of $`f`$; uniform-in-$`h`$ ergodicity
is one sufficient route. Section 3.4 shows that mixing can become very
slow for large steps, so such conditions are not automatic. Accordingly,
we use Theorem 1 as a diffusion approximation, and its use for LLM
search (Section 5.2) is an empirical extrapolation.

#### Proposition 1(a), Gaussian quality.

Let $`f_{1},\dots,f_{N}`$ be i.i.d. $`N(\mu,s^{2})`$, independent of
i.i.d. Gumbel$`(0,b)`$ noise, $`\beta=1/b`$,
$`I_{N}=\arg\max_{i}(f_{i}+\varepsilon_{i})`$ and
$`p_{i}=e^{\beta f_{i}}/\sum_{j}e^{\beta f_{j}}`$. By the Gumbel-max
property, $`\Pr(I_{N}=i\mid f_{1},\dots,f_{N})=p_{i}`$, so
$`\mathbb{E}f_{I_{N}}=\sum_{i}\mathbb{E}[f_{i}p_{i}]`$. Since
$`0\leq p_{i}\leq 1`$ and
$`\partial p_{i}/\partial f_{i}=\beta p_{i}(1-p_{i})`$ is bounded,
Gaussian integration by parts (Stein’s identity) gives
$`\mathbb{E}[(f_{i}-\mu)p_{i}]=s^{2}\mathbb{E}[\partial p_{i}/\partial f_{i}]=\beta s^{2}\mathbb{E}[p_{i}(1-p_{i})]`$,
and summing over $`i`$,
$`\mathbb{E}f_{I_{N}}=\mu+\beta s^{2}\big(1-\mathbb{E}\sum_{i}p_{i}^{2}\big)`$.
With $`W_{i}=e^{\beta f_{i}}`$, which has finite first and second
moments,
$`\sum_{i}p_{i}^{2}=N^{-1}\big(N^{-1}\sum_{i}W_{i}^{2}\big)/\big(N^{-1}\sum_{i}W_{i}\big)^{2}\to 0`$
almost surely by the strong law, and since
$`0\leq\sum_{i}p_{i}^{2}\leq 1`$, also in mean. Hence
$`\mathbb{E}f_{I_{N}}\to\mu+\beta s^{2}=\kappa^{\prime}_{\mathrm{root}}(\beta)`$.
This covers Gaussian quality with Gumbel noise only; it is distinct from
part (b).

#### Gaussian constants.

With i.i.d. $`N(0,\sigma^{2})`$ noise on each evaluation, the difference
has s.d. $`\sqrt{2}\sigma`$,
$`F^{\prime}(0)=1/(\sqrt{2\pi}\sqrt{2}\sigma)=1/(2\sqrt{\pi}\sigma)`$,
and $`\beta=2/(\sqrt{\pi}\sigma)`$. With a paired minibatch, the
difference has s.d. $`\tau/\sqrt{m}`$ directly, and
$`\beta=4\sqrt{m}/(\sqrt{2\pi}\tau)`$.

## Appendix C LLM experiment details

#### Proposer calls.

Each mutation is a single-turn call with no tools, default sampling
parameters, a system prompt describing the task, and a user prompt
consisting of the current program followed by an instruction and an
interface statement. A reply’s last fenced code block is taken as the
child; if it does not define priority, the call is repeated up to three
times, after which a program that raises is recorded (it is never
accepted). Transport errors are retried with back-off and never recorded
as children. Calls go through a command-line client with the default
system prompt replaced, tools disabled and one turn; from 2026-11-03
20:50 UTC the runs still in progress (test 4’s continuation and the
second-domain searches) were moved to another machine and billed to an
API key instead of a subscription, with the same client, models and
flags. Stored children replay identically on either machine. _Restarts._
Every child is written only after it is generated and evaluated, and
every restart used the same command, so it replayed the stored steps and
generated no child twice. Besides the Sonnet multi-start restart
described below, the runs were restarted when they moved machine (20:50
UTC) and 19 minutes later, when the spend ledger was corrected to price
calls from per-model usage. The two prompt-domain searches were also
restarted after one crash each, caused by an uncaught task-model timeout
(the timeout rule of the addendum was added then, before any search
result existed), when the spend cap was raised (2026-11-04 04:04 UTC),
and after a pause for a spend check (resumed 16:37 UTC). None of these
restarts changed a model, prompt, rule or seed. Logs are appended with a
marker line at each restart. Children are cached by parent and proposal
index, so a proposal shared across regimes or minibatch sizes is the
same program.

#### Prompts.

System prompt (BP): _“You write Python heuristics for online bin
packing. Bins have capacity 100; items arrive one at a time and each
goes to the feasible bin with the highest priority (a new bin is opened
only if none fits). Objective: use as few bins as possible. Item sizes
follow a Weibull-like distribution, typically 20-70.”_ System prompt
(TSP): _“You write Python heuristics for constructing short
travelling-salesman tours. A tour starts at city 0; at each step the
next city is the unvisited city with the highest priority. Cities are 60
random points in the unit square. Objective: the shortest closed tour.”_
User prompt: _“Here is the current priority function:”_, the program,
then one of the instructions neutral: _“Write an improved or alternative
version.”_; small edit: _“Make one small, targeted change that you
expect to improve it; keep everything else identical.”_; rewrite:
_“Write a substantially different heuristic built on a new idea, not a
tweak of this one.”_; followed by the interface statement. System prompt
(prompt domain): _“You write instructions (a system prompt) for a
language model that answers batches of letter-counting questions: how
many times a given letter appears in total in a short list of words.
Each final answer is scored by exact match. Objective: the highest
fraction of correct answers.”_ (the instances ask about two letters
combined); user prompt _“Here is the current prompt:”_, the prompt, the
p_small or p_bold instruction, and _“The questions and the required
answer format are appended automatically, so the prompt only needs to
guide how the model works. Keep it under 120 words. Reply with only the
new prompt in one code block.”_ Interface statements (BP: _“Signature
must stay def priority(item, bins): item is a float, bins is a numpy
array of remaining capacities of bins that can fit the item; return a
numpy array of the same length (higher = preferred). np and math are
available; no imports, no file or network access. Keep it under 40
lines. Reply with only one python code block.”_; TSP analogous, for
priority(cur, cand, dist, visited_frac)).

#### Evaluation.

Each program runs once, in an isolated interpreter with a 60 s limit, on
all 200 instances of its task; the per-instance scores are stored, and
every minibatch score is computed from them. A program that raises or
exceeds the limit has no score.

#### Programs generated.

BP/Haiku/neutral: 19,653 programs (both runs; 0.1% failed).
BP/Haiku/small edit: 10,480 (0.3%). BP/Haiku/rewrite: 6,322 (7.3%).
BP/Sonnet/neutral: 11,792 (0.04%). BP/Sonnet/small edit: 17,113 (0.01%).

## Appendix D Post-hoc diagnostics

None of the analyses in this appendix was registered. Unless stated
otherwise they read the program databases as they stood before the runs
beyond 150 steps (a snapshot archived with checksums) and exclude chains
started from above.

#### Noise scale and projected kernel.

$`\tau`$ is somewhat larger on walk pairs with higher-quality parents,
but recomputing $`\beta`$ from walk pairs with parent quality above
$`0.950`$ moves every prediction by at most $`0.0003`$ (tau_strata.py).
A projected-kernel predictor that fits the law of child quality given
parent quality on walk pairs only, and solves the one-dimensional
regime-R chain without assuming reversibility, predicts
$`0.9515`$–$`0.9576`$, above the tilt in every setting
(kernel_calib.py).

#### Acceptance rule.

For each walk parent–child pair we simulate the paired gate (2,000
minibatches per $`m`$, each drawn without replacement), compute the
acceptance log-odds $`\log\{P(\hat{D}>0)/P(\hat{D}<0)\}`$ and compare it
with the Gaussian value at
$`z=\Delta\sqrt{m}/(\tau_{xy}\sqrt{(N-m)/(N-1)})`$, for pairs with
$`0.05<|z|<1`$; the slope ratio is the least-squares slope through zero,
with a bootstrap error over pairs ($`\leq 0.04`$ throughout)
(beta*check_v2.py). For $`m=1,4,16,64`$ the ratios are $`1.73`$,
$`1.15`$, $`1.02`$, $`1.02`$ (Haiku neutral), $`2.04`$, $`1.32`$,
$`1.10`$, $`1.01`$ (Haiku small edit), $`1.57`$, $`1.03`$, $`0.99`$,
$`0.98`$ (Haiku rewrite), $`2.11`$, $`1.27`$, $`1.06`$, $`1.01`$ (Sonnet
neutral), $`1.99`$, $`1.22`$, $`1.04`$, $`1.01`$ (Sonnet small edit) and
$`1.57`$, $`1.12`$, $`1.00`$, $`0.99`$ (Sonnet rewrite). Single-instance
ties are $`50\%`$, $`72\%`$, $`34\%`$, $`69\%`$, $`66\%`$ and $`33\%`$
of comparisons. The Gaussian approximation is accurate for $`m\geq 16`$
and too flat for $`m\leq 4`$, most at $`m=1`$, where ties are frequent
and strict acceptance rejects them in both directions. A version of this
check reported in an earlier draft drew minibatch indices with
replacement and omitted the finite-population factor; the conclusions
are unchanged. The per-pair scale $`\tau*{xy}`$ is heterogeneous
(exploratory run: 10th to 90th percentile $`0.0031`$ to $`0.0078`$); in
the exploratory run, using the mean of $`\beta\_{xy}`$ instead of the
median moves the predictions by $`+0.0005`$, $`+0.0007`$, $`+0.0007`$
and $`+0.0002`$ for $`m=1,4,16,64`$. The check covers walk pairs, not
the pairs a search visits.

(small edit), $`0.31`$–$`0.51`$ (Sonnet, neutral) and $`0.25`$–$`0.43`$
(Sonnet, small edit) times the pre-registered value for $`m\leq 16`$,
and $`1.17`$–$`1.53`$ (Haiku) and $`0.86`$–$`2.29`$ (Sonnet) times with
the rewrite instruction; because the tilt is flat in $`\beta`$ at these

$`m=4`$ the tilt puts $`51\%`$, $`64\%`$, $`66\%`$ and $`72\%`$ of its
mass above $`0.953`$ for the four editing settings, while $`24\%`$,

prediction by an amount comparable to the shortfall.

#### (a) Lineage depth.

With the rewrite instruction a child’s quality barely depends on its
parent’s quality, but it depends on the parent’s lineage depth, the
length of the shortest path from the starting program in the
de-duplicated proposal graph (a program reached along several paths gets
the shortest; this is not necessarily the number of generations a given
run passed through) (diag*base_law.py). In the Sonnet setting, children
of parents at depth 1–2 average $`0.9506`$ and children of parents at
depth 41–99 average $`0.9444`$. The calibration walks accept every
proposal, so their second halves sit at median depth 48; the search
rejects most proposals, and its incumbents sit at median depth 8 (Haiku:
39 and 8). Tilting the walk samples from steps 1–20 instead, which uses
calibration data only, gives prediction errors of $`-0.0003`$ to
$`+0.0001`$ for Haiku at every $`m`$ and $`-0.0002`$ to $`+0.0001`$ for
Sonnet except at $`m=4`$ ($`+0.0019`$, the cell whose late window dips),
against $`-0.0008`$ to $`-0.0003`$ and $`-0.0023`$ to $`+0.0005`$ with
the registered $`\pi*{0}`$. For Haiku the dependence on depth is not
monotone (children of the starting program average $`0.9405`$, of
depth-3–5 parents $`0.9480`$). For the editing instructions the early
walk samples are correlated with the starting program and are not a
substitute for $`\pi\_{0}`$. The same account predicts that the rewrite
overshoot is transient: accepted steps mostly move the incumbent deeper
(shortest-path depth can also fall, when a child is a shallower program
already in the graph), so its level should drift down toward the
deep-walk tilt. This is an untested hypothesis: no rewrite setting was
continued beyond 150 steps.

#### (b) Selection effect at matched quality.

The question is whether a program that a search selected is harder to
improve than a program of the same quality that a walk visited. Pooling
all proposals from each parent answers it with a biased estimator. A
search incumbent receives proposals until one is accepted, so the number
of proposals a parent receives is larger the harder it is to improve,
and pooled rates over-weight such parents; walk parents receive one
proposal per visit. Weighting each parent equally biases the other way,
because the last proposal of a search parent is the accepted one and
more often better. In a toy model in which both kinds of parent draw
their improvement probability from the same Beta$`(2,6)`$ law and the
gate accepts a better child with probability $`0.6`$ and a worse one
with $`0.1`$, the pooled rate is $`0.208`$ for search parents and
$`0.254`$ for walk parents, the parent-weighted rate $`0.350`$, and the
rate of first proposals $`0.252`$ (toy_sampling_bias.py). The first
proposal from each parent in each run is drawn before any stopping
decision and is the estimator we use. Table 6 reports the logistic
regression of “child better” on parent quality and parent type under the
three weightings, for parents in the registered band $`[0.952,0.957)`$
(h2_matched.py, h2_robust.py, h2_firstprop.py). The pooled odds ratios,

#### (c) A reversible null model.

In the ergodic Barker null model considered here the stationary law is
the tilt, so a persistent finite-budget shortfall is a transient effect
within that model. To see how large such a transient can be, we use
latent quality $`z=x+e`$, where $`x`$ is inherited (an AR(1) process
with variance share $`s`$ and persistence $`r`$) and $`e`$ is drawn
afresh for every child; observed quality is $`f=Q(\Phi(z))`$, with $`Q`$
the empirical quantile function of $`\pi_{0}`$, so that a walk has
marginal $`\pi_{0}`$. Fitting $`s`$ and $`r`$ to the autocorrelation of
the calibration walks (lags 1–10, rank-Gaussianised) gives
$`s=0.94,0.94,0.62,0.90`$ and $`r=0.983,0.971,0.847,0.955`$ for Haiku
small edit, Sonnet small edit, Haiku neutral and Sonnet neutral
(toy_acf.py). We simulate regime R with Barker acceptance at the
registered $`\beta`$, from the starting program (toy_inherit.py,
toy_above.py); Barker acceptance makes the model’s stationary law
exactly the tilt, and with probit acceptance the level would sit
slightly higher (Table 1). Over steps 100–150 the model attains $`0.72`$
and $`0.79`$ of the lift for the two small-edit settings (observed
$`0.73`$ and $`0.62`$) and $`1.00`$ and $`0.98`$ for the neutral
instruction (observed $`0.88`$ and $`0.80`$). It predicts a rise of
$`0.15`$–$`0.30`$ in the attained fraction from steps 50–100 to steps
100–150 in the small-edit settings, where the data are flat
($`0.74\to 0.73`$ and $`0.66\to 0.62`$). Its runs from above stay at the
prediction ($`0.9537`$ and $`0.9549`$ at $`m=4,16`$ for Haiku small
edit), while the observed runs from above fall to $`0.9508`$ and
$`0.9509`$. With $`s=0.94`$ a program of the starting quality has
inherited almost all of its excess, so the model has no state in which
such a program is good for reasons its children lose.
