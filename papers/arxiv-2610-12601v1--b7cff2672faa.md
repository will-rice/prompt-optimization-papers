# Reflective Prompt Optimization of a Deep-Research Agent: A Topic-Disjoint, Multi-Seed Protocol for Measuring Transferable Gains and Prompt-Level Overfitting

Elias Lumer    Sahil Sen    Vamse Kumar Subbiah    Kevin Paul

###### Abstract

Prompt optimization is an attractive alternative to reinforcement
learning for adapting language-model agents, since it is cheap and
produces human-readable artifacts. On factual tasks, however, it carries
a failure mode that scalar-reward reinforcement learning does not. An
optimizer that reads the training examples can write their answers into
the prompt, so an in-distribution gain may be memorization rather than
transferable behaviour. We apply reflective prompt evolution (GEPA) to
the two system prompts of a multi-agent deep-research agent and evaluate
it under a topic-disjoint split in which no topic is shared across
train, validation, and test. Held-out citation reward rises by 0.079,
from 0.702 to 0.781, with a bootstrap 95% confidence interval of
\[0.051, 0.107\], and the gain replicates across three seeds (mean
0.107). Nearly half of the winning prompt is a memorized answer key, yet
a controlled ablation shows this content earns no reward even on the
exact training questions it encodes. The large validation-minus-held-out
gap is therefore small-sample selection under a noisy judge, not
memorization. Varying the verifier isolates what makes the gain
transfer. Decomposed per-dimension feedback is the causal ingredient,
and collapsing it to a single scalar reward makes held-out transfer
negative. The gain persists under an out-of-family judge and is largely
reproduced by a hand-written version of the discovered recipe. Credible
evaluation of prompt optimizers therefore requires held-out topics,
multiple seeds, and verifier ablations. We release the candidate
prompts, lineage diffs, figures, tables, and analysis.

## 1 Introduction

Language-model agents are increasingly adapted to downstream tasks with
reinforcement learning methods such as Group Relative Policy
Optimization \[Shao et al., 2024\], which typically need thousands of
rollouts and collapse a rich execution trace into a single scalar
reward. Prompt optimization is an attractive alternative because it is
cheap, needs no training infrastructure, and produces human-readable
artifacts that practitioners can audit. Frameworks such as
DSPy \[Khattab et al., 2024\] have made this practical by treating a
system as a program of language-model calls and optimizing its prompts
automatically, with instruction-and-demonstration search in
MIPROv2 \[Opsahl-Ong et al., 2024\] and reflective prompt evolution in
GEPA \[Agrawal et al., 2025\].

On factual tasks, prompt optimization has a failure mode that
scalar-reward reinforcement learning does not. An optimizer that sees
the training examples can copy their specific answers into the prompt,
so the reported gain then reflects memorized lookups on seen topics
rather than a transferable change in how the agent behaves. This is a
form of reward hacking \[Skalse et al., 2022, Amodei et al., 2016\], in
which the optimizer raises the measured reward without improving the
behaviour that the reward is meant to track. It is the primary risk when
a reflective optimizer is allowed to read and rewrite prompts against a
training signal. The risk is sharpened when that signal is a
rubric-style reward scored by language-model judges, which are
themselves prone to hacking in reinforcement learning with verifiable
rewards \[Mahmoud et al., 2026, Tyagi et al., 2026, Wang et al.,
2026b\], as ours is (Section 3.2). A study that reports only an
in-distribution gain therefore cannot tell the reader how much of that
gain will survive on data the optimizer never saw, and closing that gap
is the measurement problem we address.

Our contribution is a generalization-first protocol that separates the
two. We use a topic-disjoint split in which no article family is shared
across train, validation, and test, and a held-out test set large enough
for tight confidence intervals. Under this design the held-out gain
reports only transferable behaviour, and the difference between the
validation gain and the held-out gain lets us test whether the apparent
gain is memorization. The optimizer we use as the instrument is GEPA,
the Genetic-Pareto reflective prompt evolution method of Agrawal et al.
\[2025\]. GEPA feeds a language-model reflector the full trajectory
(reasoning, tool calls, and tool outputs) together with diagnostic
textual feedback, so the reflector can diagnose why a candidate failed
and propose a targeted rewrite. It keeps a Pareto frontier of candidates
so the search does not collapse to a local optimum. Because GEPA reads
execution traces, it can both discover transferable rules and copy
specific facts into the prompt, which is exactly the mixture our
protocol is designed to separate. Our configuration follows recent
practice. Decagon \[2026\] report that a frontier reflection model is
needed, that the optimization sample size has a sweet spot around 20 to
100 examples, and that prompts bloat without length regularization, and
GEPA Team \[2026\] note that enlarging the reflection batch causes
context overload. We therefore use a strong reflector and keep the train
and validation sets at 24 examples each, and we observe the bloat effect
when GEPA’s Pareto selection rejects its two longest candidates.

The object we optimize is a source-attribution reward for deep-research
agents \[Shi et al., 2025\]. Onweller et al. \[2026\] show that
deep-research agents routinely emit citations that are present but not
verified, where the cited page exists yet fails to support the specific
claim. They propose parsing and evaluation machinery for source
attribution. Our gated composite reward (Section 3.2) operationalizes
those failure modes through the levers link_works, relevant_content, and
fact_check, so optimizing against it targets a documented
attribution-quality gap. The agent’s search behaviour is in turn shaped
by its harness \[Lumer et al., 2026\], and Sen et al. \[2026a\] show
that harness design materially reshapes agentic search quality, which is
why prompt edits that change how the researcher searches and cites can
move the reward at all. More broadly, structured-retrieval agent
systems \[Sen et al., 2026b\] situate this work in the line of
retrieval-augmented agents \[Lumer et al., 2025\], though our task is
single-shot cited reporting rather than long-horizon conversational
memory.

This paper makes the following contributions.

- •
  A generalization-first evaluation protocol, combining topic-disjoint
  held-out testing, a controlled topic-shared ablation, and multi-seed
  replication, that tests whether the validation-minus-test gap is
  memorization and finds that it is not. The memorized answer key the
  optimizer writes does not improve the reward, and the gap is
  consistent with small-sample selection under a noisy judge.
- •
  A rigorous held-out evaluation of reflective prompt optimization on a
  multi-agent research system with a language-model-judge citation
  reward, with bootstrap confidence intervals and a paired significance
  test on 500 tasks, replicated across 3 optimization seeds (mean
  held-out gain 0.107, significant in every run).
- •
  A controlled verifier ablation identifying decomposed per-dimension
  feedback as the causal ingredient behind transfer, since collapsing
  the reward to a single scalar drives held-out transfer negative, while
  finer resolution and repeated scoring do not raise it and reward
  gaming tracks the weakest verifiers rather than the strongest.
- •
  A per-lever, error-analyzed account of which prompt edits drove the
  transferable gain and where the residual failures concentrate,
  including a prompt autopsy showing that nearly half of the winning
  prompt by word count is a memorized answer key that does not improve
  the reward, even on the exact training questions it encodes.

## 2 Related work

#### Automatic prompt optimization.

Automatic prompt optimization searches the natural-language input space
of a fixed model rather than its weights. Early methods treat the
language model as a black-box optimizer that proposes and scores
instructions, as in APE \[Zhou et al., 2023\] and OPRO \[Yang et al.,
2024\], and a parallel line borrows from evolutionary computation by
mutating and recombining prompts, as in Promptbreeder \[Fernando et al.,
2024\] and EvoPrompt \[Guo et al., 2024\]. Later work makes the search
more structured and sample-efficient, including PromptWizard \[Agarwal
et al., 2024\], the study of instructions and exemplars by Wan et al.
\[2024\], and the joint instruction-and-demonstration search of
MIPROv2 \[Opsahl-Ong et al., 2024\]. GEPA \[Agrawal et al., 2025\]
extends this line by reflecting on full execution traces in natural
language, and we use its reference implementation \[GEPA-AI, 2025\] as
the optimizer in this study. A related text-space optimizer,
SkillOpt \[Yang et al., 2026c\], trains a persistent skill document with
bounded, validation-gated edits and an update rule that explicitly
forbids hardcoding task-specific values, a design we return to in
Section 6.

#### Optimizing multi-module language-model programs.

Modern agents are multi-module language-model programs, so optimizing
them is more than tuning a single instruction \[Bai and Shi, 2026\]. The
DSPy line frames these systems as declarative programs that can be
compiled and optimized \[Khattab et al., 2022, Khattab et al., 2024\],
and LangProbe \[Tan et al., 2025\] provides a benchmark for such
programs. Recent methods optimize across modules, whether by combining
fine-tuning with prompt search \[Soylu et al., 2024\], aligning local
rewards to a global objective in Optimas \[Wu et al., 2025b\], or
composing policy gradients with prompt optimization in multi-module
GRPO \[Ziems et al., 2025\]. Our system is a two-module program, an
orchestrator and a researcher, and we optimize both.

#### Reflection and natural-language feedback.

A growing body of work treats natural-language feedback, rather than a
scalar, as the learning signal. TextGrad backpropagates textual
critiques through a system of language-model calls \[Yuksekgonul et al.,
2025\], and Xu et al. \[2025\] give conditions under which such feedback
provably improves a policy. Related test-time methods accumulate
reusable lessons in an external memory, as in dynamic
cheatsheets \[Suzgun et al., 2025\] and agent workflow memory \[Wang et
al., 2024\]. These build on prompting strategies that externalize
reasoning and action, including chain-of-thought \[Wei et al., 2023\]
and ReAct \[Yao et al., 2023\]. Reflective prompt evolution inherits the
premise that a written diagnosis carries more information than a single
reward number.

#### Reinforcement learning and verifiable rewards for agents.

The alternative to prompt optimization is reinforcement learning on the
model weights. GRPO and its relatives are effective but sample-hungry,
often needing thousands of rollouts \[Shao et al., 2024\], and a line of
work applies them to retrieval and search agents \[Xu et al., 2026\],
including Search-R1 \[Jin et al., 2025\], ReSearch \[Chen et al.,
2025\], R1-Searcher \[Song et al., 2025\], and FrugalRAG \[Java et al.,
2025\], with Wu et al. \[2025a\] reducing wasteful searches. Because
these methods need a reward, verifiable-reward engineering has become
its own topic, from generalizing verifiable instruction
following \[Pyatkin et al., 2025\] to verification pipelines such as
VerIF \[Peng et al., 2025\]. Rubric-style rewards are especially prone
to hacking, which has motivated policy-aware rubric design \[Tyagi et
al., 2026, Yang et al., 2026a\], direct characterizations of rubric
reward hacking \[Mahmoud et al., 2026, Wang et al., 2026b\], and
rubric-free distillation alternatives \[Rezaei et al., 2026\]. Our
reward is a language-model-judged rubric rather than a programmatic
verifier, and we optimize the prompt rather than the weights, which
avoids rollout cost but introduces the leakage risk we measure.

#### Citation faithfulness and source attribution.

Our task is citation quality for deep-research agents, where the concern
is whether a cited source actually supports its claim. Onweller et al.
\[2026\] document that such agents routinely emit citations that are
present but unverified, and they provide parsing and evaluation
machinery for source attribution that our reward operationalizes.
Faithfulness has also been pursued through training, whether by teaching
contextual faithfulness with synthetic tasks and reinforcement
learning \[Si et al., 2025\] or by detecting and mitigating
hallucination in reasoning models \[Sun et al., 2025\], and it is
measured by multi-hop verification benchmarks such as HotpotQA \[Yang et
al., 2018\] and HoVer \[Jiang et al., 2020\]. The reliability of
language-model rubric verifiers for this exact setting, deep-research
source attribution, has itself been benchmarked against human
labels \[Leung et al., 2026\], which informs the per-dimension judge
choices in our reward. Because agent behaviour depends heavily on the
surrounding harness \[Sen et al., 2026a, Lumer et al., 2026\] and
retrieval structure \[Sen et al., 2026b, Lumer et al., 2025\], prompt
edits that change how an agent searches and cites can move citation
quality without any weight update. We differ from all of this work in
what we measure. Prior prompt-optimization studies report
in-distribution gains, but on factual tasks such a gain conflates a
transferable behavioural change with answers the optimizer memorized
from the training examples, and our protocol separates the two, testing
directly whether the reported gain survives on topics the optimizer
never saw.

## 3 Method

### 3.1 System under optimization

The agent is a multi-agent research system in which an orchestrator
plans and delegates to a research sub-agent \[Lumer et al., 2026\] that
searches a frozen corpus with a search tool, consolidates citations, and
writes a cited report. We optimize the two system prompts.

- •
  The orchestrator prompt, covering planning, delegation, synthesis,
  report structure, and citation rules.
- •
  The researcher prompt, covering search strategy, evidence standards,
  and response format.

### 3.2 Reward

The reward is a gated composite in $`[0,1]`$, unchanged by this study,
designed around the source-attribution failure modes documented for
deep-research agents \[Onweller et al., 2026\].

```math
\displaystyle R=\texttt{answer\_gate}\cdot\big( \displaystyle w_{\text{link}}\,\texttt{link\_works}+w_{\text{rel}}\,\texttt{relevant\_content} \tag{1}
```

```math
\displaystyle+w_{\text{fact}}\,\texttt{fact\_check}+w_{\text{num}}\,\texttt{citation\_numbering}\big)
```

The answer_gate is a correctness gate, so a wrong answer zeroes the
whole reward regardless of citation quality, and it is evaluated by a
tiered check that escalates from an exact substring match to a coverage
test to a language-model judgment (Appendix C). The citation levers are
per-citation scores averaged over a task.

- •
  link_works is 1 when a cited URL exists in the corpus.
- •
  relevant_content measures whether the cited source is about the claim.
- •
  fact_check measures whether the cited source supports the specific
  claim and figures.
- •
  citation_numbering checks that inline references are numbered and
  matched to a source list.

The 4 weights and the verbatim judge prompts are given in Appendix C.

### 3.3 GEPA configuration

We optimize both components with the open-source GEPA
implementation \[GEPA-AI, 2025\], using Pareto candidate selection, a
reflection minibatch size of 3, and a rollout budget of 240 metric
calls. GEPA is wired to the agent through a standard adapter with three
functions. A rollout function runs the agent with the candidate prompts,
an evaluation function scores with the composite reward and renders
textual feedback that names each lever and surfaces the weakest
citations’ judge reasons, and a record function logs traces for the
reflector.

### 3.4 Topic-disjoint split

Each task identifier encodes its source article, and questions from the
same article share a slug. We group tasks by slug and assign whole topic
groups to test first, then validation, then train, which guarantees zero
topic overlap across the 3 splits (Table 1). Train and validation are 24
tasks over 12 topics each, and test is 500 tasks over 254 topics. The
small train and validation sets sit within the 20 to 100 window reported
for GEPA \[Decagon, 2026\]. The 500-task set is held-out evaluation
data, not optimization data.

### 3.5 Ablation protocols

Beyond the main held-out evaluation we run two sets of controlled
experiments that each vary a single axis of the setup while holding the
rest fixed. The first varies the data split and the optimizer seed to
test whether the gain is real and whether the validation-minus-held-out
gap is memorization, and the second varies the verifier to test which
property of the reward signal makes the learned rules transfer.

#### Split and seed.

A _multi-seed_ protocol repeats the optimization with an identical
configuration under 3 independent seeds and compares the held-out gains,
which separates the direction and significance of the effect from
run-to-run variance in which candidate the optimizer selects. A
_topic-shared_ evaluation then measures whether the memorized answer key
actually earns reward. We hold the seed and the selected best prompt
fixed, run no further search, and score both on questions drawn from the
12 training article families. These questions are the 24 exact training
questions the prompt could have memorized, plus 96 fresh in-corpus
questions on the same families (8 per family, verbatim-quote verified
and deduplicated). Because the frozen corpus is unchanged, link
resolution and search behave identically. We add no documents, only new
questions over documents the agent can already retrieve. Six families
whose answers appear verbatim in the winning prompt are flagged, so the
topic-shared set decomposes into memorized and non-memorized families
(leaky_split_manifest.json).

#### Verifier configurations.

The second set of experiments holds the data, policy, reflector, and
rollout budget fixed and varies only the verifier GEPA optimizes
against. The canonical verifier uses a fine-grained reward, a single
scoring pass, and per-dimension decomposed textual feedback that names
each citation lever. From it we form 3 single-knob variants: a _coarse_
3-level reward resolution, _3x repeated_ scoring with judge averaging
and a majority answer gate, and a _scalar_ variant that collapses the
per-dimension feedback into one aggregate number. We run each of the 4
configurations at 2 seeds, for 8 optimization runs. We then re-score
every optimized prompt on a fixed held-out set of 120 topic-disjoint
tasks with a single canonical verifier, so that transfer is comparable
across configurations regardless of the verifier each was optimized
under. Per configuration we report the validation gain under its own
verifier, the canonical held-out gain (transfer), and their difference
as a gaming gap.

## 4 Experiments

### 4.1 Experimental setup

After searching on the 24-task validation set, we evaluate the seed
prompt and the best prompt on the full 500-task held-out test set with a
checkpointed and resumable runner, scoring every task with the same
gated composite reward used during optimization. We report the mean
paired reward change with a bootstrap 95% confidence interval over
tasks \[Efron, 1979\], a Wilcoxon signed-rank test \[Wilcoxon, 1945\]
and a paired $`t`$-test for significance, the effect size $`d_{z}`$, and
the win, tie, and loss counts. For the lever-level analysis we re-score
every candidate on validation, and we re-score the seed and best prompts
on a 200-task held-out subset with the reward’s per-citation reasons
enabled, which lets us decompose the reward and collect the material for
the error analysis.

### 4.2 Results

#### Optimization trajectory.

Over 240 rollouts GEPA produced 7 candidates (Table 3), and validation
reward climbs along the accepted frontier from 0.672 at the seed to
0.874 at the best candidate, discovered at rollout 156 (Figure 1). Both
prompt components contributed, with the first gain coming from the
researcher prompt and the two largest coming from the orchestrator
prompt. The last two candidates grew the prompts further and both
regressed on validation, so Pareto selection kept the shorter candidate 4. The rejection of the longer candidates 5 and 6 is in-run evidence for
the bloat effect that production users mitigate with length
regularization \[Decagon, 2026\], supplied here by the Pareto objective
without an explicit length penalty.

the seed by 0.079 with a tight bootstrap confidence interval and a
highly significant Wilcoxon test (Table 4). Because this test set shares
no topic with anything the optimizer saw, it is our headline held-out
estimate. The gain is a net improvement rather than a clean sweep, with
287 tasks improving, 147 regressing, and 66 unchanged. The per-task
reward distribution shifts tasks into the top bucket (Figure 3), the
per-topic change is right-shifted with a positive mean (Figure 3), and
the per-topic scatter is denser above the line of equality (Figure 4).

### 4.3 Robustness across seeds

Repeating the optimization under 3 independent seeds shows the held-out
gain is a property of the method, not of one lucky run (Table 6). Every
seed produces a positive held-out gain whose bootstrap confidence
interval excludes zero, with a mean of 0.107 and a range of 0.079 to
0.130. What is not stable is the validation-minus-held-out gap, which
ranges from 0.034 to 0.160, a factor of about 5. A quantity that
measured the memorized portion of the same selected prompt should not
swing this much across seeds, so the spread is direct evidence that the
gap reflects optimizer-selection and judge variance rather than a stable
memorization signal.

### 4.4 Does the memorized answer key earn reward?

The topic-shared evaluation isolates the effect of the memorized answer
key by scoring the fixed seed and best prompts on questions from the
training families, with no further search (Table 7). On the 24 exact
training questions, which is the best possible case for a memorized
lookup, the best prompt beats the seed by only 0.090. That gain is
statistically indistinguishable from the 0.079 held-out gain and nowhere
near the 0.202 validation gain, and the answer gate stays flat at 0.92
for both prompts, so the memorized key does not even raise answer
recall. If memorization drove the validation gain, this exact-leak
number would approach 0.202, but it does not. On fresh same-topic
questions the memorized families gain less (0.071) than the
non-memorized families (0.154), so the answer-key blocks behave as
prompt bloat that slightly depresses performance rather than as a useful
lookup table. Several memorized entries do not even match the dataset’s
gold answers, which reinforces that the key is not a reliable table of
answers. A topic-shared split does inflate the apparent gain modestly
(0.112 versus the 0.079 disjoint gain), but the inflation is the
transfer of general citation discipline to same-topic questions, not
memorized reward, and it remains far below the validation gain.

### 4.5 Which verifier property drives transfer?

This ablation varies the verifier while holding the data, policy,
reflector, and budget fixed, and re-scores every optimized prompt on one
canonical held-out verifier so the configurations are comparable
(Table 8, Figure 6). It varies three properties of the reward signal
that supervises the search, its resolution, whether scoring is repeated,
and whether the feedback is decomposed per dimension, and asks which of
them determines what transfers and where reward gaming appears. The
canonical held-out baseline is 0.682 over 120 topic-disjoint tasks, and
each configuration is 2 seeds, so we read the cell means as directional
rather than precise (Section 6).

#### Decomposed feedback is what makes the gain transfer.

Replacing the per-dimension textual feedback with a single scalar reward
drives held-out transfer negative, from $`+0.075`$ for the decomposed
reference to $`-0.021`$ for the scalar variant. Per seed the scalar
reward either found no accepted improvement at all or raised validation
while regressing on held-out, so a reflector given only a scalar has
nothing to diagnose and cannot discover transferable citation-discipline
rules. The levers that carry the transferable gain in the main results,
link_works, relevant_content, and fact_check, are exactly the
per-dimension signals that the scalar variant discards.

#### Finer or repeated scoring buys stability, not transfer.

Along the reliability ordering of coarse, fine, and fine with 3x
repetition, mean transfer is flat to declining at 0.079, 0.075, and
0.059, so more granular or more repeated judging did not improve
generalization at a fixed budget, and repetition cost 3x the judge
calls. Its one benefit was stability, since repeated scoring gave the
tightest seed-to-seed spread at 0.018, against 0.037 for the reference
and 0.079 for the coarse verifier.

#### Reward gaming tracks weak verifiers, and held-out testing exposes it.

The gaming gap, the difference between the validation gain and the
held-out transfer, is largest for the low-information verifiers rather
than the strongest one, at 0.071 for coarse and 0.061 for scalar on top
of its negative transfer, against 0.043 for the fine decomposed
reference (Figure 7). Stronger verification therefore did not induce
more gaming in this setting, low-information verification did, and the
held-out re-scoring is what reveals it, most starkly for the scalar cell
where a positive validation delta hides a held-out regression.

### 4.6 A hand-written recipe baseline

The transferable rules the optimizer found are describable, which raises
the question of whether a practitioner could write them by hand and skip
the search, so we test that directly. We construct a _recipe_ prompt
equal to the best prompt with all memorized answer-key content removed,
dropping the Known Benchmark Facts and Known Prior Research Hints blocks
and the two answer-bearing citation examples, and keeping only the
describable rules of answer-first structure, one claim per citation,
verified page content, corpus-only URLs, and sequential numbering. We
score this recipe against the seed and the best prompt on 100 held-out
tasks under the same policy, reward, and corpus (Table 9).

The recipe alone recovers 0.047 of the 0.083 total held-out gain on
these tasks, about 57%, so a practitioner who writes down the rules
captures most of the improvement. The full optimized prompt adds a
further 0.036, but this increment is not individually significant at 100
tasks (95% confidence interval $`[-0.021,+0.096]`$), consistent with the
memorized answer key being inert (Section 4.4). The total seed-to-best
gain of 0.083 is significant and matches the headline 0.079 on the full
500-task set. The improvement is therefore largely a transferable,
describable recipe, and reflective search adds only a small residual
that we cannot separate from noise at this sample size.

### 4.7 Cost and latency

Because prompt optimization changes only the prompt, the optimized agent
runs at inference with no added machinery, and we confirm it carries no
latency penalty. On 6 held-out queries per prompt at concurrency one,
the optimized prompt averaged 138.8 seconds per query against 159.9 for
the seed, and output length was unchanged at about 3200 tokens. The gain
does cost input tokens, since mean total policy tokens rose about 32%,
from 146.0k to 193.1k per query, almost entirely on the input side (an
added 47k), because the optimized prompt retrieves and grounds more
context per query. GEPA’s search is a one-time cost of 240 policy
rollouts, on the order of 40M policy tokens, after which every query
runs at the per-query cost above with no recurring optimizer overhead.
These are small-sample point estimates over 6 queries per prompt,
reported for scale rather than as a powered comparison.

### 4.8 Judge validation and self-preference

The reward is scored by language-model judges \[Zheng et al., 2023\], so
we check that they agree with a human and are not inflated by a shared
provider family \[Panickssery et al., 2024, Pombal et al., 2026\]. One
author labeled 48 held-out (attribution, citation) pairs blind to the
judge decision, and we re-scored the same pairs with an out-of-family
judge, Anthropic Claude Opus 4.8 (Table 10). The in-family
factual-support judge, which scores the binding reward lever, matched
the human on 80.0% of pairs (Cohen’s $`\kappa=0.60`$; Cohen, 1960), and
its disagreements are mildly lenient, accepting topically-close sources
on 7 of its 9 mismatches. The relevance judge matched on 77.8%
($`\kappa=0.23`$), and all 10 of its disagreements are one-directional
under-crediting, where it withholds relevance because the source does
not also support the specific fact, so it never inflates the score. The
out-of-family judge agrees with the human as well as or better than the
in-family judge, on 91.1% of relevance and 80.0% of factual-support
decisions, and where the two families differ it is the in-family judge
that is the more lenient. Re-scoring the composite with the
out-of-family judge on 12 held-out tasks, the seed-to-best gain persists
and is if anything larger, 0.129 against 0.069 in-family on the same
tasks, so the improvement is not an artifact of the judges’ provider
family. These are spot-checks with 48 pairs and a single annotator, and
they complement the larger judge-versus-human benchmark of Leung et al.
\[2026\].

## 5 Discussion

The winning lineage rewrote the researcher prompt once and the
orchestrator twice. Across these edits the reflector added
topic-independent rules, each targeting a different citation lever. It
moved the answer to the front of the report, split multi-claim sentences
so that one claim carries one citation, required the agent to verify
that the cited page’s main content supports the specific claim, and
restricted citations to corpus URLs. These edits are what raise the 3
citation levers on held-out data.

The verifier ablation (Section 4.5) explains why the reflector could
find these transferable rules at all. Each of them answers a specific
diagnostic the decomposed reward surfaces, that a link failed to
resolve, that a cited page was off-topic, or that a page did not support
the claim, and the reflector rewrites the prompt to close the named
failure. When the same reward is collapsed to a single scalar the
reflector loses that diagnostic channel, and transfer turns negative, so
the mechanism behind the gain is the per-dimension feedback rather than
the optimizer’s search machinery on its own. This also reframes the
reward signal as the design variable that matters most, since a
practitioner who reports only a scalar would both learn less and be more
exposed to the gaming that the held-out protocol exists to catch.

The reflector also made a second kind of edit. It wrote Known Benchmark
Facts and Known Prior Research Hints sections that memorized specific
training answers, for example a language count for one model, a product
name for one company, and a policy date for one central bank. This is a
textbook case of prompt-level overfitting \[Fu et al., 2026\], in which
the optimizer copies specific training values into the prompt instead of
learning a rule that generalizes, an attempted reward hack against the
training signal \[Skalse et al., 2022, Mahmoud et al., 2026\]. This
memorization is not confined to the candidates that Pareto selection
rejected. It appears in the first accepted edit and grows to dominate
the winning program, in which the memorized answer key is 52% of the
orchestrator prompt and 39% of the researcher prompt by word count.
Nearly half of the deployed program is therefore a table of verbatim
training questions paired with their exact answers and source URLs
(Table 11). The natural reading is that this key is what inflates the
validation score, but the topic-shared ablation (Section 4.4) shows it
is not. The memorized key adds only 0.090 even on the exact training
questions it encodes, which is indistinguishable from the transferable
held-out gain and far below the 0.202 validation gain, and it helps the
memorized families less than the non-memorized ones. The memorized
content is therefore attempted reward hacking that does not pay off,
raising the prompt’s length far more than its reward, and the
validation-minus-held-out gap is better explained by small-sample
selection under a noisy judge, which we take up next.

topic-disjoint held-out test, not the Pareto objective, that ultimately
separates the transferable recipe from the memorized and over-instructed
content.

### 5.1 Why the topic-disjoint protocol is necessary

The protocol has three parts, and each catches a failure the others
miss. The topic-disjoint split is what makes a held-out score mean
transfer, because no article family is shared across train, validation,
and test, so a memorized training answer cannot inflate the held-out
number. Held-out evaluation then converts the apparent validation gain
into a transfer estimate, and the gap between the two measures the part
that does not transfer. A practitioner who selected and reported on
validation alone would have described a gain of 0.202 when the
transferable gain is 0.079, an overstatement of about 2.6 times.

The gap is not, however, a stable measurement of memorized reward, and
two results establish this. Across the candidates the memorized
answer-key fraction and the validation reward rise together (Pearson
$`r=0.83`$), from 0% at the seed to 48% at the selected best (Table 11,
Table 3), which invites the reading that the optimizer is buying
validation reward with memorization. That reading mistakes correlation
for causation, because memorization and the transferable
citation-discipline edits were added along the same accepted lineage, so
the two rise together. The topic-shared ablation (Section 4.4)
nonetheless shows the memorized content earns no reward even on the
questions it encodes. What remains true is that a validation-only
workflow surfaces the most-memorized program as its best, so the
practitioner not only mis-sizes the gain but also ships the most
overfitted prompt, even though that overfitting is chasing judge noise
rather than memorized reward.

The prompt autopsy supplies qualitative ground truth for the overfitting
itself. Reading the winning prompt confirms that its Known Benchmark
Facts sections pair specific training questions with their exact
answers, for example a fixed language count for one model and a fixed
policy date for one central bank. The overfitting is therefore literal
memorization rather than benign paraphrase. The autopsy establishes that
the memorized content is real and large, and the ablation establishes
that it is non-functional. Together they separate a genuine
prompt-hygiene problem from the size of the transfer gap.

The topic-shared counterfactual is no longer only a prediction. The
ablation in Section 4.4 constructs same-family questions and scores the
fixed prompts on them, and it does not collapse the gap toward the
validation number. The apparent gain rises only to 0.112, well short of
the 0.202 validation gain, and the memorized families gain less than the
non-memorized ones. Because the gap is dominated by small-sample
selection under a noisy judge, we expect it to be largest not where
memorization is heaviest but where the validation set is smallest and
the judge noisiest. We separately expect memorization pressure to be
largest in narrow, stationary domains where the same entities recur
(Section 6).

## 6 Limitations and Future Work

#### The reward is a language-model judge, validated only at small scale here.

Every number in this paper is measured by the composite reward, which is
itself scored by language-model judges. A spot-check of 48 held-out
pairs (Section 4.8) shows the judges agree with a human, most strongly
on the binding factual-support lever ($`\kappa=0.60`$), and the
per-dimension judge models were validated more broadly against human
labels in a companion benchmark \[Leung et al., 2026\]. Our own check is
single-annotator and small, so a larger multi-annotator study on the
optimized outputs would strengthen it, but the reported gain is a gain
against judges of demonstrated human agreement rather than an unaudited
proxy.

#### Self-preference and same-family bias.

The policy, the reflector, and the citation judges are all from the same
provider family, which risks the reflector learning to please the
judge’s scoring idiosyncrasies and same-family agreement inflating the
measured reward \[Panickssery et al., 2024, Pombal et al., 2026\]. We
check this directly in Section 4.8, where an out-of-family judge agrees
with the human as well as or better than the in-family judge and the
seed-to-best gain persists under it, so the improvement is not an
artifact of the shared family. The check uses one alternative model on a
small sample, so a larger multi-provider judge panel would strengthen
it, though the current evidence points against a self-preference
confound.

#### Constraining the optimizer against memorization.

Our protocol detects prompt-level overfitting but does not prevent it.
The reflector is free to write anything into the prompt, including the
memorized answer key of Section 5, and the topic-disjoint split only
discounts that content at evaluation rather than stopping it at the
source. Here the memorized key turned out not to improve the reward, so
constraining it is a matter of prompt hygiene and auditability rather
than recovering lost accuracy, but in a domain where such content did
transfer the same edit would be an active hazard. A complementary
direction is to constrain the update step itself \[Fu et al., 2026\].
SkillOpt \[Yang et al., 2026c\] enforces generalizability inside the
optimizer prompt, instructing the editor that edits must be
generalizable and must not hardcode task-specific values, filtering
example-specific proposals before they are applied, and accepting only
bounded edits that pass a held-out validation gate. A
generalization-constrained, validation-gated update rule of this kind
could suppress memorized-answer-key edits during optimization instead of
leaving them to be caught after the fact, and pairing such a rule with
our protocol is a natural next step.

#### Small-sample validation and the winner’s curse.

The optimizer selects its best candidate on 24 validation tasks scored
by a stochastic judge, so the reported validation reward of the winner
is subject to winner’s-curse inflation. Selecting the maximum over
several noisy candidates favours the one that caught the most upward
noise, a regressional-Goodhart effect \[Manheim and Garrabrant, 2018,
Gao et al., 2023\]. This is the most likely source of the
validation-minus-held-out gap, consistent with the gap’s instability
across seeds (0.034 to 0.160, Section 4.3) and with the ablation showing
the memorized content earns no reward (Section 4.4). A larger validation
set, an explicit selection penalty, or repeated scoring to average out
judge noise would shrink the gap, but we did not run these and leave
them for future work.

#### Reward engineering as a complementary guard against hacking.

A complementary intervention point is the reward function itself.
Verbatim answer injection is detectable at scoring time: a
prompt-content novelty penalty, subtracting a term proportional to token
overlap between the candidate prompt and training questions, would
directly penalize this strategy in-run rather than catching it post-hoc
via the held-out split. In addition, a judge could be prompted to
identify leakage. More broadly, reward ensembles have shown promise in
improving robustness against reward hacking \[Coste et al., 2024\], and
the same principle applies to prompt optimization: scoring candidates
against multiple independent reward heads makes it harder to inflate any
single proxy. Designing more robust and tamper-resistant reward
functions and detecting misalignment via behavioral or distributional
anomaly detection \[Yang et al., 2026b\] are both active directions for
mitigating reward hacking, and adapting these techniques to the
prompt-optimization setting, where the policy becomes the prompt instead
of model weights, is a natural extension of the present work \[Wang et
al., 2026a\].

#### Three seeds, not many.

The held-out gain replicates across 3 independent seeds (Section 4.3),
so its direction and significance are robust rather than a single lucky
run. What varies is the magnitude of the gain (0.079 to 0.130) and which
candidate the optimizer selects, and 3 seeds is too few to characterize
that spread precisely. We therefore do not treat the
validation-minus-held-out gap as a stable estimate of anything. It is a
diagnostic that flags overfitting, not a calibrated measurement.

#### The verifier ablation is directional.

The verifier ablation (Section 4.5) runs each of the 4 configurations at
only 2 seeds, and the seed-to-seed spreads are wide. We therefore read
the cell means as directional rather than as tight estimates and do not
lean on the exact ordering among the decomposed cells. The robust
readings are the two that survive the spread. Scalar feedback collapses
transfer, and there is no reliability-to-transfer trend. All cells are
re-scored by a single canonical verifier from one provider family on a
120-task held-out subset, so the transfer numbers inherit that judge’s
biases, and confirming the mechanism with an out-of-family verifier and
more seeds is the natural follow-up.

#### Reward under-specification and corpus binding.

The reward weights, the tiered answer-gate definition, and the judge
prompts are released with the artifact but should also be stated in the
paper. The search tool queries a frozen corpus, so the
link-accessibility and URL-discipline conclusions are relative to that
corpus.

#### A single agent, task, and domain.

We study one multi-agent deep-research agent on one citation task drawn
from a single news corpus, so the size of the leakage and the exact
transferable recipe are specific to this setting. Whether reflective
optimization leaks as heavily on other tasks, agents, and domains is
open, and we expect the memorization pressure to be larger, not smaller,
in narrow domains. A domain with a small and recurring entity space, a
fixed document template, or a reward that keys on a few specific facts
gives the optimizer more opportunity to bake answers into the prompt and
more reward for doing so. Replicating the protocol on such a domain,
with splits that are disjoint by entity or document source rather than
by news topic, is the natural test of external validity.

#### Held-out monitoring for deployed prompt optimization.

In this setting the gap was dominated by selection noise, but the same
instrumentation is what would catch the more dangerous case in which
memorized content does transfer. Recomputing the held-out gain on a
schedule against a refreshed, entity-disjoint set, and pairing it with a
periodic prompt autopsy, gives two complementary signals for a deployed
prompt-optimized system. The held-out score tracks whether the gain
still generalizes, and the autopsy flags when the optimizer has started
baking specific entities into the prompt. In a stationary domain a
baked-in answer keeps scoring well until the entity it names changes, at
which point the held-out score falls while the in-distribution score
does not. Tracking these signals over time, rather than treating any
single validation-minus-held-out gap as a calibrated leakage
measurement, is a concrete direction for keeping reflective optimization
auditable in deployment.

## 7 Conclusion

Reflective prompt optimization delivered a statistically significant,
generalizing improvement to a deep-research agent’s citation reward. The
held-out gain was 0.079 on 500 topic-disjoint tasks with no weight
updates, it replicated across 3 seeds at a mean of 0.107, and it spread
across the citation-quality levers rather than concentrating in one.
Factual support, the hardest lever, carried the largest share. The
methodological contribution is the protocol rather than the optimizer. A
topic-disjoint held-out test, a controlled topic-shared ablation,
multi-seed replication, and a verifier ablation together let us ask
whether the large validation-minus-held-out gap is memorization, and the
answer is that it is not. The optimizer does write a literal memorized
answer key, nearly half of the best prompt by word count, but that
content earns no reward even on the exact training questions it encodes.
The validation over-statement is therefore small-sample selection under
a noisy judge, not memorized reward. Varying the verifier shows what
does drive the gain. Decomposed per-dimension feedback is the causal
ingredient, since a scalar reward drives held-out transfer negative
while finer or repeated scoring does not raise it. The recipe the search
discovered is a portable prompting pattern for citation-faithful
research agents, namely an answer-first structure, one claim per
citation, verified page content, and corpus-only URLs. A hand-written
version of this recipe recovers most of the held-out gain, which
confirms the improvement is a transferable rule set rather than a
property of the search itself. It complements rather than replaces
reinforcement learning, and it generalizes only when the reward is
decomposed and the evaluation is held-out and multi-seed.

optimization step runs the agent with the seed prompts on the train and
validation splits and searches with GEPA under the configuration in

and re-scores the seed and best prompts on the 200-task held-out subset
with per-citation reasons enabled. The reporting step derives the prompt
lineage and diffs, the paired held-out statistics, the figures, the

## Appendix C Reward weights, answer gate, and judge prompts

This appendix specifies the gated composite reward of Equation (1)
exactly as used in the run analysed here and in every re-evaluation.

#### Composite weights and aggregation.

The four weights are $`w_{\text{link}}=0.25`$, $`w_{\text{rel}}=0.30`$,
$`w_{\text{fact}}=0.30`$, and $`w_{\text{num}}=0.15`$, which sum to
1.00. Each of link_works, relevant_content, and fact_check is the
unweighted mean over every parsed (attribution, citation) pair in the
report, and a report with no parseable citation pair scores 0 on all
three. Two levers are deterministic rather than model-scored. link_works
is 1 when the cited URL resolves to content in the frozen corpus, and
citation_numbering rewards inline \[n\](url) citations numbered $`1..N`$
contiguously with a matching Sources list, penalizing gaps, duplicates,
and mismatches. All judges see frozen-corpus content only, with no live
web fetch at reward time.

#### Answer gate.

The gate is a stateless, cheapest-first cascade returning $`\{0,1\}`$.
Tier 1 is a deterministic number-canonicalized substring match of the
gold answer in the report (thousands separators stripped, unit forms
such as “41 billion”, “41bn”, and “41B” unified, quotes and dashes
normalized, whitespace collapsed, lowercased); a multi-part gold answer
joined by ; or \| passes only if all parts are present. Tier 2 is a
deterministic coverage router on the fraction of the gold answer’s
distinctive tokens present in the report, with a pass threshold of 1.0
and a fail threshold of 0.5. Coverage at or above 1.0 passes, coverage
below 0.5 fails with no language-model call, and the near-miss band in
between escalates to Tier 3. Tier 3 fires only in that near-miss band
and uses OpenAI GPT-5-mini (gpt-5-mini-2025-08-07, max 256 tokens,
report truncated to 6000 characters), reasoning before emitting a final
VERDICT: yes or VERDICT: no line. Its verbatim prompt is:

> You judge whether a research REPORT answers the QUESTION with the same
> fact as the GOLD ANSWER. Paraphrase, restatement, and rounding-free
> equivalents count as the same fact; a different or absent fact does
> not.  
> First reason in 1-3 short sentences about whether the report expresses
> the gold fact. Then, on a FINAL separate line, output exactly
> ’VERDICT: yes’ or ’VERDICT: no’.  
> QUESTION: {question}  
> GOLD ANSWER: {truth}  
> REPORT: {report\[:6000\]}

#### Citation judges.

The source-relevance and factual-support levers are binary ($`0/1`$)
language-model judges that return a structured {score, reason} object
and see the cited URL’s corpus content truncated to 5000 characters.
Source relevance uses OpenAI GPT-5-mini (gpt-5-mini-2025-08-07) and
factual support uses OpenAI GPT-5.4-mini (gpt-5.4-mini-2026-03-17),
following the per-dimension picks of Leung et al. \[2026\]. The verbatim
system prompts are:

> Source relevance (OpenAI GPT-5-mini). You are an expert evaluator
> assessing content relevance. Your task is to determine if the content
> at a URL is relevant to a given attribution text. Consider whether the
> URL content discusses the same topic as the attribution, provides
> supporting information for its claims, and has a clear connection to
> it. This is a binary evaluation: score = 1 if the content IS relevant,
> score = 0 if it is NOT.  
> Factual support (OpenAI GPT-5.4-mini). You are an expert fact-checker
> evaluating factual accuracy. Your task is to determine if the factual
> claims in an attribution text are supported by the content at a URL.
> Consider whether the specific facts, numbers, dates, and claims in the
> attribution are present in the URL content, whether they match or are
> consistent, and whether there are any contradictions. This is a binary
> evaluation: score = 1 if the facts ARE supported, score = 0 if they
> are NOT (contradicted, missing, or unverifiable).

Each judge is called with the attribution text, the cited URL, and the
truncated URL content, and is asked to evaluate relevance or factual
support for that pair.
