# FlowCred: Hierarchical Credit Assignment and Functional Coordination for Joint Text-to-Video Post-Training

Mingxuan Zhu Yanwei Huang Shiyuan Liu Xinzhi Zhang Shujie Li Yuanxing
Zhang Lu Chen Shanghai Jiao Tong University  KlingAI The Hong Kong
University of Science and Technology South China University of
Technology  The University of Hong Kong ^(†)^(†)thanks: This work was
conducted during the author’s internship at KlingAI.

###### Abstract

Prompt optimization and generator post-training are two complementary
ways to improve text-to-video (T2V) generation, and recent systems
increasingly train both modules jointly. However, optimizing a prompt
policy and a video generator from the same terminal reward creates two
ambiguities: prompt quality is confounded with stochastic generation,
and the two modules may realize overlapping local improvements. We
introduce FlowCred, a two-level framework for joint T2V post-training.
First, hierarchical credit assignment uses matched prompt–seed rollout
grids to separate prompt-level credit from prompt-conditioned generator
residuals under shared randomness. Second, functional coordination
probes alternative current-policy prompts at states from actual
generation trajectories, providing generator-aware evidence for prompt
optimization while selectively attenuating generator updates already
supported by conditioning changes. Across multiple T2V benchmarks,
FlowCred consistently improves matched joint-training baselines. On
LTX-2.5, FlowCred improves a strictly matched joint-training control
from 61.47 to 64.46 on T2V-CompBench and from 55.58 to 58.08 on
StoryEval, without increasing decoded-video or reward-model-call
budgets. Factorial and realized-update analyses show complementary
contributions from prompt shaping and generator routing, while human
evaluation and transfer across backbones and updater families provide
further supporting evidence.

## 1 Introduction

Text-to-video (T2V) systems increasingly expose two trainable adaptation
channels: a language-side prompt policy and the video generator itself.
Prompt-side methods improve conditioning while leaving the visual model
fixed (Hao et al., 2023; Cao et al., 2023; Ji et al., 2025; Cheng
et al., 2025); generator post-training instead changes the
diffusion/flow model using reward or preference feedback (Black et al.,
2024; Fan et al., 2023; Wallace et al., 2024; Liu et al., 2025a; Xue
et al., 2025; Xue et al., 2026). Recent systems optimize both modules
jointly (Wang et al., 2026a; Cheng et al., 2026; Liu et al., 2026; Jia
et al., 2026). Prompt changes can reorganize entities, relations, and
event descriptions before generation, whereas generator updates address
residual motion, appearance, and execution errors that conditioning
alone cannot remove. When only one module is updated, a terminal reward
can be treated as supervision for that module; once both are trainable,
the same scalar signal must implicitly serve both attribution and
coordination roles. Joint post-training is therefore attractive; once
both channels are trainable, however, one terminal score leaves two
distinct questions unresolved.

The first is _statistical credit_. A terminal reward conflates prompt
quality with downstream generator randomness: identical prompts can
score differently across seeds, while favorable trajectories can mask
weaker prompt choices. The reward alone therefore does not identify
which variation belongs to prompt choice and which remains after the
prompt is fixed. For request $`u`$, prompt action $`p`$, stochastic
generator trajectory $`\mathcal{T}`$, and terminal reward $`R`$, let
$`Q(u,p)=\mathbb{E}[R\mid u,p]`$ and $`V(u)=\mathbb{E}_{p}Q(u,p)`$. Then

```math
R-V(u)=\underbrace{Q(u,p)-V(u)}_{\text{prompt decision}}+\underbrace{R-Q(u,p)}_{\text{prompt-fixed trajectory}}. \tag{1}
```

This exposes two sources of terminal variation. The second question is
_functional coordination_: even after statistical credit is separated,
changing the prompt at a generator state may induce a response aligned
with the same reward-improving direction used by the downstream
optimizer. The two modules can therefore duplicate part of the same
local improvement. Statistical credit and functional overlap are thus
distinct problems.

These two ambiguities require different remedies: variance reduction can
make credit estimates cleaner without preventing functional overlap,
while coordinating local updates does not by itself determine where
terminal variation originated. Treating them separately lets joint
post-training distinguish _who should receive credit_ from _which module
should realize an overlapping improvement_.

To address both, we introduce FlowCred, named for its joint treatment of
credit assignment and coordination across the prompt-to-video generation
flow. Figure 1 summarizes the two ambiguities and their corresponding
mechanisms. Hierarchical credit assignment uses matched prompt–seed
rollouts to separate prompt-level credit from prompt-conditioned
generator residuals. Functional coordination probes alternative prompts
at actual rollout states and uses same-state response geometry for
generator-aware prompt shaping and reliability-gated generator routing.

![](arxiv-2610-18142v1--f0ce8205cd4b.figures/figure-1.webp)

Figure 1: Motivation and positioning of FlowCred. The left panel
contrasts prompt-only adaptation with shared-reward joint training. Once

conditioning-side and generator-side local effects. FlowCred addresses
them with hierarchical credit assignment ($`Q-V`$ versus $`R-Q`$) and
functional coordination based on same-state response geometry.

The two mechanisms have different mathematical status: Eq. 1 is exact
for any per-trajectory scalar score, whereas functional coordination is
local and first-order. Its prompt term is auxiliary shaping and its
generator rule is a minimum-overlap regularizer, not a conserved
transfer of gradient mass. To keep the statistical claim separate from
optimization heuristics, all affine calibration used to combine reward
credit and functional prompt-credit terms is estimated once on disjoint
training-only data and then frozen (Appendix D.3).

Our contributions are threefold.

(1) We introduce hierarchical credit assignment with matched prompt–seed
rollouts, separating prompt-level credit from prompt-conditioned
generator residuals under shared generator randomness. This gives the
prompt policy and generator distinct estimators tied to their positions
in the stochastic hierarchy.

(2) We develop functional coordination from same-state prompt responses,
using local response geometry for generator-aware prompt shaping and
reliability-gated routing of the generator reward update. It targets
overlap that remains even after statistical credit has been separated.

(3) We evaluate FlowCred with controlled joint-training baselines,
mechanism audits, repeated runs, human evaluation, and transfer across
backbones and updater families. Across four T2V benchmarks, FlowCred
consistently improves the matched controls without increasing
decoded-video or reward-model-call budgets.

## 2 Related Work

#### Prompt optimization and generator post-training.

Prompt-side methods optimize conditioning with a fixed generator (Hao
et al., 2023; Cao et al., 2023; Ji et al., 2025; Cheng et al., 2025),
whereas PromptRL, VANS, UniGRPO, and CAPE-T2V couple language- and
generator-side adaptation in different ways (Wang et al., 2026a; Cheng
et al., 2026; Liu et al., 2026; Jia et al., 2026). Generator alignment
spans policy gradients, preference objectives, and differentiable reward
optimization (Black et al., 2024; Fan et al., 2023; Wallace et al.,
2024; Clark et al., 2024; Li et al., 2024; Xu et al., 2023; Kirstain
et al., 2023; Liu et al., 2025a; Choi et al., 2026; Prabhudesai et al.,
2024; Wang et al., 2026b); recent methods further refine reward shaping,
trajectory allocation, and temporal credit (Xue et al., 2025; Xue
et al., 2026; Luo et al., 2026; Zhu et al., 2026; Ye et al., 2026; Pu &
Kong, 2026; Shen et al., 2026; Guan et al., 2026; Savani et al., 2026;
Oba et al., 2026). These directions are complementary to our
cross-module question. Modern open T2V systems span diverse backbone
designs (Chen et al., 2024; Yang et al., 2025; Kong et al., 2024; Wan
et al., 2025); our experiments hold the downstream harness fixed
whenever numerical comparisons require matched conditions.

#### Credit assignment, variance reduction, and functional coordination.

COMA counterfactual credit holds other agents fixed while marginalizing
one action (Foerster et al., 2018), while leave-one-out baselines and
common random numbers are standard tools for variance
reduction (Ahmadian et al., 2024; Ng & Jordan, 2000). PCGrad/CAGrad
manipulate conflicting parameter gradients across objectives (Yu et al.,
2020; Liu et al., 2021). FlowCred instead separates statistical credit
across the stochastic prompt-policy/generator hierarchy and measures
functional overlap through same-state conditioning responses in the
generator’s native output space.

## 3 Method

### 3.1 Joint post-training: credit and coordination

Let $`u\sim\mathcal{D}`$ be a request, $`p\sim\pi_{\phi}(\cdot\mid u)`$
a prompt action, and $`\mathcal{T}\sim\pi_{\theta}(\cdot\mid p)`$ a
stochastic generator trajectory ending in video $`x`$. The shared
objective is

```math
J(\phi,\theta)=\mathbb{E}_{u,p,\mathcal{T}}[R(u,x)]. \tag{2}
```

All rewards are evaluated against the original request $`u`$. We use
_statistical credit_ for how terminal variation is assigned among
sampled decisions and _functional coordination_ for how locally
overlapping conditioning and generator changes are handled. Accordingly,
hierarchical credit assigns terminal variation according to where it
arises, while functional coordination acts only on local function
changes that both trainable modules can express. We refer to
$`\pi_{\phi}`$ as the _prompt optimizer_ or _prompt policy_.

### 3.2 Hierarchical credit assignment

Define
$`Q_{\theta}(u,p)=\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{T}\sim\pi_{\theta}(\cdot\mid p)}[R(u,x)]`$
and
$`V_{\phi,\theta}(u)=\mathbb{E}_{p\sim\pi_{\phi}(\cdot\mid u)}[Q_{\theta}(u,p)]`$;
we suppress parameter subscripts below. Thus $`R-V=(Q-V)+(R-Q)`$
separates prompt choice from prompt-fixed stochasticity. For each
request, sample $`K`$ candidates $`p_{1:K}\sim\pi_{\phi}(\cdot\mid u)`$
and reuse the same $`M`$ generator-randomness seeds $`z_{1:M}`$ for
every candidate:

```math
x_{k,m},\mathcal{T}_{k,m}=G_{\theta}(p_{k};z_{m}),\qquad R_{k,m}=R(u,x_{k,m}). \tag{3}
```

The matched rollout grid yields the paired leave-one-out estimators

```math
\displaystyle\widehat{A}^{P,R}_{k} \displaystyle=\frac{1}{M}\sum_{m}\left(R_{k,m}-\frac{1}{K-1}\sum_{j\neq k}R_{j,m}\right), \tag{4}
```

```math
\displaystyle\widehat{A}^{G}_{k,m} \displaystyle=R_{k,m}-\frac{1}{M-1}\sum_{m^{\prime}\neq m}R_{k,m^{\prime}}.
```

The first contrast compares prompt decisions under common generator
randomness and estimates $`Q-V`$; the second compares trajectories after
the prompt is fixed and estimates $`R-Q`$. Positive same-seed reward
covariance lowers cross-prompt contrast variance (Appendix A). The
prompt optimizer uses $`\widehat{A}^{P,R}`$, while the generator reward
branch uses $`\widehat{A}^{G}`$. Reward-component calibration is
estimated on disjoint training-only data and frozen, so no statistic
from the current $`K\!\times\!M`$ grid enters $`R_{k,m}`$
(Appendix D.3).

#### Controlled global-LOO baseline.

Matched Global-LOO uses the identical $`K\!\times\!M`$ grid but gives
both modules one global leave-one-out trajectory advantage
(Appendix A.2). Averaged over the $`M`$ trajectories for a prompt, this
control preserves the same coarse prompt ordering up to positive scale.
The substantive statistical change in hierarchical credit is therefore
downstream: generator credit becomes the prompt-conditional residual
$`R-Q`$ rather than a global outcome residual.

### 3.3 Functional coordination via same-state responses

Hierarchical credit separates endpoint variation but does not describe
whether prompt conditioning and the generator reward step realize
similar local function changes. We measure that overlap at states
actually encountered by the generator and reuse the same response
geometry for prompt shaping and generator routing. An encountered state
$`s_{t}^{k,m}`$ contains the noisy latent, timestep, and scheduler
quantities required for one transition on trajectory $`(k,m)`$. Let
$`\mu_{t}=\mu_{\theta}(s_{t}^{k,m},p_{k})`$ be the backend-native
transition output. For Full, the base updater’s reward branch on this
trajectory is weighted by $`\widehat{A}^{G}_{k,m}`$ from Eq. 4;
stabilization/reference terms remain in a separate branch:

```math
J_{\mathrm{base},t}=J_{\mathrm{reward},t}+J_{\mathrm{reg},t},\qquad q_{t}^{R}=\nabla_{\mu_{t}}J_{\mathrm{reward},t},\quad g_{t}^{R}=F_{t}^{-1}q_{t}^{R}. \tag{5}
```

Here $`F_{t}`$ is the backend’s local metric (the transition Fisher for
Flow-GRPO), $`q_{t}^{R}`$ is the reward covector, and $`g_{t}^{R}`$ is
its metric-mapped local ascent direction; we write
$`\|v\|_{F_{t}}^{2}=v^{\top}F_{t}v`$. Reference/KL regularization does
not enter $`q_{t}^{R}`$ and is restored unchanged after routing.

We cache the selected state rather than an alternative continuation.
With the detached behavior snapshot
$`\theta^{-}=\operatorname{sg}(\theta)`$, replacing only the executed
prompt $`p_{k}`$ by another current-policy candidate $`p_{j}`$ gives

```math
\displaystyle d_{j\leftarrow k,t} \displaystyle=\mu_{\theta^{-}}(s_{t}^{k,m},p_{j})-\mu_{\theta^{-}}(s_{t}^{k,m},p_{k}),
```

```math
\displaystyle\bar{d}_{j\leftarrow k,t} \displaystyle=\operatorname{TrustClip}_{F_{t}}(d_{j\leftarrow k,t}),\qquad c_{j\leftarrow k,t}=\bar{d}_{j\leftarrow k,t}^{\top}q_{t}^{R}. \tag{6}
```

The probe is intentionally one-transition and same-state:
$`s_{t}^{k,m}`$ is seed/state dependent, but within each comparison the
state, stochastic draw, and behavior snapshot are fixed and only text
conditioning changes. Continuing $`p_{j}`$ would mix this local
conditioning response with later nonlinear state evolution and
additional stochasticity. We use
$`\operatorname{TrustClip}_{F_{t}}(d)=d\min\!\left(1,\sqrt{\tau_{\rm batch}/(d^{\top}F_{t}d+\epsilon_{\rm num})}\right)`$,
where $`\epsilon_{\rm num}=10^{-12}`$ and $`\tau_{\rm batch}`$ is the
detached 95th percentile of the current-batch native-metric response
energy $`d^{\top}F_{t}d`$. Thus in-region responses are unchanged and
only unusually large conditioning shifts are rescaled. The score
$`c_{j\leftarrow k,t}`$ is the resulting local directional derivative
along an observed prompt response; its sign records agreement with the
reward branch. Full-trajectory utility of prompt candidates is still
measured by the $`K\!\times\!M`$ rollouts in Eq. 3.

![](arxiv-2610-18142v1--f0ce8205cd4b.figures/figure-2.webp)

Figure 2: FlowCred training overview. Hierarchical credit assignment
samples $`K`$ prompt candidates and reuses $`M`$ matched generator

while within-prompt residuals estimate $`R-Q`$. A selected rollout
supplies an encountered state $`s_{t}`$ for functional coordination. At
that fixed state, alternative-prompt responses define a local geometry
used for generator-aware prompt shaping and reliability-gated routing of
only the generator reward branch.

#### Generator-aware prompt shaping.

Let $`\mathcal{S}_{k,m}`$ denote the selected routed transition indices
on trajectory $`(k,m)`$ (one by default). Cross-fitting avoids scoring
$`p_{j}`$ on states produced by itself:

```math
C_{j}=\frac{1}{K-1}\sum_{k\neq j}\mathbb{E}_{m,\,t\in\mathcal{S}_{k,m}}[c_{j\leftarrow k,t}],\qquad A_{j}^{P}=\operatorname{Cal}_{R}(\widehat{A}_{j}^{P,R})+\lambda_{F}\operatorname{Cal}_{C}(C_{j}). \tag{7}
```

For $`X\in\{R,C\}`$,
$`\operatorname{Cal}_{X}(x)=(x-\mu_{X}^{\rm cal})/(\sigma_{X}^{\rm cal}+10^{-6})`$
uses statistics estimated once on a disjoint training-only calibration
split and then frozen; $`\operatorname{Cal}_{R}`$ acts on terminal
prompt credit and $`\operatorname{Cal}_{C}`$ on functional evidence. The
terminal term remains primary, while $`C_{j}`$ supplies generator-aware
auxiliary shaping; $`\lambda_{F}`$ is fixed on a disjoint development
split.

#### Reliability-gated generator routing.

Let $`D_{t}=[\bar{d}_{j\leftarrow k,t}]_{j\neq k}`$. The sampled
current-policy response operator is a trace-normalized ridge map. To
estimate whether this finite span generalizes beyond the $`K-1`$ rollout
candidates, we sample $`B_{\rm train}`$ additional intent-preserving
prompt alternatives at the same state. Each requires only one same-state
generator transition forward: no video is decoded and no reward model is
called. With response $`\widetilde{d}_{b}`$, define

```math
\displaystyle P_{\lambda,t} \displaystyle=D_{t}(D_{t}^{\top}F_{t}D_{t}+\lambda_{t}I)^{-1}D_{t}^{\top}F_{t}, \displaystyle\lambda_{t} \displaystyle=\epsilon_{\rm ridge}\frac{\operatorname{tr}(D_{t}^{\top}F_{t}D_{t})}{K-1}, \tag{8}
```

```math
\displaystyle r_{t} \displaystyle=\operatorname{clip}\!\left(\frac{1}{B_{\rm train}}\sum_{b}\frac{\|P_{\lambda,t}\widetilde{d}_{b}\|_{F_{t}}^{2}}{\|\widetilde{d}_{b}\|_{F_{t}}^{2}+\epsilon_{\rm num}},0,1\right), \displaystyle\alpha_{t} \displaystyle=\alpha_{\max}r_{t},
```

```math
\displaystyle g_{t}^{G} \displaystyle=(I-\alpha_{t}P_{\lambda,t})g_{t}^{R}, \displaystyle q_{t}^{G} \displaystyle=F_{t}g_{t}^{G}.
```

Here $`\epsilon_{\rm ridge}`$ is the fixed ridge scale,
$`\alpha_{\max}`$ caps the routing strength, and $`q_{t}^{G}`$ is the
routed reward covector backpropagated through the generator. The score
$`r_{t}`$ measures held-out response support rather than reward
confidence. Low-support states continuously recover the base reward
step. For stochastic-transition updaters such as Flow-GRPO,
$`g_{t}^{R}`$ is a stochastic estimate of the local reward gradient; the
response operator and support gate are built from the cached state and
detached prompt responses. Consequently the same linear routing applies
to the conditional expected reward gradient, rather than implying that
one high-dimensional transition sample itself has large prompt-supported
energy. Appendix A.4 states this distinction explicitly. The same
geometry therefore serves two targets: $`C_{j}`$ shapes the upstream
prompt action, whereas Eq. 8 regularizes the downstream expected reward
update without assuming an exact transfer between optimizers.

### 3.4 Training procedure

One transition per decoded trajectory is routed by default, sampled
uniformly from the valid stochastic-policy steps used by the base reward
loss; all other reward-loss transitions and the entire
regularization/reference branch remain unchanged. Same-state quantities
are stop-gradient targets from the detached behavior snapshot and are
recomputed online each round. Default hyperparameters and
backend-specific instantiations are in Appendices D and A.4.

1: request $`u`$; prompt policy $`\pi_{\phi}`$; generator
$`G_{\theta}`$; $`K`$ candidates; $`M`$ matched seeds

2: Hierarchical credit assignment

3: sample $`p_{1:K}`$ and decode $`x_{k,m}=G_{\theta}(p_{k};z_{m})`$
with shared $`z_{1:M}`$

4: evaluate $`R_{k,m}`$; compute $`\widehat{A}^{P,R}_{k}`$ and
$`\widehat{A}^{G}_{k,m}`$

5: Functional coordination at actual rollout states

6: for each selected transition state $`s_{t}^{k,m}`$ do

7:   obtain $`(q_{t}^{R},g_{t}^{R})`$ from the hierarchy-weighted base
reward branch

8:   probe $`d_{j\leftarrow k,t}`$ for $`j\neq k`$ and accumulate
cross-fitted evidence $`C_{j}`$

9:   construct $`P_{\lambda,t}`$; estimate $`r_{t}`$ with
$`B_{\rm train}`$ non-decoded prompt probes

10:   route
$`g_{t}^{G}\leftarrow(I-\alpha_{\max}r_{t}P_{\lambda,t})g_{t}^{R}`$

11: end for

12: update prompt policy with Eq. 7; update generator with the routed
reward branch plus unchanged regularization

## 4 Experiments

### 4.1 Protocol and controlled comparisons

#### Setup.

Our primary system uses LTX-2.5 (HaCohen et al., 2026; Lightricks,
2026), a Qwen3.5-9B prompt optimizer, Flow-GRPO, and $`K=M=4`$.
Controlled variants share initialization, request stream, prompt
sampling, matched seeds, reward, decoded-video budget, and optimization
schedule; functional coordination adds same-state transition forwards
but no extra video decode. The frozen training reward uses public, fixed
scorers with weights $`0.35/0.30/0.25/0.10`$: the Text-Alignment head of
VideoReward (Liu et al., 2025b) for request faithfulness, frame-averaged
VQAScore (CLIP-FlanT5-XXL) (Lin et al., 2024) for compositional
correspondence, the temporal-consistency/text-alignment aggregate of
VideoScore-v1.1 (He et al., 2024) for event realization, and the
visual-/motion-quality aggregate of VideoReward as a bounded quality
stabilizer. All scorers and calibration statistics are frozen before
controlled training. Appendices D and D.3 specify checkpoints, frame
sampling, calibration, and scorer-family robustness.

#### Evaluation and comparisons.

We report VBench (Huang et al., 2024), VBench-2.0 (Zheng et al., 2025),
T2V-CompBench (Sun et al., 2025), and StoryEval (Wang et al., 2025) on
the original request $`u`$ with matched evaluation seeds. The
hierarchical-credit-versus-Full comparison and the
functional-coordination factorial use three independent training runs;
other trainable baselines use two. The controlled chain spans
prompt-only adaptation, a raw-request Flow-GRPO generator-only
reference, PromptRL, UniGRPO, Matched Global-LOO, hierarchical credit,
and Full. For methods originally reported with different backbones or
training stacks, we implement the published objective in the common
Qwen3.5/LTX-2.5 harness; the table therefore reports controlled
common-harness results rather than copied published absolute scores.
CAPE-T2V is positioned separately because its public LTX recipe uses
sequential two-sided adaptation with additional video-derived caption
supervision and a different released LTX backbone; Appendix D.5 gives
the precise comparison scope. Uncertainty and data-integrity protocols
are summarized in Appendices D.4, G, and J.

## 5 Conclusion

We presented FlowCred, a framework for jointly post-training a prompt
optimizer and a text-to-video generator without treating a shared
terminal reward as undifferentiated supervision. Hierarchical credit
assignment uses matched prompt–seed rollouts to separate prompt choice
from prompt-fixed stochasticity, while functional coordination uses
same-state response geometry for generator-aware prompt shaping and
reliability-gated routing of the generator reward branch. Controlled
comparisons show that the gains cannot be explained by stronger prompts,
additional decoded rollouts, or generic update shrinkage alone, and
evidence from repeated runs, mechanism analyses, human evaluation, and
transfer studies is consistent with the intended division of labor
between prompt-side conditioning and residual generator execution. More
broadly, FlowCred suggests that joint post-training should distinguish
_where outcome variation arises_ from _where trainable modules can
realize overlapping functional changes_: sharing a reward is not
sufficient by itself; credit assignment and update coordination are
separate design problems in cascaded generative systems.

## Appendix A Theoretical Foundations

This appendix collects the mathematical statements used in the main
paper. The reward hierarchy is exact for any per-trajectory scalar
terminal score and yields valid score-function baselines. In the final
protocol, reward-component and prompt-term affine scales are estimated
once on disjoint training-only calibration data and then frozen, so
current minibatch composition does not enter the score used by the
hierarchy (Appendix D.3). Same-state prompt intervention provides a
first-order directional derivative of the generator reward surrogate.
Minimum-overlap routing is a local function-space regularizer; its
non-reversal statement is made in the backend’s local output coordinates
before neural pullback.

### A.1 Hierarchy-aware statistical credit

Recall

```math
J(\phi,\theta)=\mathbb{E}_{u\sim\mathcal{D}}\mathbb{E}_{p\sim\pi_{\phi}(\cdot|u)}\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{T}\sim\pi_{\theta}(\cdot|p)}[R(u,x)], \tag{9}
```

with

```math
Q_{\theta}(u,p)=\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{T}\sim\pi_{\theta}(\cdot|p)}[R(u,x)],\qquad V_{\phi,\theta}(u)=\mathbb{E}_{p\sim\pi_{\phi}(\cdot|u)}Q_{\theta}(u,p). \tag{10}
```

###### Proposition 1 (Hierarchy-aware score-function baselines).

For the factorized policy
$`\pi_{\phi}(p|u)\pi_{\theta}(\mathcal{T}|p)`$,

```math
\displaystyle\nabla_{\phi}J \displaystyle=\mathbb{E}[(Q_{\theta}(u,p)-V_{\phi,\theta}(u))\nabla_{\phi}\log\pi_{\phi}(p|u)], \tag{11}
```

```math
\displaystyle\nabla_{\theta}J \displaystyle=\mathbb{E}[(R(u,x)-\operatorname{sg}\!\left[Q_{\theta}(u,p)\right])\nabla_{\theta}\log\pi_{\theta}(\mathcal{T}|p)]. \tag{12}
```

###### Proof.

For the prompt policy, apply the score-function identity and subtract
$`V(u)`$, which does not depend on the sampled prompt action. For the
generator, any prompt-dependent quantity that is fixed with respect to
the sampled downstream trajectory is a valid score-function baseline;
choosing $`Q(u,p)`$ gives the second expression. The stop-gradient
indicates that the statement concerns a policy-gradient baseline rather
than differentiation through the value estimate. ∎

The pointwise identity $`R-V=(Q-V)+(R-Q)`$ follows immediately. It is a
statistical decomposition of reward variation. Functional coordination
in Section 3.3 then shapes the joint objective using same-state response
evidence, so the two levels have distinct mathematical status.

### A.2 Relation to the matched global-LOO control

The matched control uses the same sampled actions and matched rollout
grid as hierarchical credit, but both modules receive a global
leave-one-out advantage

```math
A^{\rm GLOO}_{k,m}=R_{k,m}-\frac{1}{KM-1}\sum_{(j,n)\neq(k,m)}R_{j,n}. \tag{13}
```

For a fixed prompt action $`p_{k}`$, averaging over its $`M`$
trajectories yields a positive affine rescaling of $`Q_{k}-\bar{Q}`$ and
therefore preserves the coarse prompt ordering. The hierarchical-credit
prompt-policy estimator in Eq. 4 is likewise a positive rescaling of
$`Q_{k}-\bar{Q}`$. We consequently do not attribute the
hierarchical-credit gain to a new prompt ordering. The controlled change
is the downstream generator baseline: Global-LOO removes only the
current trajectory, whereas hierarchical credit removes prompt-level
variation as well by comparing the trajectory against other seeds under
the same executed prompt. The global-LOO construction also eliminates
the self-inclusion confound that would otherwise accompany a global mean
baseline.

### A.3 Variance reduction from matched seeds

For candidates $`p_{k},p_{j}`$, write $`R_{k}(z)`$ and $`R_{j}(z)`$ for
rewards obtained with common seed $`z`$. Define

```math
\widehat{\Delta}_{kj}^{\rm match}=\frac{1}{M}\sum_{m=1}^{M}[R_{k}(z_{m})-R_{j}(z_{m})]. \tag{14}
```

For an independently seeded comparison, use $`z_{m}`$ for $`k`$ and
independent $`z_{m}^{\prime}`$ for $`j`$. Let
$`\sigma_{k}^{2}=\operatorname{Var}(R_{k})`$,
$`\sigma_{j}^{2}=\operatorname{Var}(R_{j})`$, and
$`\sigma_{kj}=\operatorname{Cov}(R_{k}(z),R_{j}(z))`$.

###### Proposition 2 (Matched-seed variance).

Assume independent seed pairs across $`m`$ and finite second moments.
Then

```math
\displaystyle\operatorname{Var}(\widehat{\Delta}_{kj}^{\rm match}) \displaystyle=\frac{\sigma_{k}^{2}+\sigma_{j}^{2}-2\sigma_{kj}}{M}, \tag{15}
```

```math
\displaystyle\operatorname{Var}(\widehat{\Delta}_{kj}^{\rm ind}) \displaystyle=\frac{\sigma_{k}^{2}+\sigma_{j}^{2}}{M}. \tag{16}
```

Hence matched seeds reduce variance by $`2\sigma_{kj}/M`$ whenever
$`\sigma_{kj}\geq 0`$.

###### Proof.

Apply the variance identity to $`R_{k}(z)-R_{j}(z)`$ and use
independence across $`m`$. The independent estimator has zero
cross-covariance by construction. ∎

The prompt-policy estimator in Eq. 4 averages these seed-wise contrasts
over the other $`K-1`$ candidates, so common randomness enters the
actual training estimator rather than only an auxiliary analysis.

### A.4 Reward-only transition geometry

For a Gaussian transition $`\pi(a|\mu)=\mathcal{N}(\mu,\Sigma)`$ with
covariance independent of $`\mu`$,

```math
\nabla_{\mu}\log\pi(a|\mu)=\Sigma^{-1}(a-\mu),\qquad F=\mathbb{E}[\nabla_{\mu}\log\pi\nabla_{\mu}\log\pi^{\top}]=\Sigma^{-1}. \tag{17}
```

If an unclipped reward surrogate has local coefficient $`\widehat{A}`$,
then $`q^{R}=\widehat{A}\Sigma^{-1}(a-\mu)`$ and
$`g^{R}=F^{-1}q^{R}=\widehat{A}(a-\mu)`$. This provides intuition for
the Fisher coordinate used in the main text. The implementation
differentiates the actual reward branch of Flow-GRPO after its ratio and
clipping operations. Reference/KL terms stay inside $`J_{\rm reg}`$ and
never enter $`q^{R}`$, preventing reward credit from being contaminated
by a return-to-reference direction.

#### Stochastic sample versus expected reward direction.

For Flow-GRPO, the single-transition vector above is a stochastic
score-function sample, so its high-dimensional direction need not itself
align strongly with a low-rank prompt-response span. Let $`P_{t}`$ and
$`\alpha_{t}`$ be constructed from the cached pre-action state and
detached prompt responses, hence fixed with respect to the current
transition draw conditional on that state. Linearity gives

```math
\mathbb{E}[(I-\alpha_{t}P_{t})g_{t}^{R}\mid s_{t}]=(I-\alpha_{t}P_{t})\,\mathbb{E}[g_{t}^{R}\mid s_{t}]. \tag{18}
```

Thus routing is interpreted as a regularizer on the conditional expected
reward update. We do not use the projection energy of one stochastic
transition sample as a semantic-overlap statistic; the mechanism audits
below instead use realized neural displacements after the optimizer
step.

### A.5 Directional prompt-policy evidence

Fix a behavior state $`s_{t}`$, executed action $`p_{k}`$, and
differentiable local reward surrogate $`J_{\rm reward,t}(\mu)`$. Let
$`\mu_{k}=\mu(s_{t},p_{k})`$,
$`q_{t}^{R}=\nabla_{\mu}J_{\rm reward,t}(\mu_{k})`$, and
$`d=\mu(s_{t},p_{j})-\mu_{k}`$. Taylor’s theorem gives

```math
J_{\rm reward,t}(\mu_{k}+\varepsilon d)=J_{\rm reward,t}(\mu_{k})+\varepsilon(q_{t}^{R})^{\top}d+\frac{\varepsilon^{2}}{2}d^{\top}H(\xi)d. \tag{19}
```

Therefore $`c=d^{\top}q_{t}^{R}`$ is exactly the directional derivative
at $`\varepsilon=0`$. It characterizes the local reward change along an
observed same-state conditioning response; fresh-seed evaluation
measures how far that local signal transfers to full trajectories. The
held-out-response and fresh-seed experiments in Appendix F test whether
this local quantity carries predictive information beyond the
construction states.

### A.6 Minimum-overlap routing in Fisher coordinates

For positive-definite $`F`$, define
$`\langle a,b\rangle_{F}=a^{\top}Fb`$ and $`\|a\|_{F}^{2}=a^{\top}Fa`$.
Let $`P`$ be the $`F`$-orthogonal projector onto the response span,

```math
P=D(D^{\top}FD)^{\dagger}D^{\top}F. \tag{20}
```

Then $`P^{2}=P`$ and $`P^{\top}F=FP`$.

###### Proposition 3 (Minimum-overlap solution).

For $`\eta\geq 0`$, the local problem

```math
\min_{\delta}\ \frac{1}{2}\|\delta-g^{R}\|_{F}^{2}+\frac{\eta}{2}\|P\delta\|_{F}^{2} \tag{21}
```

has solution

```math
\delta^{*}=(I-\alpha P)g^{R},\qquad\alpha=\frac{\eta}{1+\eta}, \tag{22}
```

and

```math
(q^{R})^{\top}\delta^{*}=\|(I-P)g^{R}\|_{F}^{2}+(1-\alpha)\|Pg^{R}\|_{F}^{2}\geq(1-\alpha)\|g^{R}\|_{F}^{2}\geq 0. \tag{23}
```

###### Proof.

The first-order condition is
$`F(\delta-g^{R})+\eta P^{\top}FP\delta=0`$. Using $`P^{\top}F=FP`$ and
$`P^{2}=P`$ yields $`(I+\eta P)\delta=g^{R}`$. Its inverse is
$`I-\eta(1+\eta)^{-1}P`$. The final expression follows from the
$`F`$-orthogonal decomposition $`g^{R}=Pg^{R}+(I-P)g^{R}`$. ∎

The statement is made in an unconstrained transition-mean coordinate.
The neural update in Eq. 8 is a pullback through $`J_{\mu,\theta}`$; an
arbitrary mean-space displacement need not be exactly realizable by the
parameterization. We therefore use the proposition to motivate the local
routing rule and test the realized parameter update directly through the
audit in Appendix F.1.

### A.7 Ridge stabilization and contraction

The implemented operator in Eq. 8 uses ridge regularization. Let
$`B=F^{1/2}D`$. In whitened coordinates,

```math
\widetilde{P}_{\lambda}=B(B^{\top}B+\lambda_{t}I)^{-1}B^{\top},\qquad\lambda_{t}=\epsilon_{\rm ridge}\,\frac{\operatorname{tr}(D_{t}^{\top}F_{t}D_{t})}{K-1},\quad\epsilon_{\rm ridge}=10^{-3}. \tag{24}
```

If $`B=U\operatorname{diag}(s_{i})V^{\top}`$, then

```math
\widetilde{P}_{\lambda}=U\operatorname{diag}\!\left(\frac{s_{i}^{2}}{s_{i}^{2}+\lambda_{t}}\right)U^{\top}. \tag{25}
```

Hence $`\widetilde{P}_{\lambda}`$ is symmetric positive semidefinite
with eigenvalues in $`[0,1)`$. For $`0\leq\alpha\leq 1`$ and
$`\widetilde{g}=F^{1/2}g^{R}`$,

```math
(q^{R})^{\top}(I-\alpha P_{\lambda})g^{R}=\widetilde{g}^{\top}(I-\alpha\widetilde{P}_{\lambda})\widetilde{g}\geq(1-\alpha)\|\widetilde{g}\|_{2}^{2}\geq 0. \tag{26}
```

This covers the stabilized local operator used in training without
calling it an exact projector.

### A.8 Reference action and finite response span

The conditioning reference is the executed prompt action $`p_{k}`$
because it is the upstream action that produced $`s_{t}`$. The
intervention of interest is therefore $`p_{k}\rightarrow p_{j}`$. Using
the raw request $`u`$ as the reference mixes candidate-specific
variation with the shared raw-to-enhanced displacement and can overstate
directions common to all enhanced prompts; we keep that choice as an
ablation.

With $`K=4`$, the main response matrix has at most three columns, so the
geometry is a finite, policy-dependent response span. It measures
overlap with alternatives actually proposed by the current prompt
policy. Rank-matched random spans, wrong-query spans, held-out response
prediction, and generator swaps are designed around that finite claim.

## Appendix B Reliability, Trust Regions, and Detachment

For selected states, the prompt policy samples $`B_{\rm train}=6`$
additional intent-preserving textual alternatives $`\widetilde{p}_{b}`$
that are not rendered into videos. The main span is always constructed
from the $`K-1`$ real rollout candidates; these non-decoded probes
require only one same-state generator transition forward and no
reward-model call. For
$`\widetilde{d}_{b}=\mu(s_{t},\widetilde{p}_{b})-\mu(s_{t},p_{k})`$,
define

```math
e_{b,t}=\frac{\|P_{\lambda,t}\widetilde{d}_{b}\|_{F_{t}}^{2}}{\|\widetilde{d}_{b}\|_{F_{t}}^{2}+\epsilon_{\rm num}},\qquad r_{t}=\operatorname{clip}\!\left(\frac{1}{B_{\rm train}}\sum_{b=1}^{B_{\rm train}}e_{b,t},0,1\right),\qquad\alpha_{t}=\alpha_{\max}r_{t}, \tag{27}
```

with $`\epsilon_{\rm num}=10^{-12}`$. The score measures
support/coverage of the sampled response span, not correctness of the
reward direction. The default uses $`\alpha_{\max}=0.75`$.
Evaluation-time mechanism analysis independently samples
$`B_{\rm eval}=12`$ probes on held-out requests; these probes never
participate in the training gate.

For a response $`d`$, the trust operator is

```math
\operatorname{TrustClip}_{F_{t}}(d)=d\,\min\!\left(1,\sqrt{\frac{\tau_{\rm batch}}{d^{\top}F_{t}d+\epsilon_{\rm num}}}\right), \tag{28}
```

where $`\tau_{\rm batch}`$ is the detached 95th percentile of
native-metric response energies $`d^{\top}F_{t}d`$ in the current
training batch. The percentile rule is fixed across all experiments; the
threshold value is batch-adaptive and is not scheduled by timestep or
training iteration. Thus in-region responses are unchanged and only
unusually large conditioning shifts are rescaled. Trust clipping and
reliability gating address different failure modes: clipping prevents a
rare high-norm conditioning response from dominating $`D_{t}`$ or the
directional score, whereas $`r_{t}`$ asks whether the resulting span
generalizes to held-out current-policy responses. We apply three
safeguards before a response enters $`D_{t}`$: (i) prompt-level intent
filtering rejects alternatives that alter the requested event or
introduce unsupported entities; (ii) Eq. 28 limits response magnitude;
and (iii) near-collinear columns are stabilized by the trace-normalized
ridge term rather than explicitly inverted. The percentile rule is
batch-adaptive and is used as a robustifier rather than a separately
tuned reward weight; fixed-$`\alpha`$ and binary-gate controls isolate
the subsequent routing logic.

The behavior generator snapshot $`\theta^{-}`$ is detached when
computing alternative responses. Reward-only local directions,
reliability scores, $`D_{t}`$, $`P_{\lambda,t}`$, $`r_{t}`$, and all
cross-fitted functional credits are treated as stop-gradient targets in
the optimizer step. This prevents the generator from changing the
geometry to obtain easier routing and prevents the prompt policy from
differentiating through generator probes rather than learning from the
resulting credit.

## Appendix D Implementation and Evaluation Protocol

### D.1 Backbone, prompt optimizer, and training pool

The primary generator is the public LTX-2.5 trainable DiT checkpoint
from the official Lightricks/LTX-2.5 release (HaCohen et al., 2026;
Lightricks, 2026), specifically
diffusion_models/ltx-2.5-22b-dev-transformer-bf16.safetensors, with its
native flow parameterization and scheduler. The prompt optimizer is the
instruction-tuned Qwen/Qwen3.5-9B checkpoint (Qwen Team, 2026), used as
a text-generation policy; we do not use the separate Qwen3.5-9B-Base
checkpoint. The main configuration uses full-parameter prompt-policy
updates. HunyuanVideo-1.5 (Wu et al., 2025) provides backbone transfer,
while RVM (Choi et al., 2026) provides an alternative generator reward
updater under the same LTX evaluation harness.

The training pool contains 8,000 T2V requests. We build it from 4,800
programmatically instantiated compositional templates over entities,
attributes, relations, counts, and actions; 2,000 independently
generated motion/interaction requests; and 1,200 manually curated
multi-event requests, then stratify the mixture across object/attribute
binding, spatial relation, motion binding, action binding, interaction,
numeracy, and longer event descriptions. Before training, requests are
deduplicated against every benchmark split by normalized exact match,
5-gram MinHash (Jaccard threshold 0.80), and sentence-embedding cosine
(threshold 0.92); no retained training request crosses any of these
thresholds. Validation prompts are disjoint at the request level and are
never used for benchmark reporting. Prompt candidates are constrained to
preserve the user request, avoid unsupported entities, and remain within
the generator context budget. Candidate validity is checked by a frozen
Qwen/Qwen3.5-9B copy that is separate from the trainable prompt-policy
weights. The checker receives the original request and one candidate and
deterministically returns PASS/FAIL under a fixed rubric: a candidate
fails if it changes required entities, counts, relations, actions, or
event ordering, or introduces unsupported entities; descriptive camera,
lighting, and appearance details are permitted only when they do not
alter those constraints. Greedy decoding is used, and the same checker
and rubric are applied to all prompt-side baselines and to additional
support probes. Rejected candidates are resampled before video
generation. For the theory above, $`\pi_{\phi}`$ denotes the effective
distribution over accepted prompt actions induced by this fixed
filtering rule. The exactness claim concerns the hierarchical reward
decomposition and its score-function baseline identity; the practical
prompt-policy update uses the clipped group-relative surrogate in
Table 5. The same accepted-action sampling rule is used for all
prompt-side methods.

#### Scorer-family independence.

We additionally evaluate the final hierarchical-credit and Full
checkpoints with frozen VisionReward (Xu et al., 2026), which is not
used anywhere in training, calibration, model selection, or the four
headline benchmarks. On 300 held-out matched request/seed pairs,
VisionReward prefers Full on 63.3% of pairs and its normalized scalar
score increases by 0.18 standard deviations on average. This
scorer-family check is consistent with the blinded human preference
study in Appendix L and complements the reward-axis interventions below.

For Flow-GRPO (Liu et al., 2025a), Eq. 4 supplies the hierarchy-aware
trajectory advantage used by the reward branch. We use the fixed LTX
configuration in Table 5: ratio clip $`10^{-3}`$, KL coefficient
$`0.004`$, one inner epoch, and bfloat16 sampling/training; these
settings are held fixed across the generator-side controlled
comparisons. The routed state is sampled uniformly from the valid
stochastic-policy transitions contributing to the reward loss.
Concretely, the cached state stores the current noisy latent together
with the timestep and scheduler scalars needed to evaluate the next
transition; behavior parameters, stochastic draw, and all non-text state
are fixed during alternative-prompt probing. The functional probe
differentiates the post-clipping reward branch with respect to the
transition mean. The reference/KL branch is never routed. Prompt-policy
GRPO uses Eq. 7, with terminal and functional components calibrated
separately by the frozen statistics defined in Section 3.3 before
weighted combination.

#### Instantiation with RVM.

RVM (Choi et al., 2026) uses reward-weighted velocity matching together
with an optional anchor toward a reference velocity field. In the
reported RVM transfer, functional coordination is instantiated in this
backend’s native velocity-output coordinates. At a sampled noised
endpoint state $`(x_{t},t)`$ under executed prompt $`p_{k}`$, write
$`h_{t}=v_{\theta}(x_{t},t,p_{k})`$. We obtain the local reward-driven
direction from the reward-weighted velocity branch,
$`q_{t}^{R}=-\nabla_{h_{t}}\mathcal{L}_{\rm RVM,reward}`$, absorbing the
backend’s scalar timestep weight into $`q_{t}^{R}`$ and using the
Euclidean metric in $`h_{t}`$ coordinates. Alternative-prompt responses
use the identical noised state and time under the detached behavior
snapshot,

```math
d^{\rm RVM}_{j\leftarrow k,t}=v_{\theta^{-}}(x_{t},t,p_{j})-v_{\theta^{-}}(x_{t},t,p_{k}). \tag{30}
```

The same trust clipping, ridge response operator, and held-out support
gate are then applied in velocity-output space. Routing modifies only
the reward-weighted velocity branch; RVM’s anchor/reference branch is
restored unchanged. Thus functional coordination requires a
backend-native local reward direction and same-state conditioning
responses, while the local coordinate and metric are supplied by the
updater rather than assumed to be a stochastic-transition Fisher
parameterization.

#### Instantiation with differentiable reward flow.

We additionally instantiate functional coordination on a Diffusion-DRF
differentiable reward-flow backend (Wang et al., 2026b). Following the
public DRF setup, the critic is frozen Qwen2.5-VL-7B; ten uniformly
sampled video frames are used for VLM feedback, and gradients are
truncated to the final three denoising steps. At a routed state we take
the differentiable reward gradient with respect to the backend
transition output as $`q_{t}^{R}`$ and use its native Euclidean output
metric for $`g_{t}^{R}`$. Alternative-prompt responses are evaluated at
the identical state under the detached generator snapshot. The same
trust clip, trace-normalized ridge operator, and held-out support gate
are then applied before backpropagating the routed reward direction.
Critic weights, frame sampling, the three-step backpropagation window,
gradient checkpointing, and non-reward stabilization terms are held
fixed between hierarchy-only and Full. This experiment tests whether
cross-module routing remains useful when the generator already receives
dense differentiable feedback rather than a scalar policy-gradient
advantage.

#### Backend applicability.

Functional coordination requires two backend-local objects: (i) an
output coordinate in which changing text conditioning at a fixed state
yields a response vector, and (ii) a local improvement covector
$`q_{t}^{R}`$ supplied by the generator updater. Flow-GRPO, RVM, and
differentiable reward flow provide both directly. A preference objective
can be compatible if its implementation exposes a differentiable
state-local pairwise loss, in which case the loss gradient can play the
role of $`q_{t}^{R}`$; a purely trajectory-level preference update with
no local improvement covector is outside the current formulation.
Likewise, closed API-only generators are outside our target setting
because joint generator post-training itself already assumes access to
internal transitions and parameters.

### D.4 Benchmark protocol

VBench (Huang et al., 2024) and VBench-2.0 (Zheng et al., 2025) use
their official aggregate/component definitions. T2V-CompBench (Sun
et al., 2025) uses its official compositional dimensions and reported
aggregate. StoryEval (Wang et al., 2025) follows the same official
event-completion protocol for every generator updater and backbone.
Every checkpoint uses identical resolution, duration, scheduler steps,
negative-prompt convention, and three fixed generation seeds per
benchmark request. The three scores for a request are averaged first;
official benchmark aggregation is then applied to those request-level
means. This preserves matched stochasticity while preventing seed count
from being treated as sample size.

We distinguish three sources of uncertainty: training-run variation,
within-checkpoint generation randomness, and request sampling. The
headline hierarchical-credit-versus-Full comparison uses three
independent training runs; the functional-coordination factorial uses
the same three-run protocol, and other trainable baselines use two
independent runs. Confidence intervals for the headline increment are
computed on paired run-level deltas using a Student-$`t`$ interval;
request-level paired bootstrap intervals are retained as a within-run
sensitivity analysis. Generation seeds are averaged within each
checkpoint and are never treated as independent training replicates. The
same checkpoint and seed definitions are used across all controlled
comparisons.

### D.5 Baseline implementation

All trainable baselines draw from the same 8,000-request training pool
and use the same number of request draws and decoded videos unless a
comparison explicitly targets compute. The frozen official LTX-2.5
prompt enhancer uses the reference LTX-2.5 enhancement path (Lightricks,
2026): google/gemma-4-E2B-it with LTX2_5_T2V_DEFAULT_SYSTEM_PROMPT,
greedy decoding (do_sample=False), no_repeat_ngram_size=5, and at most
600 new tokens. Prompt-only updates Qwen3.5-9B while freezing LTX-2.5;
the Flow-GRPO generator-only row keeps the original request as its text
interface and is included as a single-side reference rather than as the
matched joint-training control. Prompt-A-Video (Ji et al., 2025) and
VPO (Cheng et al., 2025) use their published prompt-side objectives
within the common Qwen3.5-9B prompt optimizer, training pool, LTX-2.5
generator, and evaluation harness. Because their original backbones and
training stacks differ, the reported values are controlled
common-harness results rather than copied published absolute scores.

PromptRL under the common video harness uses the published shared-reward
joint-training principle: the trainable prompt refiner and Flow-GRPO
generator receive the same terminal video reward, with candidate count,
rollout count, reward stack, initialization, and update budget held
fixed. UniGRPO adds group-relative normalization for the text policy and
generator branch within the same joint rollout groups, retaining the
published unified sparse-terminal-reward idea while keeping our LTX-2.5
backbone and reward stack fixed. We use these rows to compare
joint-optimization principles within one harness, not as claims of exact
reproduction of the original image-generation systems (Wang et al.,
2026a; Liu et al., 2026). Matched Global-LOO then trains the same two
modules on the same $`K\times M`$ grid with the global leave-one-out
definition in Appendix A.2; hierarchical credit changes only the credit
hierarchy, and Full adds functional prompt shaping and generator
routing.

CAPE-T2V (Jia et al., 2026) is the closest T2V-specific two-sided system
in our comparison set, but it addresses the prompt–caption distribution
gap through sequential Anchored-PE training followed by DiT adaptation
on a separately constructed 54k video-derived caption set. Its released
LTX pipeline targets LTX-2.3, whereas our mechanism-controlled study
uses online joint reward post-training on LTX-2.5 with no auxiliary
video-caption supervision. We therefore use CAPE-T2V for external
positioning rather than treating its public absolute scores as a
matched-budget ranking.

### F.1 Realized-update audit

For cached states, let
$`\Delta\mu_{t}^{\rm real}=\mu_{\theta^{+}}(s_{t},p_{k})-\mu_{\theta}(s_{t},p_{k})`$.
Hierarchical credit and Full are branched from the same checkpoint with
the same minibatch, optimizer state, cached states, scheduler state, and
random state. We report

```math
\rho_{\rm span}^{\rm real}=\frac{\|P_{\lambda,t}\Delta\mu_{t}^{\rm real}\|_{F_{t}}^{2}}{\|\Delta\mu_{t}^{\rm real}\|_{F_{t}}^{2}+\epsilon}, \tag{31}
```

together with
$`\|\Delta\mu_{\rm Full}^{\rm real}\|_{F}/\|\Delta\mu_{\rm HC}^{\rm real}\|_{F}`$.
The realized comparison is 0.487$`\rightarrow`$0.264 for supported
overlap and 0.931 for the update-norm ratio. These quantities are
measured after the neural optimizer step and avoid interpreting the
energy projection of an individual stochastic transition sample.

## Appendix K Realized Cross-Module Coordination Audit

The response span establishes local conditioning capacity; this audit
asks whether the trained modules subsequently move in the complementary
pattern predicted by functional coordination. For each held-out routed
state, we cache the pre-update behavior generator and estimate the
prompt-policy-induced displacement

```math
\Delta\mu_{\rm P}=\mathbb{E}_{p\sim\pi_{\phi^{+}}}[\mu_{\theta^{-}}(s,p)]-\mathbb{E}_{p\sim\pi_{\phi}}[\mu_{\theta^{-}}(s,p)].
```

We measure its native-metric cosine with the supported direction and the
supported component retained by the realized generator step. The four
variants use identical held-out states and prompt-sampling seeds.
