# PID-Gated Continual Prompt Adaptation Under Noisy Feedback

[Zhentao Xu](https://orcid.org/0009-0000-5827-7879)
Affiliation: Independent Researcher Affiliation: Sunnyvale, CA, USA
Email: [frankxu@umich.edu](mailto:)

###### Abstract

Deploying LLM agents in non-stationary environments requires continually
revising their instructions as the task drifts, yet rewriting after
every reported mistake can turn noisy feedback into persistent errors.
This raises an often-overlooked scheduling question in prompt
adaptation: _when_ should feedback trigger a lasting instruction update,
and at what editor-call cost? Taking inspiration from
proportional-integral-derivative (PID) control, we introduce a family of
lightweight gates that react to the current error, accumulate evidence
over time, and roll back deterioration after an edit. We benchmark these
gates against both simple baselines and advanced prompt optimizers
(TextGrad and GEPA), over a diverse suite of drifting tasks, LLM
parameter sizes, feedback-noise levels, and random seeds. PID-gated
continual prompt adaptation yields a more favorable Pareto trade-off
between task performance (high accuracy, low error) and editor-call
budget than the baselines considered, pointing to control-theoretic
scheduling as a simple, general tool for robust prompt adaptation under
unreliable feedback. We open source our code at
[https://github.com/zhentao-xu/pid-gated-agent-learning](https://github.com/zhentao-xu/pid-gated-agent-learning).

## 1 Introduction

Consider an agent guessing a hidden integer under noisy high/low
feedback: it holds a belief about the plausible range $`[L,U]`$ that
must tighten as evidence accumulates, yet a single mislabeled “higher”
reply can widen the wrong bound and lock in errors for many rounds. This
is the general dilemma of adapting instructions in a changing
environment: revising after every reported mistake can turn a useful
rule into a persistent source of errors, while waiting leaves the agent
following an outdated rule. The challenge is deciding when a mistake
warrants a lasting change.

Language models offer several ways to learn from feedback: revising an
answer \[11\], retaining verbal experience across attempts \[18\], or
optimizing prompts through textual feedback \[26\] and candidate search
\[24, 1\]. In each, however, the scheduling decision is fixed: revisions
fire after every attempt or after every observed error subject to a call
ceiling, offering no principled way to withhold an edit when the
feedback itself might be wrong. We instead ask a complementary
scheduling question: _when should feedback trigger a persistent
instruction update?_ This connects prompt adaptation to concept drift
\[7\] and continual learning \[23\].

Proportional-integral-derivative (PID) control \[3\] suggests three
complementary responses: reacting to current error, accumulating
evidence over time, and monitoring deterioration after an edit \[4\]. We
translate this intuition into discrete editing gates and compare them
against online TextGrad and GEPA using one LLM-based editor and
structured predictors. Across our empirical study (Section 5, Table 1,
Figure 2), the PID gates collectively yield a more favorable Pareto
trade-off between task performance and editor-call budget than the
baselines.

## 2 Related Work

#### Self-evolving LLM agents and noisy evaluators.

Methods that improve LLM behavior from natural-language feedback split
by _what_ they update. In the textual loop, Self-Refine \[11\],
Reflexion \[18\], TextGrad \[26\], OPRO \[24\], and GEPA \[1\] revise
prompts, chains-of-thought, or candidate instructions in response to
observed errors, and skill-library agents such as Voyager \[20\] and
SkillWeaver \[28\] accumulate reusable textual skills. In the parameter
loop, STaR \[27\], Self-Rewarding Language Models \[25\], RLHF \[13\],
and Constitutional AI \[5\] convert feedback into weight updates. Our
PID gates target the textual loop, where every edit is a stochastic LLM
rewrite of a persistent artifact, so the scheduling question of _when_
to invoke the editor is especially acute. The feedback signal itself is
unreliable in both regimes: LLM-as-judge suffers position, verbosity,
and self-preference biases \[29, 22, 14\], sycophancy \[17\], and models
cannot reliably self-correct \[8\]. This motivates evidence-gated
editing rather than reacting to every reported error.

#### Control theory for learning and continual adaptation.

Feedback control has long informed machine learning: SGD-momentum as PI
control \[2\], KL-weight scheduling in VAEs as PID regulation \[16\],
constrained RL as a PID-Lagrangian \[19\], and deep-learning optimizers
as continuous-time PID \[6\]. Our work instantiates PID intuition
_outside_ the parameter space of a differentiable model, on
natural-language artifacts driven by a stochastic LLM editor. On the
continual-learning side, catastrophic forgetting \[12\] and the
stability-plasticity dilemma have driven decades of research \[15, 21,
23, 7\]. Classical parameter-space defenses (elastic weight
consolidation \[9\], replay memory \[10\]) have natural prompt-space
analogues in the buffered evidence and rollback rules used here.

## 3 Method: PID-Gated Adaptation

### 3.1 System overview

Figure 1 lays out the five-component closed loop. At each step $`t`$, a
drifting environment presents input $`x_{t}`$ with hidden target
$`y_{t}`$; the predictor emits $`\hat{y}_{t}=f(\theta_{t},x_{t})`$ under
the current prompt $`\theta_{t}`$; a noisy sensor returns an observed
label $`\tilde{y}_{t}`$ that disagrees with $`y_{t}`$ at rate $`\eta`$;
the comparator then produces an observed error $`e_{t}`$ (binary
$`\mathbf{1}[\hat{y}_{t}\neq\tilde{y}_{t}]`$ for categorical tasks, or a
bucketed distance for numeric ones) and, on mismatch, a natural-language
verbalization $`v_{t}`$. A PID controller reads the histories
$`\{e_{\tau},v_{\tau}\}_{\tau\leq t}`$ and decides whether to invoke the
LLM editor $`g`$, which returns
$`\theta_{t+1}=g(\theta_{t},\{v_{\tau}\})`$. Because $`g`$ is a
stochastic actuator with no gradient and $`\theta`$ is a persistent
state whose bad edits compound, the controller’s role is scheduling
(_when_ and on what evidence to call $`g`$) rather than choosing the
prompt itself; the goal is to track $`y_{t}`$ at low editing cost while
remaining robust to corruption in $`\tilde{y}_{t}`$. In the guess-number
game, for instance, $`\theta_{t}`$ encodes the agent’s belief about the
plausible value range as integer bounds $`[L,U]`$; on each turn
($`x_{t}`$), the LLM predictor $`f`$ emits a numeric guess
($`\hat{y}_{t}`$) conditioned on this belief; the environment scores it
against a hidden target ($`y_{t}`$) and returns high/low/correct
feedback with a distance bucket ($`\tilde{y}_{t}`$) that the sensor may
flip; the comparator packages this into $`(e_{t},v_{t})`$, e.g., “guess
was too low, distance 10–19”; when the controller fires, the LLM editor
$`g`$ rewrites $`[L,U]`$, tightening the belief for $`\theta_{t+1}`$.

Figure 1: The regulated textual feedback loop. Signals enter from

### 3.2 PID-inspired Gating

We map each classical PID component onto a discrete editing gate. All
three gates share the same eligibility check (unused call budget, error
evidence since the last reset, no mandatory pause) and invoke the same
LLM editor $`g`$, which receives the current prompt and up to $`K`$
recent observed examples (including correct ones; $`K=8`$ in our
experiments); invalid proposals retain the prompt and every model call
counts against the budget. The gate controls only _when_ $`g`$ is
called, never the content of $`\theta_{t+1}`$.

#### Proportional Gate (P).

The proportional term reacts to the current error: the _Proportional
Gate_ fires whenever the observer detects an error, i.e., $`e_{t}=1`$ in
the binary case or $`e_{t}\geq\tau_{P}`$ when the error is a
non-negative bucketed distance, and eligibility holds. It is the most
responsive schedule while the most vulnerable to isolated corruption in
$`\tilde{y}_{t}`$.

```math
a_{t}=\mathbf{1}[e_{t}\geq\tau_{P}]\,\mathbf{1}[\text{eligible at }t]. \tag{1}
```

#### Integral Gate (I).

The integral term accumulates evidence over time. The _Integral Gate_
maintains a decayed error sum and resets $`A_{t}`$ to zero after any
attempted edit.

```math
A_{t}=\alpha A_{t-1}+e_{t},\qquad a_{t}=\mathbf{1}[A_{t}\geq\tau_{I}]\,\mathbf{1}[\text{eligible at }t]. \tag{2}
```

#### Derivative Gate (D).

The derivative term watches for post-edit deterioration. On firing, the
_Derivative Gate_ snapshots the current prompt, holds the replacement
fixed for $`W`$ observations, and restores the snapshot if the post-edit
error mean rises by at least $`\tau_{D}`$ over the pre-edit mean. Full
update order is in Appendix A.

```math
r_{t}=\mathbf{1}[\bar{e}_{\text{post}}-\bar{e}_{\text{pre}}\geq\tau_{D}]. \tag{3}
```

## 4 Experiments

### 4.1 Tasks

We design three simple but representative tasks to test the
self-evolving agent’s capability across different output types
(categorical and numeric) and drift patterns (sudden reversal, abrupt
state shift, and smooth continuous drift). Together they probe how
quickly the agent detects change, how it accumulates evidence under
noisy feedback, and how gracefully it tracks a target that never stops
moving. Hidden targets, boundaries, and initial rules are
seed-randomized and never shown to the editor, so the study isolates
LLM-mediated policy editing from end-to-end agent reasoning.

- •
  Parity (categorical, sudden rule flips). Inputs are integers 1–10; the
  hidden rule assigns Red/Blue by parity, and the mapping reverses near
  steps 30, 60, and 90. Feedback is a categorical color, corrupted
  independently with probability $`\eta`$. Stresses categorical rule
  detection under discrete reversals.
- •
  Guess number (numeric, abrupt state shifts). The agent guesses a
  hidden integer in $`[1,100]`$ that shifts abruptly and periodically;
  feedback is high/low/correct paired with a distance bucket. Stresses
  numeric adaptation when the target state jumps.
- •
  Track moving target (numeric, smooth continuous drift). The hidden
  integer follows a smooth continuous trajectory (e.g., sinusoidal,
  rectangular, or exponential); our implementation uses a sinusoid
  completing $`\sim\!1.5`$ cycles over 120 steps, with no discrete
  change points. Stresses continuous tracking without visible boundaries
  a controller could anchor to.

### 4.2 Experimental Setup

We evaluate seven policies (No Edit, Fixed Cadence, Proportional (P),
Integral (I), Derivative (D), TextGrad, GEPA) on the three tasks under
three feedback-corruption levels $`\eta\in\{0,0.1,0.2\}`$ with $`T=120`$
steps per trajectory, using the same LLM as both predictor and editor;
results are mean $`\pm`$ std over 5 seeds per cell. No Edit keeps the
initial prompt unchanged for the full trajectory (zero edits, zero
editor calls) and serves as a no-adaptation reference; Fixed Cadence
fires an edit every 15 steps regardless of the error signal and serves
as a data-independent control. Seeded inputs, hidden targets, and
controller settings are paired across corruption levels for matched
comparison. Evaluation metrics are task-specific: classification
accuracy for Parity (higher is better), mean absolute error (MAE) for
Guess number (lower is better), and root-mean-squared error (RMSE) for
Track moving target (lower is better). Model calls are counted at every
generation stage; tokens and deterministic candidate evaluations are
reported separately. TextGrad and GEPA use the default parameters from
their published libraries; interface and accounting details are given in
Appendix B. We report primary results on
[Qwen3-14B](https://huggingface.co/Qwen/Qwen3-14B) (Table 1) and a
scaling-up ablation on
[Qwen3.8-27B](https://huggingface.co/Qwen/Qwen3.8-27B) (Table 2).

Table 1 summarizes the primary comparison on Qwen3-14B at $`\eta=0.1`$:
on every task, a PID gate attains the top quality metric among learned
schedules ($`90.2\%`$ accuracy on Parity for Proportional; $`4.38`$ MAE
on Guess number and $`6.79`$ RMSE on Track for Integral), while all PID
gates issue fewer LLM calls than TextGrad and GEPA. The Integral Gate
occupies a particularly efficient operating point: it matches or beats
TextGrad on every quality metric while using roughly
$`2.7`$–$`3.5\times`$ fewer editor calls, and the Derivative Gate uses
the fewest calls on each task at a modest quality trade-off. The same
cost-quality ordering replicates on a larger LLM (Table 2): on
Qwen3.8-27B, the Integral Gate takes the top Parity accuracy
($`89.7\%`$) and the top Track RMSE ($`6.34`$) at roughly $`3\times`$
fewer calls than TextGrad, with Guess-number MAE within $`0.3`$ of the
best. Absolute quality tracks editor capacity, but the Pareto ordering
between PID gates and the optimizer baselines is preserved. Figure 2
visualizes the 14B snapshot: PID points sit up and to the left of the
TextGrad/GEPA cluster, i.e., comparable or higher quality at
substantially lower cost. This ordering is consistent across all three
noise levels $`\eta\in\{0,0.1,0.2\}`$ (Appendix C, Figure 3): the PID
gates occupy a better cost–quality frontier than TextGrad and GEPA under
both clean and corrupted feedback.

## 6 Conclusion and Future Work

We introduced PID-gated scheduling (Proportional, Integral, Derivative)
that decides _when_ to invoke LLM prompt editors for continual prompt
adaptation under drifting tasks and noisy feedback. Across three
drifting tasks, the PID gates collectively yield a more favorable Pareto
trade-off between task performance and editor-call budget than the
baselines considered, typically matching the strongest optimizer at a
fraction of its call cost (Section 5, Tables 1–2, Figure 2). Future work
includes the _how-much_ question (edit magnitude), for which a natural
extension is an “edit gain” that limits the magnitude of the change from
$`\theta_{t}`$ to $`\theta_{t+1}`$ so one controller regulates both
frequency and magnitude; and online auto-tuning of the gate thresholds,
borrowing established methods from classical control.

## Appendix A Update Order and Gate Characterization

1: prompt $`\theta`$; editor $`g`$; gate
$`G\in\{\mathrm{P},\mathrm{I},\mathrm{D}\}`$; decay $`\alpha`$;
thresholds $`\tau_{P},\tau_{I},\tau_{D}`$ ($`\tau_{I}`$ is shared by I
and D); guard window $`W`$; history size $`K`$; call cap $`B`$; horizon
$`T`$

2: $`A\leftarrow 0`$; $`\mathcal{H}\leftarrow\varnothing`$;
$`\mathcal{R}\leftarrow\varnothing`$ (recent-error queue, length $`W`$);
pending $`\leftarrow\varnothing`$ (holds
$`(\theta_{\mathrm{snap}},\overline{e}_{\mathrm{pre}},\text{post-window})`$
for D); cooldown$`\leftarrow 0`$; calls$`\leftarrow 0`$;
evidence$`\leftarrow`$False $`\triangleright`$ True once any error has
been seen since the last reset

3: for $`t=0,\ldots,T-1`$ do

4:   Predict $`\hat{y}_{t}=f(\theta,x_{t})`$; observe $`\tilde{y}_{t}`$;
set $`e_{t}=\mathbf{1}[\hat{y}_{t}\neq\tilde{y}_{t}]`$ (categorical) or
a bucketed distance (numeric)

5:   Append $`(x_{t},\hat{y}_{t},\tilde{y}_{t},e_{t})`$ to
$`\mathcal{H}`$; retain the latest $`K`$ entries

6:   $`\text{restored}\leftarrow\textsc{False}`$ $`\triangleright`$ set
if a D-gate rollback just happened; skips accumulator update

7:   if $`G=\mathrm{D}`$ and pending $`\neq\varnothing`$
then$`\triangleright`$ regression check

8:    Append $`e_{t}`$ to pending’s post-edit window

9:    if $`W`$ post-edit observations collected then

10:      if
$`\overline{e}_{\mathrm{post}}\geq\overline{e}_{\mathrm{pre}}+\tau_{D}`$
then

11:       Restore $`\theta\leftarrow\theta_{\mathrm{snap}}`$;
$`A\leftarrow 0`$; evidence$`\leftarrow`$False;
$`\text{restored}\leftarrow\textsc{True}`$

12:      end if

13:      pending$`\leftarrow\varnothing`$

14:    end if

15:   end if

16:   Append $`e_{t}`$ to $`\mathcal{R}`$ (drop oldest if
$`|\mathcal{R}|>W`$)

17:   if not restored then

18:    $`A\leftarrow\alpha A+e_{t}`$; if $`e_{t}\geq 1`$ then
evidence$`\leftarrow`$True

19:   end if

20:   if cooldown $`>0`$ then

21:    cooldown$`\leftarrow`$cooldown$`-1`$; continue

22:   end if

23:
  fire$`\leftarrow\bigl(G{=}\mathrm{P}\wedge e_{t}\geq\tau_{P}\bigr)\vee\bigl(G\in\{\mathrm{I},\mathrm{D}\}\wedge A\geq\tau_{I}\bigr)`$

24:   if fire and evidence and calls $`<B`$ then

25:    if $`G=\mathrm{D}`$ and $`|\mathcal{R}|=W`$ then

26:
     pending$`\leftarrow\bigl(\theta,\ \overline{e}_{\mathrm{pre}}=\tfrac{1}{W}\sum\mathcal{R},\ \text{empty post-window}\bigr)`$

27:    end if

28:    $`\theta\leftarrow g(\theta,\mathcal{H})`$;
calls$`\leftarrow`$calls$`+1`$

29:    $`A\leftarrow 0`$; evidence$`\leftarrow`$False; if
$`G=\mathrm{D}`$ then cooldown$`\leftarrow W`$

30:   end if

31: end for

Algorithm 1 PID-inspired gate editing (Proportional, Integral,
Derivative). One pass over $`T`$ steps; the gate $`G`$ selects the
trigger condition and whether the post-edit regression check is armed.
The Derivative gate reuses the Integral trigger ($`A\geq\tau_{I}`$) and
adds a pre/post error-mean regression check that can roll back the edit.

Default settings (shared across gates): $`\alpha=0.9`$, $`W=5`$,
$`K=8`$, $`B=180`$. Gate-specific: $`\tau_{I}=1.5`$ (used by I and D),
$`\tau_{D}=0.15`$ (D only), $`\tau_{P}=1`$ (P only, binary-error case).

## Appendix B Published Optimizers as Online Baselines

TextGrad \[26\] and GEPA \[1\] use their pinned official packages, with
inference routed exclusively through the common LLM-based editor. Both
trigger after two observed errors since the previous invocation and
require at least three remaining calls. Each restarts from the currently
deployed prompt and receives at most eight past/current observations.
Neither accesses clean evaluation metrics or future observations. These
adaptations compare online use of the algorithms with measured resource
costs, rather than reproducing their original task suites or claiming
that all implementations are equivalent.

#### TextGrad.

Version 0.1.8 uses an explicit engine for each of three stages: TextLoss
critiques the current structured prompt against the task specification
and observed feedback; backward produces textual gradient feedback;
TextualGradientDescent proposes an updated variable. Gradient memory is
zero. Loss and backward stages request concise critiques of at most 100
words; the update retains the package’s required variable tags.
Deterministic prediction is outside the autograd graph: the optimized
variable is the structured policy prompt itself. Missing tags or an
invalid structured proposal retain the deployed prompt and consume all
three calls.

#### GEPA.

Version 0.1.4 starts a fresh search population from the deployed prompt,
uses instance-level Pareto parent selection, and permits at most three
model reflections per invocation. Accepted proposals must strictly
improve on a common training minibatch before full validation.
Alternating history entries form disjoint training and validation
subsets when at least four observations are available, with the newest
in training; smaller histories reuse the same observations in both.
Minibatches contain at most three training examples. Optional merging is
disabled, and perfect-score minibatches may skip reflection. Validation
is past noisy evidence, not an independent estimate of future task
performance.

The common client counts all reflection calls. Candidate-example
evaluations include rejected and malformed candidates and are logged
separately. A nominal metric-evaluation limit provides an additional
termination condition for skipped reflections; the official search
checks it between iterations, so realized evaluations can exceed that
nominal limit. Every proposal, selected parent, fitness evaluation,
validation score, and final selection is retained. An invocation
containing an invalid proposal may still deploy a different valid
candidate; invalid-proposal count is therefore distinct from final
prompt changes. Unexpected model or library failures halt the run rather
than being relabeled successful no-ops.

## Appendix C Robustness Across Feedback-Noise Levels

Figure 3 extends the primary comparison (Figure 2) by plotting all three
noise levels $`\eta\in\{0,0.1,0.2\}`$ together, with marker face shade
encoding noise (hollow $`\to`$ half-filled $`\to`$ solid). Across every
corruption level and on every task, the PID gates sit on a better
cost–quality frontier than TextGrad and GEPA: they reach comparable or
higher quality at a substantially lower editor-call cost. The Integral
Gate, in particular, holds its operating point across the whole noise
range, matching or beating the strongest baseline at a fraction of its
call count. This replicates the Pareto conclusion of Section 5 and shows
that the ordering is not an artifact of the single noise level
highlighted in the main text.
