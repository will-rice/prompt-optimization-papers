# EMO: A Closed-Loop Test-Time Computation Framework via Alternating Context Optimization and Logits Steering

###### Abstract

Test-time context optimization adapts frozen language models through
textual feedback, but ordinary conditional generation provides no
separate control over how strongly to reinforce the distributional
changes induced by a context update. We introduce EMO, a training-free
framework inspired by expectation–maximization that alternates
question-specific context estimation (E-step) and distribution
refinement (M-step). The E-step revises a system context using textual
gradients from an external judge without access to gold answers. The
M-step contrasts next-token distributions under the revised and
preceding contexts at a shared prefix to construct a
confidence-constrained logit correction. Feedback on the steered
response drives the next context update, coupling adaptation across
rounds with intervention within each generation. Theoretically, we
connect parameter and context optimization through a shared target-KL
objective and additive logit representations, and derive the steering
vector from context-induced logit displacements. Under
context-improvement and faithful-steering assumptions, we prove a
one-round decrease in target KL divergence for sufficiently small
steering strength. Under greedy decoding across eight models and six
benchmarks, EMO improves the zero-shot baseline in 39 of 40 aggregated
settings, averaging $`+9.10`$ percentage points. With the same judge, it
adds $`+1.53`$ points on average over context-only optimization
(TextGrad), with consistent MMLU-Pro gains across models. At matched
target-model generation budgets, accuracy advantages over best-of-$`N`$
are strongest on Llama, while oracle coverage is higher in most
comparisons despite fewer candidates. The coverage–selection gap
motivates allocating test-time computation jointly to candidate
generation and verification.

## 1 Introduction

Parameter updates and context refinement provide complementary routes to
adapting a large language model (LLM). Parameter optimization, including
fine-tuning, reinforcement learning, and preference alignment,
incorporates task data or feedback into model weights (Hu et al., 2021;
Shao et al., 2024; Rafailov et al., 2023). For an individual test
question, context optimization adapts a frozen model by using feedback
to revise its textual instructions before generating a new
response (Yuksekgonul et al., 2024; Zhang et al., 2026; Madaan et al.,
2023). We study how to translate such question-specific feedback into
explicit control of the decoding process.

A revised context already changes the next-token distribution at every
prefix. Ordinary conditional generation, however, provides no separate
control over the strength or token support of those changes. Comparing
the distributions before and after a context update reveals how the
revision changes token preferences at the current prefix. This motivates
our central question: _how can feedback-driven context updates supply a
controllable intervention on the generation they guide?_

We propose EMO, which alternates context optimization and distribution
refinement for each question. The _E-step_ revises a system context
using textual gradients (Yuksekgonul et al., 2024) from an external
judge without access to gold answers. The _M-step_ compares the revised
and preceding contexts at a shared generated prefix, constructs a
steering vector from their distribution contrast, and applies a
confidence-constrained correction to the revised context’s logits. The
resulting response is evaluated to provide feedback for the next E-step.
Each context update thus determines a decoding intervention whose output
informs the next update, closing the loop while model weights remain
fixed.

A logit-space view explains the relationship between the two steps.
Parameter and context updates both admit additive logit representations,
while EMO uses the context-induced displacement to construct its
additional steering correction. Context edits induce finite logit
changes through a frozen model, whereas parameter updates also alter the
model’s response to subsequent contexts and prefixes. This
representation links adaptation to decoding, while our one-round
analysis identifies sufficient conditions for improvement toward a
target distribution.

Across eight models and six benchmarks, EMO improves over Base in 39 of
40 aggregated settings, averaging $`+9.10`$ percentage points, with
benefits spanning model scale, generation, family, and thinking mode.
With the judge and candidate count held fixed, adding the M-step yields
$`+1.53`$ points on average over context-only optimization and improves
MMLU-Pro for all eight models. At matched target-model generation
budgets, EMO also achieves higher oracle coverage than independent
sampling in seven of nine settings despite producing fewer candidates,
indicating benefits in candidate generation alongside improvements in
selected accuracy.

Our contributions are summarized as follows:

- •
  EMO: coupled context optimization and steering. A training-free
  framework that turns successive context updates into prefix-dependent
  decoding interventions and uses the steered responses to drive further
  context adaptation.
- •
  Logit-space interpretation and conditional analysis. We relate the
  context-induced logit displacement to the implemented steering vector
  (Eq. 5) and establish one-round target-KL improvement under
  context-improvement and faithful-steering assumptions (Theorem 1).
- •
  Empirical evidence across four model axes. Broad improvements over
  Base and additional gains over context-only optimization, supported by
  component ablations, decoding comparisons, and generation-budget
  analyses that distinguish candidate coverage from answer selection.

## 2 Related Work

Parameter optimization. Parameter adaptation includes supervised
fine-tuning and its parameter-efficient variants (Hu et al., 2021),
reinforcement learning (Schulman et al., 2017; Shao et al., 2024; Guo et
al., 2025; Yu et al., 2025), preference optimization (Rafailov et al.,
2023), and distillation (Snell et al., 2022; Agarwal et al., 2024).
These approaches incorporate training signals into model weights. EMO
operates on a frozen checkpoint and uses feedback on the current
question to adapt its context and decoding, making it complementary to
parameter adaptation.

Context optimization. A second line adapts textual inputs while keeping
model weights frozen, including textual gradients and prompt
optimization (Yuksekgonul et al., 2024; Yang et al., 2024),
self-correction (Madaan et al., 2023; Shinn et al., 2023; Gou et al.,
2024), and agentic context engineering that evolves skills and
memories (Zhang et al., 2026; Ye et al., 2026). These approaches use
textual revisions to influence subsequent generation. EMO’s E-step
builds on this idea; its M-step additionally uses the distributional
change between successive contexts to control decoding at each prefix.
The steered response then becomes the basis for further textual
feedback.

Inference-time intervention. A third family controls generation at
inference time by modifying internal activations (Turner et al., 2023)
or contrasting next-token distributions during decoding (Li et al.,
2023). Closely related task-aware approaches include CoS (He et al.,
2025), SDA (Xia and Deng, 2026), SVDecode (Hu et al., 2026), and
JitRL (Li et al., 2026). EMO connects decoding-time intervention to
per-question context optimization: each response informs the next
context update, whose distribution contrast supplies the steering signal
at every subsequent prefix. The logit-space view in Section 3.1
formalizes how these context updates determine prefix-dependent decoding
interventions. Test-time compute can also be spent on parallel sampling,
including self-consistency (Wang et al., 2022) and best-of-$`N`$ with a
verifier or judge (Cobbe et al., 2021; Brown et al., 2024), or
sequential refinement (Snell et al., 2024). Our experiments compare EMO
with context-only refinement and, separately, with SC/BoN at matched
target-model generation budgets (Section 4.4).

## 3 Method

EMO couples context refinement and decoding interventions through
feedback on generated responses. Context updates induce
question-specific changes in token preferences; steering converts these
changes into controlled logit corrections at each decoding prefix. We
first relate the two updates in logit space, then describe the algorithm
and the conditions for target alignment.

### 3.1 A Unified View: Optimization as Logits Steering

Let $`\xi=(\bm{\theta},C)`$ denote model parameters and system context
jointly, and let $`\mathcal{V}`$ be the token vocabulary. The response
distribution is
$`P_{\xi}(y\mid q)=\prod_{t}P_{\xi}(y_{t}\mid q,y_{<t})`$, where $`q`$
is a question and $`y_{t},y_{<t}`$ denote token $`t`$ and its prefix in
response $`y`$. Numerical vectors use bold lowercase and matrices bold
uppercase; scalars are italic. Distributions retain uppercase symbols,
interpreted as probability columns in matrix operations.

##### A shared alignment objective.

SFT minimizes reference-response negative log-likelihood (NLL) over
$`\bm{\theta}`$ with $`C`$ fixed. TextGrad (Yuksekgonul et al., 2024)
instead refines text from loss feedback with $`\bm{\theta}`$ frozen;
when instantiated with reference NLL, it targets the same objective
through $`C`$.

###### Lemma 1 (Unified Objective: NLL–KL Equivalence).

With one reference response per question,

```math
\mathcal{L}(\xi)=\mathbb{E}_{(q,y^{\star})\sim\mathcal{D}}[-\log P_{\xi}(y^{\star}\mid q)]=\mathbb{E}_{q\sim\mathcal{D}}\mathrm{KL}\!\left(\hat{P}_{\mathrm{target}}(\cdot\mid q)\,\|\,P_{\xi}(\cdot\mid q)\right), \tag{1}
```

where $`\mathcal{D}`$ is the empirical distribution of
question–reference pairs, $`y^{\star}=y^{\star}(q)`$ is the reference,
$`\mathcal{L}`$ is the mean NLL, and
$`\hat{P}_{\mathrm{target}}(\cdot\mid q)=\delta_{y^{\star}(q)}`$ is a
point mass at that reference.

###### Proof.

The target assigns probability one to $`y^{\star}(q)`$, so its KL is
$`\log[1/P_{\xi}(y^{\star}\mid q)]`$. Averaging over questions gives
Eq. 1. For a general fixed target, cross-entropy differs from target KL
only by the target entropy (Appendix A.1). ∎

This identity provides a common objective, not identical feasible
distributions or update directions for parameter and context
optimization. At test time, judge feedback provides a surrogate signal
for this unavailable reference-based objective; textual gradients encode
natural-language criticism rather than derivatives of strings.

##### A shared execution form.

Consider a shared history $`h=(q,y_{<t})`$, with finite logits
$`\mathbf{z}`$, model conditional $`P=\mathrm{Softmax}(\mathbf{z})`$,
and target conditional $`Q=\hat{P}_{\mathrm{target}}^{t}(\cdot\mid h)`$
at a target-supported prefix.

###### Lemma 2 (Unified Steering Form).

Any parameter or context update $`\xi\to\xi^{+}`$ induces an exact
additive correction
$`\Delta\mathbf{z}=\mathbf{z}_{\xi^{+}}-\mathbf{z}_{\xi}`$, yielding
$`\mathrm{Softmax}(\mathbf{z}_{\xi}+\Delta\mathbf{z})`$ at each history.
In unrestricted logit space, gradient descent on the conditional target
KL gives

```math
\hat{\mathbf{z}}=\mathbf{z}+\eta\bm{\delta}^{*},\qquad\bm{\delta}^{*}=\mathbf{J}_{P}\frac{Q}{P}=Q-P,\qquad\mathbf{J}_{P}=\mathrm{diag}(P)-PP^{\top}, \tag{2}
```

where $`\hat{\mathbf{z}}`$ contains updated logits, $`\eta>0`$ is an
analysis step size, $`\bm{\delta}^{*}`$ is the ideal descent direction,
and $`\mathbf{J}_{P}`$ is the softmax Jacobian. Division is
componentwise; $`\mathrm{diag}(P)`$ is the diagonal probability matrix.
If $`Q\neq P`$, every sufficiently small $`\eta>0`$ decreases the
conditional KL.

###### Proof.

The additive realization follows by substituting the logit difference.
Since $`\nabla_{P}\mathrm{KL}(Q\|P)=-Q/P`$ and $`\mathbf{J}_{P}`$ is
symmetric, the chain rule gives
$`-\nabla_{\mathbf{z}}\mathrm{KL}(Q\|P)=\mathbf{J}_{P}(Q/P)=Q-P`$. The
directional derivative along $`\bm{\delta}^{*}`$ is therefore
$`-\|Q-P\|_{2}^{2}<0`$ when $`Q\neq P`$; differentiability gives the
stated local decrease. ∎

Parameter gradients propagate through the model Jacobian, whereas
textual rewrites induce finite logit differences; neither generally
follows the direct-logit direction $`Q-P`$ (Appendix A.2). Since $`Q`$
is unavailable, EMO constructs a computable direction from the
context-induced distribution contrast.

### 3.2 EMO: Alternating Context Optimization and Logits Steering

EMO alternates context estimation (E-step) and distribution refinement
(M-step) per question. The EM analogy is structural: textual feedback is
not a posterior expectation, and steering does not maximize a specified
common likelihood.

#### 3.2.1 E-Step: Context Optimization via Textual Gradients

For each question, initialize $`C_{0}`$ to the shared system context and
generate $`R_{0}=\mathrm{Generate}(\bm{\theta},C_{0},q)`$ using ordinary
target-model decoding. Judge feedback guides the context update:

```math
C_{k+1}=\mathrm{Opt}\!\left(C_{k},g_{C}^{(k)}\right),\qquad g_{C}^{(k)}=\mathrm{Backward}(C_{k},q,R_{k},F_{k}), \tag{3}
```

where $`k`$ indexes rounds, $`R_{k}`$ is the current response, and
$`F_{k}`$ is feedback from a judge without gold-answer access.
$`\mathrm{Backward}`$ translates the judge’s criticism of $`R_{k}`$ into
suggested changes to $`C_{k}`$, producing the textual gradient
$`g_{C}^{(k)}`$; $`\mathrm{Opt}`$ applies this guidance to construct
$`C_{k+1}`$. Subsequent rounds use the preceding M-step’s steered
response as $`R_{k}`$. Rewrites update the domain role, reasoning
strategies, pitfalls, and output requirements while keeping the question
fixed. Feedback and rewrite prompts forbid final-answer disclosure; a
pattern filter rejects suspected answer-bearing rewrites by setting
$`C_{k+1}=C_{k}`$. Target-KL improvement is an analysis assumption
(Section 3.3), not guaranteed by the textual update.

#### 3.2.2 M-Step: Distribution Contrast as Logits Steering

At each decoding step, evaluate
$`P_{\mathrm{opt}}=P_{\bm{\theta}}(\cdot\mid C_{k+1},q,y_{<t})`$ and
$`P_{\mathrm{base}}=P_{\bm{\theta}}(\cdot\mid C_{k},q,y_{<t})`$ on the
same generated prefix. Here “base” denotes the preceding context
$`C_{k}`$, not the initial context $`C_{0}`$; round, question, and
prefix indices are suppressed. Both streams consume the newly generated
prefix rather than their own independently sampled responses, isolating
the effect of the context revision. Following SVDecode (Hu et al.,
2026), the contrast yields:

```math
\bm{\delta}_{t}=\mathbf{J}_{P_{\mathrm{opt}}}\log\frac{P_{\mathrm{opt}}}{P_{\mathrm{base}}}=\nabla_{\mathbf{z}_{t}^{\mathrm{opt}}}\mathrm{KL}(P_{\mathrm{opt}}\|P_{\mathrm{base}}), \tag{4}
```

where $`\bm{\delta}_{t}`$ is the raw steering vector,
$`\mathbf{z}_{t}^{\mathrm{opt}}`$ contains logits under $`C_{k+1}`$, and
$`\mathbf{J}_{P_{\mathrm{opt}}}`$ is the softmax Jacobian;
differentiation holds $`P_{\mathrm{base}}`$ fixed. Logarithms and ratios
act componentwise. For nonzero $`\bm{\delta}_{t}`$, an unmasked positive
step locally increases divergence from the preceding-context
distribution; this alone does not ensure target alignment.

##### From context updates to steering.

At a shared prefix, context updates give:

```math
\Delta\mathbf{z}_{t}=\mathbf{z}_{t}^{\mathrm{opt}}-\mathbf{z}_{t}^{\mathrm{base}},\qquad\bm{\delta}_{t}=\mathbf{J}_{P_{\mathrm{opt}}}\Delta\mathbf{z}_{t}, \tag{5}
```

where $`\mathbf{z}_{t}^{\mathrm{base}}`$ contains logits under $`C_{k}`$
and $`\Delta\mathbf{z}_{t}`$ is the context-induced displacement. This
identity follows because $`\log(P_{\mathrm{opt}}/P_{\mathrm{base}})`$
differs from $`\Delta\mathbf{z}_{t}`$ only by a constant across tokens,
which the softmax Jacobian annihilates. The Jacobian centers and
probability-weights the displacement from Lemma 2. Writing
$`r_{t}(v)=\log P_{\mathrm{opt}}(v)-\log P_{\mathrm{base}}(v)`$ gives
the implementation directly:

```math
\delta_{t}(v)=P_{\mathrm{opt}}(v)\left[r_{t}(v)-\sum_{w\in\mathcal{V}}P_{\mathrm{opt}}(w)r_{t}(w)\right], \tag{6}
```

where $`v,w\in\mathcal{V}`$ index tokens and $`r_{t}`$ is the context
log-ratio. A positive correction requires an above-average log-ratio,
not merely $`P_{\mathrm{opt}}(v)>P_{\mathrm{base}}(v)`$. Using
log-softmax values, this expression costs $`O(|\mathcal{V}|)`$ per
prefix without a dense Jacobian or parameter backpropagation
(Appendix A.3).

##### Confidence-constrained decoding.

To restrict the contrast signal to tokens supported by the optimized
context, set
$`\mathbb{I}_{t}(v)=\mathbf{1}\{P_{\mathrm{opt}}(v)\geq\alpha\max_{v^{\prime}}P_{\mathrm{opt}}(v^{\prime})\}`$
and apply

```math
\hat{\delta}_{t}(v)=\mathbb{I}_{t}(v)\delta_{t}(v)+(1-\mathbb{I}_{t}(v))\lambda,\qquad\alpha\in(0,1],\quad\lambda=-100, \tag{7}
```

where $`v,v^{\prime}`$ are tokens, $`\mathbb{I}_{t}`$ indicates whether
a token passes the relative threshold $`\alpha`$, $`\lambda`$ penalizes
exclusion, and $`\delta_{t}(v),\hat{\delta}_{t}(v)`$ are entries of
$`\bm{\delta}_{t},\hat{\bm{\delta}}_{t}`$. Increasing $`\alpha`$ can
only shrink the retained set, which always includes a maximizer;
excluded tokens receive finite penalties. The constrained vector then
corrects the logits used for greedy decoding or sampling:

```math
\tilde{\mathbf{z}}_{t}=\mathbf{z}_{t}^{\mathrm{opt}}+\mu\hat{\bm{\delta}}_{t},\qquad y_{t}=\mathrm{Decode}\!\left(\mathrm{Softmax}(\tilde{\mathbf{z}}_{t})\right), \tag{8}
```

where $`\tilde{\mathbf{z}}_{t}`$ contains corrected logits,
$`\mu\geq 0`$ scales the constrained vector, and $`\mathrm{Decode}`$
selects $`y_{t}`$ greedily or by sampling. The defaults are
$`(\mu,\alpha)=(2.4,0.1)`$; excluded tokens receive the additive
correction $`\mu\lambda`$. At $`\mu=0`$, the method reduces to
context-only optimization. Both contrast and masking are recomputed at
each prefix, before sampling temperature or truncation is applied.

##### Closed-loop execution.

Algorithm 1 performs $`K`$ optimization rounds, generating at most $`T`$
tokens per response and stopping at the end-of-sequence (EOS) token.
Feedback always concerns the most recent response; the highest-scoring
candidate is used only for final selection. It returns the earliest
response maximizing the stored score $`a_{i}=\mathcal{J}(q,R_{i})`$,
where $`\mathcal{J}`$ is the judge and $`i\in\{0,\ldots,K\}`$ indexes
candidates. Two context-conditioned streams maintain separate key–value
caches while sharing the generated prefix. Thus $`K=4`$ yields five
candidates and nine target-model generation equivalents; this excludes
judge/rewrite costs and response-length differences and does not imply
equal latency.

### 3.3 Theoretical Analysis

Fix a question, the contexts $`C_{k},C_{k+1}`$, a finite vocabulary, and
a maximum horizon $`T`$. Let $`Q`$ now denote a fixed target _sequence_
distribution, $`Q_{t}(\cdot\mid h)`$ its conditionals, and
$`\widetilde{P}_{\mu}`$ the sequence distribution induced by Eq. 8
before temperature scaling or truncation. Assume finite logits at
nonterminal histories; after EOS, use shared deterministic padding with
zero steering.

###### Assumption 1 (TextGrad Oracle).

The context update improves the target objective:
$`\mathrm{KL}(Q\|P_{\bm{\theta},C_{k+1}})<\mathrm{KL}(Q\|P_{\bm{\theta},C_{k}})`$.

###### Assumption 2 (Faithful Constrained Steering).

For fixed $`\alpha`$, the actual masked direction has positive
target-weighted alignment:

```math
\Gamma=\sum_{t=1}^{T}\mathbb{E}_{Y_{<t}\sim Q}\left\langle Q_{t}(\cdot\mid h)-P_{\mathrm{opt}}(\cdot\mid h),\hat{\bm{\delta}}_{t}(h)\right\rangle>0. \tag{9}
```

Here histories follow $`Q`$, and $`\Gamma`$ aggregates alignment across
positions; individual histories need not all improve.

###### Theorem 1 (Conditional One-Round Improvement).

Under the stated finite-horizon setting and Assumptions 1–2, every
sufficiently small $`\mu>0`$ gives
$`\mathrm{KL}(Q\|\widetilde{P}_{\mu})<\mathrm{KL}(Q\|P_{\bm{\theta},C_{k+1}})<\mathrm{KL}(Q\|P_{\bm{\theta},C_{k}})`$.

###### Proof.

At a fixed history, $`\hat{\bm{\delta}}_{t}(h)`$ does not depend on
$`\mu`$. The KL chain rule and the conditional gradient in Lemma 2 give

```math
\left.\frac{d}{d\mu}\mathrm{KL}(Q\|\widetilde{P}_{\mu})\right|_{\mu=0}=\sum_{t=1}^{T}\mathbb{E}_{Y_{<t}\sim Q}\left\langle P_{\mathrm{opt}}-Q_{t},\hat{\bm{\delta}}_{t}\right\rangle=-\Gamma<0. \tag{10}
```

Finite vocabulary and horizon justify differentiation under the sums.
Since $`\widetilde{P}_{0}=P_{\bm{\theta},C_{k+1}}`$, differentiability
gives the first inequality; Assumption 1 gives the second. ∎

Appendix A.4 gives an explicit sufficient step-size interval and shows
why context improvement alone does not imply faithful steering. The
guarantee concerns full softmax distributions and unsteered context
baselines; it certifies neither the default $`\mu=2.4`$, monotonic
accuracy, nor improvement between steered rounds. Judge selection
preserves the best stored score, not necessarily correctness
(Appendix A.5).

## 4 Experiments

### 4.1 Experimental Setup

##### Models and benchmarks.

We evaluate eight models spanning 3B–32B parameters and two families:
Qwen3-8B/14B/32B, Qwen2.5-7B, Qwen3.5-9B, DeepSeek-R1-0528-Qwen3-8B,
Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct, and Llama-3.2-3B-Instruct, covering both thinking
and non-thinking models (Appendix B.2). We use six benchmarks covering
multiple-choice and free-form formats: AIME-2025, AIME-2026,
GPQA-Diamond, GSM8K, MATH-500, and MMLU-Pro (560 questions: 40 per
category, sampling seed 42). The main table averages the two AIME
editions into one of five equally weighted task groups (Appendix B.1).

##### Judge and scoring.

The external judge, Qwen3.5-122B-A10B (FP8), assigns scores from 0 to 10
without access to gold answers; feedback prompts forbid final-answer
disclosure. Judge prompting and decoding settings are detailed in
Appendix B.3. Reported accuracy uses task-specific answer extraction and
gold-based correctness rules, independently of judge scores. Under
_judge-select_, the highest-scoring response among the initial
generation and $`K`$ optimization rounds is selected, with ties resolved
in favor of the earliest response. We separately report _oracle
coverage_: the fraction of questions with at least one correct response
in the candidate pool.

##### Baselines and protocol.

We compare Base (zero-shot decoding) and TextGrad (our E-step without
the M-step), adapting TextGrad from task-level to per-question
optimization to match our protocol. TextGrad and EMO share the initial
context, baseline response, judge, and $`K=4`$ optimization rounds, with
judge-select over five candidates. EMO uses fixed defaults $`\mu=2.4`$
and $`\alpha=0.1`$ (Appendix B.5). Main results use greedy target
decoding; budget comparisons use temperature 0.6, top-$`p`$ 0.95, and
seeds $`\{0,1,2\}`$ against self-consistency (SC) and best-of-$`N`$
(BoN) (Section 4.4). Paired significance tests compare EMO at $`N=9`$
with Base and BoN@10 (Appendix B.4.5). Full decoding settings and token
limits appear in Appendix B.3.

### 4.2 Main Results

##### Broad gains across the model grid.

Table 1 separates overall gains over Base from incremental gains over
context-only optimization. EMO improves over Base in 39 of 40 cells (47
of 48 before merging the two AIME editions), averaging $`+9.10`$
percentage points across the 40 model–task-group pairs. All eight models
improve on average; EMO also exceeds TextGrad in 25 cells and ties in
four, with a mean increment of $`+1.53`$ points. Using Qwen3-8B as a
common reference, we organize the analysis along four axes: model scale,
generation, family, and thinking mode.

##### Scale: complementing model scaling.

We first test whether adaptation benefits persist as target models
scale. Qwen3-14B with EMO exceeds Qwen3-32B Base on all five task
groups, averaging $`69.80`$ versus $`63.86`$. Likewise, optimized
Llama-3.2-3B exceeds Llama-3.1-8B Base on every task group. Larger
models also retain substantial gains: Qwen3-32B improves by $`8.68`$
points on average and $`30.0`$ on AIME. Accuracy headroom alone is
insufficient to predict improvement: Qwen3 gains little on
near-saturated GSM8K, yet Llama gains little on AIME despite near-zero
baselines. Mean gains across Qwen3-8B/14B/32B are non-monotonic
($`9.42/7.44/8.68`$): model size alone does not determine adaptation
benefit. Adaptation can close size-related gaps, but its benefit depends
on whether feedback-guided generation can reach correct solutions.

##### Generation: sustained gains as models evolve.

Across Qwen2.5-7B, Qwen3-8B, and Qwen3.5-9B, EMO yields mean gains of
$`9.64`$, $`9.42`$, and $`6.40`$ points over Base. The incremental gains
over TextGrad are $`2.74`$, $`1.76`$, and $`4.74`$ points, respectively:
the newest generation has the smallest overall gain but the largest
M-step increment. On Qwen3.5-9B, context-only optimization gains just
$`1.66`$ points on average, whereas the full method gains $`6.40`$. AIME
makes this contrast concrete: Base, TextGrad, and EMO score $`25.0`$,
$`26.7`$, and $`45.0`$. Thus, limited gains from context rewrites need
not imply limited scope for adaptation: steering can further exploit the
distributional changes induced by those rewrites.

##### Family: narrowing gaps at matched model size.

Qwen3-8B and Llama-3.1-8B allow a cross-family comparison at matched
parameter scale. On MMLU-Pro, their accuracy gap shrinks from $`18.2`$
points at Base to $`3.6`$ after applying EMO to both models. The
corresponding gaps shrink from $`31.8`$ to $`17.4`$ on MATH-500 and from
$`9.3`$ to $`2.9`$ on GSM8K. Steering contributes directly to the
MMLU-Pro result: its increment over TextGrad is $`10.0`$ points for
Llama versus $`2.7`$ for Qwen. On Llama-3.1-8B/MATH-500, however,
TextGrad already reaches $`65.4`$ versus EMO’s $`65.6`$. The narrowed
family gaps therefore reflect task-dependent contributions from context
refinement and steering.

##### Thinking mode: gains with and without explicit reasoning modes.

The non-thinking Qwen2.5-7B, Llama-3.1-8B, and Llama-3.2-3B gain
$`9.64`$, $`12.22`$, and $`12.96`$ points on average. Qwen2.5-7B also
exceeds TextGrad on all five task groups, showing that M-step gains do
not require a native thinking mode. Models with thinking modes also
improve: the Qwen3 series gains $`7.44`$–$`9.42`$ points, and the
reasoning-distilled DR1-Qwen3-8B gains $`6.02`$. DR1 improves over Base
on all five task groups, showing that reasoning distillation leaves
scope for question-specific adaptation. Yet its M-step increment is
$`-0.64`$ points on average, with an additional gain only on MMLU-Pro
($`68.2`$ vs. $`67.0`$). DR1 and Qwen3.5-9B therefore have similar
overall gains ($`6.02`$ vs. $`6.40`$) but contrasting M-step
contributions ($`-0.64`$ vs. $`+4.74`$). One possible explanation is
that distillation reshapes context-conditioned distributions and their
sensitivity to logit interventions, altering the benefit of a fixed
steering rule. Establishing this mechanism requires matched
pre-/post-distillation controls.

##### Where the M-step is most effective.

Across these axes, MMLU-Pro provides the most consistent steering gains:
all eight models exceed TextGrad, by $`3.76`$ points on average.
MMLU-Pro oracle coverage also improves over TextGrad for all eight
models (mean $`+9.6`$), whereas mean oracle increments on MATH-500 and
GSM8K are at most $`1.4`$ points (Appendix B.4.2). At equal candidate
counts, these results associate steering gains with broader coverage;
mixed GPQA-D results show that multiple-choice format alone does not
explain the effect. Figure 1 shows how the advantage develops across
rounds on Qwen3-14B/GPQA-D. From a shared $`52.5`$ baseline, EMO
continues improving to $`65.2`$, while TextGrad levels off near
$`56`$–$`59`$ and ends at $`58.1`$. EMO’s oracle curve also remains
above TextGrad’s after the first update, reaching $`79.8`$ at $`K=4`$.
Because oracle coverage does not depend on final answer selection, the
curves indicate that improved candidate generation contributes to the
$`7.1`$-point accuracy advantage. We next analyze components, generation
budgets, and selection (Section 4.5).

##### External judging supplies the larger average contribution.

Table 3 varies M-step inclusion and judge choice jointly to distinguish
their contributions on Qwen3-8B. With the M-step retained, replacing
self-judging with the external judge raises average accuracy by $`5.56`$
points; without the M-step, the increase is $`4.74`$ points. With
steering, the external judge’s largest advantages are on AIME ($`46.7`$
vs. $`31.7`$) and GPQA-D ($`56.6`$ vs. $`51.0`$). Since judge choice
affects both feedback and response selection, these differences measure
their combined effect.

##### Steering adds to context optimization under both judge choices.

Holding judge choice fixed, adding the M-step improves average accuracy
by $`1.76`$ points with the external judge and $`0.94`$ with
self-judging. These contributions interact: on Qwen3-8B/GPQA-D, steering
gains $`2.1`$ points with the external judge but loses $`3.5`$ with
self-judging. On Qwen3-32B/GPQA-D, however, steering loses $`2.5`$ under
either judge (Appendix B.4.3). These results show that steering
effectiveness depends on both feedback quality and the target model,
rather than on judge choice alone.

##### Robustness across decoding settings.

Table 3 tests whether gains depend on deterministic target decoding: EMO
improves over Base in all 18 single-seed configurations spanning greedy,
top-$`p`$, and top-$`k`$ decoding. For Llama-3.1-8B on MMLU-Pro, the
respective gains are $`+18.6`$, $`+21.8`$, and $`+25.2`$ points. The
full method’s gains persist under stochastic token selection; this
comparison does not isolate the M-step under sampling.

##### Confidence-constrained execution.

The hyperparameter sweep tests correction strength and retained token
support separately. On Qwen3-8B/GPQA-D, accuracy stays at
$`54.5`$–$`56.6`$ for $`\mu=1.6`$–$`3.2`$ at $`\alpha=0.1`$. At
$`\mu=2.4`$, thresholds $`\alpha=0.1`$–$`0.4`$ yield $`56.6`$–$`58.6`$,
whereas the more permissive $`\alpha=0.05`$ reduces accuracy to $`50.0`$
(Appendix B.5). Gains persist across the tested strengths but weaken at
the most permissive threshold, supporting confidence-constrained
execution. A mask-only ablation would be needed to separate the contrast
signal’s contribution from the support constraint.

##### Budget accounting.

We compare EMO with SC and judge-selected BoN under top-$`p`$ sampling,
using three seeds (Table 4, Figure 2; full results in Appendix B.4.4).
SC/BoN share a sample pool and match EMO’s decoding settings,
per-response token limits, and seeds; BoN also uses the same judge and
tie-breaking rule. The separately generated $`N=1`$ responses can differ
in accuracy despite matching seed identifiers (e.g., $`40.3`$ for EMO
vs. $`37.8`$ for SC/BoN on Llama-3.2-3B/MATH-500), so endpoint gaps
compare the complete procedures rather than isolating the effect of
later feedback rounds. One initial generation and two
context-conditioned streams per optimization round give EMO@$`K`$ a
budget of $`N=2K+1`$ target-model generation equivalents, matched to
$`N`$ independent samples for SC/BoN. The EMO pool contains only $`K+1`$
candidates because the two streams jointly generate one response per
round. This accounting excludes judge/rewrite calls and response-length
differences; total FLOPs and latency are not matched.

##### Clear advantages on Llama, task-dependent gains on Qwen.

At $`N=9`$, EMO exceeds BoN on all six Llama tasks: Llama-3.1-8B GPQA-D
($`37.4`$ vs. $`32.2`$), MATH-500 ($`65.6`$ vs. $`60.8`$), MMLU-Pro
($`68.2`$ vs. $`62.5`$), and Llama-3.2-3B GPQA-D ($`36.5`$ vs.
$`30.0`$), MATH-500 ($`60.3`$ vs. $`52.7`$), MMLU-Pro ($`66.2`$ vs.
$`53.5`$). It also exceeds SC on all six. The margin widens on the
smaller Llama-3.2-3B, reaching $`12.7`$ points on MMLU-Pro. On
Llama-3.1-8B/MMLU-Pro, BoN already exceeds SC by $`11.1`$ points using
the same sample pool: candidate availability alone does not ensure
successful majority voting. EMO then raises both coverage and selected
accuracy beyond BoN. For Qwen3-8B, EMO gains $`2.5`$ points over BoN on
MMLU-Pro, but the endpoint means are close on GPQA-D ($`60.3`$ vs.
$`60.4`$) and MATH-500 ($`85.0`$ vs. $`84.9`$). At $`N=9`$, SC is
strongest on Qwen3-8B/MATH-500 ($`86.1`$). In the two near-ties on Qwen,
EMO also lacks an oracle-coverage advantage (Appendix Table 13); its
clearest accuracy gains coincide with expanded candidate coverage.

### 4.5 Iteration Dynamics and Selection

##### Early gains with task-dependent returns to further iterations.

In the sampling experiments, the first two optimization rounds capture
roughly $`70`$–$`89\%`$ of the final gain in five of six Qwen3-8B and
Llama-3.1-8B model–task pairs (Appendix Table 10); on Llama-3.2-3B the
corresponding range is $`65`$–$`81\%`$. On the Llama-3.2-3B/MATH-500
pair in Figure 2(b), $`K=2`$ reaches $`55.5`$ accuracy ($`76\%`$ of the
eventual gain over the $`40.3`$ initial accuracy), and the last two
rounds add another $`4.8`$ points. Qwen3-8B/GPQA-D is the exception:
$`K=2`$ captures about $`46\%`$, and accuracy continues to improve
through $`K=4`$. These trajectories motivate task-dependent iteration
budgets, with additional rounds remaining useful where early gains are
limited.

##### Candidate coverage and answer selection impose different limits.

At $`N=9`$, EMO’s five-candidate oracle coverage exceeds that of nine
independent samples in seven of nine settings (Appendix Table 13).
Figure 2(a) illustrates the largest coverage advantage, on
Llama-3.1-8B/GPQA-D. EMO already reaches $`35.5`$ accuracy at $`K=1`$
($`N=3`$), exceeding BoN with ten samples ($`33.0`$). Its dashed oracle
curve separates further from independent sampling, reaching $`73.7`$
versus $`60.3`$ at $`N=9`$, despite using five rather than nine
candidates. However, the growing gap between EMO’s dashed and solid
curves exposes a selection bottleneck: from $`K=1`$ to $`K=4`$, coverage
rises from $`49.3`$ to $`73.7`$, while selected accuracy rises only from
$`35.5`$ to $`37.4`$. At $`N=9`$, selected accuracy as a fraction of
coverage is $`50.7\%`$ for EMO versus $`53.4\%`$ for BoN: the advantage
comes from broader coverage despite a lower fraction realized by
selection (Appendix B.4.4). Figure 2(b) shows a different pattern on
Llama-3.2-3B/MATH-500: at $`N=9`$, EMO exceeds BoN in both coverage
($`70.6`$ vs. $`66.5`$) and selected accuracy ($`60.3`$ vs. $`52.7`$).
The selected-to-covered fraction is also higher ($`85.4\%`$ vs.
$`79.2\%`$), so the accuracy gap accompanies gains in both candidate
coverage and the fraction of covered cases selected correctly. At the
other extreme, Llama-3.2-3B/AIME has oracle coverage of only $`3.3`$ and
$`6.7`$ on the two editions (Appendix Table 8), so better selection
alone would yield limited gains. These regimes call for stronger ranking
when correct candidates exist and better generation when coverage is
low.

## 5 Conclusion

EMO couples question-specific context optimization with logits steering,
using each response to refine the context that guides the next
generation. Its logit-space analysis clarifies this connection and
identifies conditions for one-round target-KL improvement. Across eight
models and six benchmarks, the results show that model scaling and
reasoning distillation leave scope for adaptation, with steering
benefits that depend on the model and task. The broader implication is
to treat generation and verification as coupled uses of test-time
computation. Feedback can make further attempts more productive, while
reliable selection determines how much of their expanded coverage
reaches the final answer. This perspective motivates inference systems
that adjust context revision, decoding strength, and verification effort
jointly to the bottleneck encountered during reasoning.

##### Limitations and future work.

External judging can introduce misleading feedback or miss correct
candidates. Calibrated ranking and independent checks of reasoning steps
or tool outputs could improve verification; retrieval and multiple
optimization trajectories could address low candidate coverage.
Distribution access and two model streams limit deployment, and
generation-equivalent budgets omit judge/rewrite costs. Smaller judges,
selective M-step activation, and adaptive stopping could reduce
overhead; evaluations should measure total tokens, FLOPs, and latency.
Text-only interfaces could use response-level refinement and reranking,
subject to separate evaluation. The one-round guarantee assumes
successful context updates and faithful steering over full softmax
distributions; it does not certify default settings or monotonic
accuracy. Context-update acceptance checks, adaptive steering strength,
and fallback to context-only decoding could improve robustness, using
observable signals such as verifier disagreement and score margins.
Evaluation is limited to reasoning benchmarks in two model families.
Alternative judges, multilingual tasks, long-context workflows, and
tool-using agents would test transfer and error accumulation over longer
adaptation cycles.

## Appendix A Proofs and Method Supplementary Details

### A.1 Proof of the Unified Objective

For each question with reference response $`y^{\star}`$, define
$`Q(y\mid q)=\mathbf{1}\{y=y^{\star}\}`$. Then

```math
\mathrm{KL}(Q\|P_{\xi})=\sum_{y:Q(y\mid q)>0}Q(y\mid q)\log\frac{Q(y\mid q)}{P_{\xi}(y\mid q)}=-\log P_{\xi}(y^{\star}\mid q). \tag{11}
```

Averaging over the empirical question distribution proves Lemma 1. The
equality holds independently of which component of
$`\xi=(\bm{\theta},C)`$ is varied. For a general fixed target,
cross-entropy equals $`\mathrm{KL}(Q\|P_{\xi})+H(Q)`$, and the entropy
term is independent of either optimization variable. Thus the minimizers
of the two losses agree within each chosen feasible set; the parameter
and context feasible sets need not have the same minimizer.

In the test-time setting, take the question distribution to be
concentrated on the current question. The target objective remains a
theoretical reference: the gold response is supplied to neither the
judge nor the textual optimizer. The ideal context optimizer is defined
operationally as producing a context with smaller target loss whenever
an improving update is assumed to exist. No differentiable structure on
the space of strings is needed for that condition, and the objective
identity does not assert that arbitrary textual feedback exactly
differentiates NLL.

### A.2 Proof of the Unified Steering Form

##### Exact realization of either optimization update.

At a fixed history, let $`\mathbf{z}=\mathbf{z}_{\xi}(h)`$ and
$`\mathbf{z}^{+}=\mathbf{z}_{\xi^{+}}(h)`$ be the before/after logits.
Defining $`\Delta\mathbf{z}=\mathbf{z}^{+}-\mathbf{z}`$ immediately
gives
$`\mathrm{Softmax}(\mathbf{z}+\Delta\mathbf{z})=P_{\xi^{+}}(\cdot\mid h)`$.
Equivalently, the log-ratio $`\log(P_{\xi^{+}}/P_{\xi})`$ differs from
$`\Delta\mathbf{z}`$ only by the scalar difference of log-normalizers.
Softmax ignores this token-independent constant. For parameter
optimization, the difference is
$`f_{\bm{\phi}}(C,h)-f_{\bm{\theta}}(C,h)`$; for context optimization it
is $`f_{\bm{\theta}}(C^{\prime},h)-f_{\bm{\theta}}(C,h)`$. Applying the
equality at every history and multiplying the conditional probabilities
reproduces the optimized autoregressive distribution. For multiple
optimization steps, the logit differences telescope at each fixed
history, so the same statement applies to the entire resulting output
change.

##### The ideal direct-logit gradient.

For $`P=\mathrm{Softmax}(\mathbf{z})`$ and a fixed conditional target
$`Q`$,

```math
\displaystyle\nabla_{P}\mathrm{KL}(Q\|P) \displaystyle=-Q/P, \tag{12}
```

```math
\displaystyle-\nabla_{\mathbf{z}}\mathrm{KL}(Q\|P) \displaystyle=\mathbf{J}_{P}(Q/P)=Q-P\sum_{v}Q(v)=Q-P. \tag{13}
```

The division is componentwise; finite logits ensure strictly positive
$`P`$. The chain rule uses $`\mathbf{J}_{P}^{\top}=\mathbf{J}_{P}`$.
Softmax ensures that the updated probabilities remain normalized. For
$`\bm{\delta}^{*}=Q-P`$,

```math
\left.\frac{d}{d\eta}\mathrm{KL}\!\left(Q\|\mathrm{Softmax}(\mathbf{z}+\eta\bm{\delta}^{*})\right)\right|_{\eta=0}=\langle P-Q,\bm{\delta}^{*}\rangle=-\|\bm{\delta}^{*}\|_{2}^{2}. \tag{14}
```

When $`Q\neq P`$, differentiability gives strict improvement for all
sufficiently small positive $`\eta`$. When $`Q=P`$, the direction is
zero and no strict improvement is claimed. For a point-mass target, the
reference token receives $`1-P(y^{\star})`$ and other tokens receive
$`-P(v)`$.

##### Which equivalence this establishes.

The additive realization is exact for the output of either optimization
process. The ideal gradient formula concerns direct optimization over
logits. For a differentiable parameter logit map with Jacobian
$`\mathbf{G}=\partial\mathbf{z}/\partial\bm{\theta}`$, a parameter
gradient step instead induces

```math
\mathbf{z}_{\bm{\theta}^{+}}-\mathbf{z}_{\bm{\theta}}=-\eta\mathbf{G}\mathbf{G}^{\top}(P-Q)+O(\eta^{2}), \tag{15}
```

assuming the map is twice continuously differentiable near the update.
This follows by applying the chain rule to the parameter loss and
Taylor-expanding the updated logits. For a training loss over multiple
examples and histories, the analogous statement uses stacked logits,
their Jacobian, and the full weighted loss gradient. Textual updates
induce finite differences without requiring a string derivative. These
observations establish a shared logit-space representation without
identifying different optimizers’ actual directions with $`Q-P`$.

##### Connection to reinforcement learning.

At a fixed history, let $`P`$ be a positive reference policy, $`A`$ a
fixed token advantage, and $`\beta>0`$. For the unrestricted policy
problem
$`\max_{\pi}\{\langle\pi,A\rangle-\beta^{-1}\mathrm{KL}(\pi\|P)\}`$,
define $`Z=\sum_{v}P(v)e^{\beta A(v)}`$ and
$`\pi^{*}(v)=P(v)e^{\beta A(v)}/Z`$. The objective equals
$`\beta^{-1}\log Z-\beta^{-1}\mathrm{KL}(\pi\|\pi^{*})`$, so its unique
maximizer is $`\pi^{*}`$. It therefore has the additive form
$`\pi^{*}=\mathrm{Softmax}(\mathbf{z}+\beta A)`$. This gives the
policy-improvement connection under the stated fixed-history objective;
it does not identify arbitrary parameterized RL updates with direct
target-KL gradient descent.

### A.3 From the Context Contrast to the Steering Vector

The construction follows the probability-gradient-to-logit chain rule in
SVDecode (Hu et al., 2026). Hold $`P_{\mathrm{base}}`$ fixed and
differentiate at
$`P_{\mathrm{opt}}=\mathrm{Softmax}(\mathbf{z}^{\mathrm{opt}})`$:

```math
\displaystyle\nabla_{P_{\mathrm{opt}}}\mathrm{KL}(P_{\mathrm{opt}}\|P_{\mathrm{base}}) \displaystyle=\log(P_{\mathrm{opt}}/P_{\mathrm{base}})+\mathbf{1}, \tag{16}
```

```math
\displaystyle\nabla_{\mathbf{z}^{\mathrm{opt}}}\mathrm{KL}(P_{\mathrm{opt}}\|P_{\mathrm{base}}) \displaystyle=\mathbf{J}_{P_{\mathrm{opt}}}\log(P_{\mathrm{opt}}/P_{\mathrm{base}}). \tag{17}
```

The constant term vanishes because
$`\mathbf{J}_{P_{\mathrm{opt}}}\mathbf{1}=0`$. This proves Eq. (4); the
equality defines the implemented vector, while its task-alignment
interpretation remains conditional. Writing
$`r(v)=\log P_{\mathrm{opt}}(v)-\log P_{\mathrm{base}}(v)`$ gives the
centered expression

```math
\delta_{t}(v)=P_{\mathrm{opt}}(v)\left(r(v)-\sum_{w}P_{\mathrm{opt}}(w)r(w)\right). \tag{18}
```

Thus a positive correction requires a log-ratio above its
optimized-distribution average. The condition
$`P_{\mathrm{opt}}(v)>P_{\mathrm{base}}(v)`$ alone is insufficient. The
expression is evaluated in linear vocabulary time using log-softmax
values, without forming a dense Jacobian or backpropagating through
model parameters.

For the unmasked path
$`P(u)=\mathrm{Softmax}(\mathbf{z}^{\mathrm{opt}}+u\bm{\delta}_{t})`$,

```math
\left.\frac{d}{du}\mathrm{KL}(P(u)\|P_{\mathrm{base}})\right|_{u=0}=\|\bm{\delta}_{t}\|_{2}^{2}. \tag{19}
```

This explains the positive sign for local extrapolation in Eq. (4). The
negative-gradient convention in SVDecode would give the opposite local
change for positive strength. Neither sign determines target improvement
without a target-alignment condition. In particular, the log-ratio in
Eq. (4) is an observable contrast, not an identity or a generally
consistent estimate of $`Q/P_{\mathrm{opt}}`$ in Lemma 2. After masking,
the direction is $`\hat{\bm{\delta}}_{t}`$ and all target-alignment
reasoning must use this actual vector.

##### Complementarity and sequential dependence.

The steering operator supplies an execution rule, whereas feedback and
context optimization supply task information. For any fixed direction
$`\mathbf{d}`$, the derivative of target KL at zero is
$`\langle P-Q,\mathbf{d}\rangle`$, so its usefulness depends on the
target. EMO obtains its direction only after $`C_{k+1}`$ has been
estimated; the pair $`(C_{k},C_{k+1})`$ then determines the contrast at
each prefix. The resulting steered response drives the next context
update. This is the ordered, complementary relationship underlying the
E/M terminology. Classical EM monotonicity would require a specified
latent-variable objective and corresponding expectation and maximization
steps, which are not supplied by this structural correspondence alone.

### A.4 Proof of Conditional One-Round Improvement

Fix a question and condition on the realized contexts $`C_{k},C_{k+1}`$,
including any randomness in the E-step. Abbreviate the fixed sequence
target by $`Q`$; it may be the point-mass reference target from Lemma 1
or a distribution over multiple admissible responses. Use a finite
vocabulary $`\mathcal{V}`$, maximum horizon $`T`$, finite logits at
nonterminal histories, and a fixed threshold $`\alpha`$. After EOS, pad
with shared deterministic EOS transitions and zero directions; their KL
contributions are zero. All distributions use this common finite-horizon
convention. The distributions below are full softmax distributions
before temperature scaling or decoder truncation. Let
$`\widetilde{P}_{\mu}`$ denote the steered sequence distribution, so
$`\widetilde{P}_{0}=P_{\bm{\theta},C_{k+1}}`$. For a fixed history,
$`\hat{\bm{\delta}}_{t}(h)`$ depends only on the contexts and
$`\alpha`$, not on $`\mu`$. Generated prefixes can change with $`\mu`$,
but the direction at any specified prefix remains fixed; the proof
differentiates this conditional distribution field, not a sampled
decoding trajectory. Only histories with positive probability under
$`Q`$ contribute, so target conditionals at other histories may be
defined arbitrarily.

##### The two conditions.

The oracle E-step condition is

```math
\mathrm{KL}(Q\|P_{\bm{\theta},C_{k+1}})<\mathrm{KL}(Q\|P_{\bm{\theta},C_{k}}). \tag{20}
```

Faithful steering is made precise by requiring positive aggregate
alignment of the actual masked direction with the ideal target descent
direction:

```math
\Gamma=\sum_{t=1}^{T}\mathbb{E}_{Y_{<t}\sim Q}\left\langle Q_{t}(\cdot\mid h)-P_{\mathrm{opt}}(\cdot\mid h),\hat{\bm{\delta}}_{t}(h)\right\rangle>0. \tag{21}
```

This condition includes the threshold and finite penalty from Eq. (7).
It permits some histories to have unfavorable directions, provided their
target-weighted aggregate is favorable.

##### Conditional derivative and sequence composition.

For
$`\widetilde{P}_{\mu}(\cdot\mid h)=\mathrm{Softmax}(\mathbf{z}_{t}^{\mathrm{opt}}+\mu\hat{\bm{\delta}}_{t})`$,
differentiation gives

```math
\left.\frac{d}{d\mu}\mathrm{KL}(Q_{t}\|\widetilde{P}_{\mu}(\cdot\mid h))\right|_{\mu=0}=-\langle Q_{t}-P_{\mathrm{opt}},\hat{\bm{\delta}}_{t}\rangle. \tag{22}
```

Expanding the sequence log-ratio into conditional log-ratios gives the
KL chain rule:

```math
\mathrm{KL}(Q\|\widetilde{P}_{\mu})=\sum_{t=1}^{T}\mathbb{E}_{Y_{<t}\sim Q}\mathrm{KL}\!\left(Q_{t}(\cdot\mid h)\|\widetilde{P}_{\mu}(\cdot\mid h)\right). \tag{23}
```

The vocabulary and horizon are finite, so differentiation may be
interchanged with these finite sums. The derivative of sequence KL at
$`\mu=0`$ is the negative of Eq. (21), hence strictly negative. By
differentiability there is $`\varepsilon>0`$ such that every
$`0<\mu<\varepsilon`$ gives

```math
\mathrm{KL}(Q\|\widetilde{P}_{\mu})<\mathrm{KL}(Q\|P_{\bm{\theta},C_{k+1}})<\mathrm{KL}(Q\|P_{\bm{\theta},C_{k}}), \tag{24}
```

where the second inequality is Eq. (20). This proves Theorem 1.

##### A quantitative sufficient step-size interval.

The local result can be made explicit using the curvature of sequence KL
along the fixed steering direction. Write
$`F(\mu)=\mathrm{KL}(Q\|\widetilde{P}_{\mu})`$ and
$`\mathbf{d}_{t}(h)=\hat{\bm{\delta}}_{t}(h)`$. Differentiating the
conditional log-normalizer twice gives

```math
\displaystyle F^{\prime}(\mu) \displaystyle=\sum_{t=1}^{T}\mathbb{E}_{Y_{<t}\sim Q}\left\langle\widetilde{P}_{\mu}(\cdot\mid h)-Q_{t}(\cdot\mid h),\mathbf{d}_{t}(h)\right\rangle, \tag{25}
```

```math
\displaystyle F^{\prime\prime}(\mu) \displaystyle=\sum_{t=1}^{T}\mathbb{E}_{Y_{<t}\sim Q}\operatorname{Var}_{V\sim\widetilde{P}_{\mu}(\cdot\mid h)}[d_{t}(h,V)]\geq 0, \tag{26}
```

where $`V`$ is a next-token random variable and $`d_{t}(h,v)`$ is the
$`v`$th entry of $`\mathbf{d}_{t}(h)`$. Thus $`F`$ is convex along this
one-dimensional path, even though context optimization itself is
discrete and need not be convex. Define the range of the constrained
correction and its target-weighted squared-range bound by

```math
\rho_{t}(h)=\max_{v\in\mathcal{V}}d_{t}(h,v)-\min_{v\in\mathcal{V}}d_{t}(h,v),\qquad L=\frac{1}{4}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\mathbb{E}_{Y_{<t}\sim Q}\rho_{t}(h)^{2}. \tag{27}
```

For a random variable supported on $`[a,b]`$, its variance is at most
$`(b-a)^{2}/4`$: subtracting the midpoint gives a second moment bounded
by this quantity, and variance is no larger than that second moment.
Consequently, $`0\leq F^{\prime\prime}(\mu)\leq L`$ for every finite
$`\mu`$. The bound is finite because the vocabulary, horizon, logits,
and penalty are finite. Moreover, $`\Gamma>0`$ implies $`L>0`$:
otherwise all directions at target-supported histories would be
tokenwise constant, making their inner products with
$`Q_{t}-P_{\mathrm{opt}}`$ zero. Integrating the curvature bound and
using $`F^{\prime}(0)=-\Gamma`$ yields

```math
F(\mu)\leq F(0)-\mu\Gamma+\frac{L\mu^{2}}{2},\qquad\mu\geq 0. \tag{28}
```

Hence every $`0<\mu<2\Gamma/L`$ strictly improves over the unsteered
updated-context distribution. At $`\mu=\Gamma/L`$, the bound guarantees
a decrease of at least $`\Gamma^{2}/(2L)`$. Combined with Eq. 20, this
also gives improvement over the preceding context. This sufficient
interval depends on the unknown target through $`\Gamma`$ and $`L`$; it
is an analytical guarantee, not an implementable tuning prescription or
a certificate for the empirical default.

Convexity also clarifies the role of the alignment assumption. If
$`\Gamma\leq 0`$, then $`F^{\prime}(0)\geq 0`$ and
$`F(\mu)\geq F(0)+\mu F^{\prime}(0)\geq F(0)`$ for all $`\mu\geq 0`$.
Thus, for fixed contexts and threshold, positive aggregate alignment is
necessary and sufficient for some positive strength to improve target KL
over $`P_{\bm{\theta},C_{k+1}}`$ along this path. This statement does
not compare different context updates, thresholds, or steered rounds.

##### Role of the confidence constraint.

The alignment contribution at a history is

```math
\langle Q_{t}-P_{\mathrm{opt}},\hat{\bm{\delta}}_{t}\rangle=\sum_{v:\mathbb{I}_{t}(v)=1}(Q_{t}(v)-P_{\mathrm{opt}}(v))\delta_{t}(v)+\lambda\sum_{v:\mathbb{I}_{t}(v)=0}(Q_{t}(v)-P_{\mathrm{opt}}(v)). \tag{29}
```

Changing $`\alpha`$ changes both sets and can therefore change the sign
of this contribution. Writing $`S_{t}=\{v:\mathbb{I}_{t}(v)=1\}`$, the
excluded-token contribution is
$`\lambda[Q_{t}(S_{t}^{c})-P_{\mathrm{opt}}(S_{t}^{c})]`$. Because
$`\lambda<0`$, this term helps alignment when excluded tokens carry less
target mass than model mass, and hurts when they carry more. Relative
model confidence is therefore a plausibility criterion, not a guarantee
of target correctness. The claim applies to the masked direction at the
chosen $`\alpha`$, not merely the raw contrast. The sufficient interval
for $`\mu`$ depends on these directions and the unknown target; the
proof does not certify the empirical default or yield a test-time tuning
rule. The oracle E-step condition does not by itself imply Eq. (21).
Across rounds, the comparison distribution above is the preceding
unsteered context distribution, not the preceding round’s steered
distribution. These distinctions prevent a conditional one-round result
from being read as unconditional iterative convergence or an accuracy
guarantee.

##### Why the two assumptions are distinct.

A one-step, three-token example shows that context improvement need not
imply useful steering. Take $`Q=(1,0,0)^{\top}`$,
$`P_{\mathrm{base}}=(0.2,0.3,0.5)^{\top}`$, and
$`P_{\mathrm{opt}}=(0.25,0.65,0.10)^{\top}`$. The context update
improves target KL from $`-\log 0.2`$ to $`-\log 0.25`$. At
$`\alpha=0.1`$, all tokens pass the confidence threshold, and Eq. 18
gives $`\bm{\delta}\approx(-0.0436,0.2443,-0.2007)^{\top}`$.
Nevertheless,
$`\Gamma=\langle Q-P_{\mathrm{opt}},\bm{\delta}\rangle\approx-0.1714<0`$.
The context update increases the correct token’s probability, but the
contrast favors a competing token strongly enough that further
extrapolation raises target KL. This constructed example illustrates the
logical need for Assumption 2; it is not an experimental result.

### A.5 Non-Regression under Judge Selection

Store the score $`a_{k}=\mathcal{J}(q,R_{k})`$ once for each response,
and select the earliest candidate attaining the largest score. Then the
selected score is at least every stored score, including $`a_{0}`$.
Adding candidates can only increase or preserve this maximum. This
proves the non-regression observation when $`\mathrm{Score}`$ denotes
the stored judge score. It does not establish non-regression of
rule-based correctness, which can disagree with judge ranking.

### A.6 Implementation Conventions

The steering strength defaults to $`\mu=2.4`$ and the threshold to
$`\alpha=0.1`$; the finite penalty is fixed at $`\lambda=-100`$. An
excluded token therefore receives an additive logit change
$`\mu\lambda`$; this is distinct from overwriting its final logit with
$`-100`$. The same prefix is appended to separate KV caches for
$`C_{k}`$ and $`C_{k+1}`$. Contrast and masking are computed before any
sampling temperature or top-$`p`$ truncation. No context is carried from
one test question to the next. In the stated update, rejecting a rewrite
sets $`C_{k+1}=C_{k}`$ and makes the raw contrast zero. The confidence
penalty in Eq. 7 can still affect decoding when $`\mu>0`$; zero contrast
alone does not remove this constraint. In contrast, setting $`\mu=0`$
removes both the contrast correction and the penalty and recovers
context-only decoding. This distinguishes a rejected rewrite from
explicitly disabling the M-step.

With $`K=4`$ refinement rounds, the initial response and four steered
responses form five candidates. Each M-step uses two target-model
context streams, giving nine generation equivalents including
initialization. This counts streams rather than exact FLOPs or latency
and excludes external judge, backward, and rewrite cost as well as
differences in context and response lengths.

##### AIME 2025/2026.

The American Invitational Mathematics Examination is a
competition-mathematics benchmark with 30 problems per edition; answers
are integers in $`[0,999]`$. We use the 2025 and 2026 editions as
separate test sets (30 problems each) and merge them in the main table
for noise reduction ($`n=30`$ per year yields a granularity of $`3.3`$
points).

##### GPQA-Diamond.

The Diamond subset of the Graduate-Level Google-Proof Q&A benchmark
consists of 198 multiple-choice science questions written and validated
by domain experts (physics, chemistry, biology). Each question has four
options; we score the letter inside boxed{}  (falling back to an Answer:
X pattern).

##### GSM8K.

Grade School Math 8.5K contains 1,319 grade-school word problems in its
test split. Following the original format, models must write the final
numerical answer on the last line as \#### \<number\>; scoring is exact
numeric match after stripping commas and decimal points. GSM8K is the
most saturated benchmark in our suite (three models exceed $`89\%`$
baseline accuracy), which bounds the observable headroom.

##### MATH-500.

We use the 500-problem evaluation subset of the MATH competition
dataset (Hendrycks et al., 2021), following the held-out split of
Lightman et al. (2023). It covers seven subjects with LaTeX-form answers
(e.g., $`\frac{1}{2}`$, $`-\sqrt{3}`$, $`5r^{5}`$). This benchmark
stresses symbolic answer matching; our scoring pipeline handles numeric
tolerance, LaTeX normalization, and SymPy-based symbolic equivalence
(Appendix B.3).

##### MMLU-Pro (stratified subset).

The full MMLU-Pro test set contains 12,032 questions across 14 subject
categories with up to 10 options each. For compute efficiency we
evaluate on a category-balanced subset of 560 questions: within each of
the 14 categories we sample 40 questions uniformly at random with a
fixed seed (seed=42), giving equal representation to all categories. The
sampling procedure is implemented in datasets/process_mmlu_pro.py and is
exactly reproducible:

> rng = random.Random(42)  
> sampled = \[rng.sample(cat_items\[c\], 40) for c in categories\]

Prompts are constructed from the question stem and the full option
block, with the output format Answer: \$LETTER\$ and a step-by-step
instruction. The three-task sampling analysis (top-$`p`$, 3 seeds) uses
GPQA-Diamond, MATH-500, and MMLU-Pro to cover science MC, math
free-form, and general-knowledge MC; we exclude AIME ($`n=30`$, high
variance) and GSM8K (saturated) from that analysis.

##### Scale.

Qwen3-8B/14B/32B and Llama-3.2-3B/Llama-3.1-8B probe whether test-time
gains shrink as models grow (main-table $`\overline{\Delta}`$ analysis).
Mean gains within Qwen3 are non-monotonic ($`+9.42`$, $`+7.44`$, and
$`+8.68`$ points for 8B, 14B, and 32B), showing that parameter scale
alone does not determine adaptation benefit. The larger gains on AIME
than on near-saturated GSM8K further indicate task dependence within
each scale.

##### Generation.

Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct $`\to`$ Qwen3-8B $`\to`$ Qwen3.5-9B form a
three-generation line within one family. Every generation still gains
$`+6`$ to $`+10`$ points on average after test-time optimization,
answering the question of whether newer base models obsolete
per-question adaptation: they do not.

##### Family.

Qwen3-8B vs. Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct compares families at matched
parameter scale, while retaining differences in architecture and
training. Llama-3.1-8B gains $`22.4`$ points on MATH-500, demonstrating
applicability beyond the Qwen family; the comparison does not isolate an
individual training choice.

##### Thinking mode.

Qwen3-8B natively supports a thinking mode (explicit chain of thought),
whereas Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct and Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct do not.
Non-thinking models gain more over Base on MATH-500 ($`+9.2`$/$`+22.4`$
vs. $`+8.0`$), showing that the full framework also benefits models
without a native thinking mode. Because the checkpoints differ, these
gains do not establish that steering substitutes for thinking; on
Llama-3.1-8B, TextGrad already accounts for most of the MATH-500
improvement.

##### Reasoning distillation.

DeepSeek-R1-0528-Qwen3-8B was obtained by post-training Qwen3-8B-Base on
chain-of-thought traces distilled from DeepSeek-R1-0528 (DeepSeek-AI,
2025). EMO improves its own Base on all five task groups ($`+6.02`$
points on average), demonstrating that this distilled checkpoint retains
scope for test-time adaptation. Its mean M-step increment over TextGrad
is $`-0.64`$, although MMLU-Pro improves by $`1.2`$ points; thus the
benefits of the full adaptation loop and of steering must be
distinguished. A possible explanation is that distillation changes the
conditional distributions on which steering operates. In Eq. 5, both the
softmax Jacobian $`\mathbf{J}_{P_{\mathrm{opt}}}`$ and the
context-induced displacement $`\Delta\mathbf{z}_{t}`$ depend on the
checkpoint; changes to either can alter the steering vector even with
fixed $`\mu`$ and $`\alpha`$. Our Qwen3-8B comparison is not a
controlled pre-/post-distillation pair, so this remains a mechanistic
hypothesis. Testing it would require matched checkpoints, contexts,
prefixes, and decoding budgets, measuring conditional entropy,
context-induced logit displacements, and reference-token probability
changes. The current accuracy results do not establish reduced entropy,
overconfidence, or reinforcement-learning saturation. For this model we
report the v2 rerun with an extended generation budget (16,384 tokens),
as its verbose thinking chains saturate the default 8,192-token cap
(Appendix B.3).

### B.3 Experimental Settings

##### Metrics.

_Final accuracy_ is rule-based and gold-answer-based; the judge score is
never used for reporting, only as the internal optimization signal.

- •
  math (GSM8K): extract the \#### \<number\> final line; exact numeric
  match after removing commas and decimal points.
- •
  math_boxed (MATH-500, AIME): extract the content of the final
  \boxed{} ; equality is decided by a four-stage cascade—(i) numeric
  comparison with $`10^{-6}`$ tolerance (resolving $`\frac{1}{2}`$ vs.
  $`0.5`$), (ii) normalized string match (LaTeX normalization: stripping
  \left/\right, \tfrac$`\to`$\frac, spacing), (iii) SymPy symbolic
  equivalence via parse_latex+sympify combinations, and (iv) a
  non-trivial-substring fallback (shorter side $`\geq 2`$ characters and
  length ratio $`<3`$).
- •
  mc_boxed (GPQA, MMLU-Pro): the letter inside boxed{} , falling back to
  an Answer: X regular expression; exact letter match with gold (options
  A–J supported).

judge-select@k: the reported EMO accuracy looks at the first $`k{+}1`$
rounds (iter0..iter$`k`$) and selects the round with the highest judge
score (ties broken toward the earlier round), reporting its rule-based
correctness. The main tables use judge-select@4 over five candidates:
the initial response and four optimized responses. pass@$`N`$ is the
oracle upper bound: the question counts as solved if any of the first
$`N`$ attempts is correct; it is reported only for reference.

##### Hyperparameters.

All experiments use four optimization rounds after the initial
generation, with one E-step sample and one M-step sample per round, and
the shared initial context _“You are a helpful assistant.”_ Steering
defaults: total strength $`\mu{=}2.4`$, confidence threshold
$`\alpha{=}0.1`$, per-step steering recomputation, and penalty value
$`-100`$ on masked logits. The generation budget (max_new_tokens) is
8,192 for GPQA/MATH-500/MMLU-Pro/GSM8K and 12,288 for AIME in the greedy
main experiments, with the DeepSeek-R1 v2 rerun using 16,384
(Appendix B.2); the top-$`p`$ sampling experiments use 24,576 for
Qwen3-8B (thinking chains are long) and 12,288 for the Llama models. The
top-$`p`$ sampling experiments use temperature $`0.6`$, top-$`p`$
$`0.95`$, top-$`k`$ $`0`$, and seeds $`\{0,1,2\}`$ with full-chain
seeding (torch.manual_seed at all four sampling sites); each seed also
keys the response cache so that seeds do not share generations. A
hyperparameter sweep on Qwen3-8B/GPQA (Appendix B.5) shows gains are
insensitive to the total strength ($`+5.6`$ to $`+7.6`$ points across
$`\mu\in[1.6,3.2]`$) and robust for $`\alpha\in[0.1,0.4]`$, while
$`\alpha{=}0.05`$ collapses ($`+1.0`$) because the confidence constraint
becomes too loose. Table 7 consolidates all decoding and generation
settings per experiment.

##### Judge deployment.

The judge is Qwen3.5-122B-A10B (FP8), served with vLLM (tensor
parallelism 4, max context 32,768, CUDA graphs enabled). In the greedy
main experiments, the target model and all judge-side calls—scoring,
feedback, backward, and context rewriting—use greedy decoding. In the
sampling experiments, judge scoring remains greedy with disk caching,
while feedback, backward, and context rewriting use temperature $`0.7`$,
top-$`p`$ $`0.95`$, and no caching. Scoring and E-step calls use
separate engine instances. Both engines disable the judge’s thinking
mode. The judge never sees the gold answer; its prompt requires it to
independently solve the problem, score 0–10, and—critically—produce
feedback _without revealing or hinting at the final answer_ (numbers,
letters, or expressions), which keeps the E-step gradient estimate free
of answer leakage. An additional regex filter rejects any rewritten
context that leaks a concrete answer, retaining the previous context in
that case.

##### Answer-extraction fix and rescoring.

An early version of the math_boxed scorer hard-extracted digits from
non-numeric boxed answers (e.g., $`7\pi\to 7`$), systematically
under-reporting MATH-500 accuracy. After fixing the extraction cascade,
we rescored _all_ affected stored responses with the corrected rule
(flips occur only on MATH-500; GPQA/MMLU/GSM8K/AIME are unchanged), and
all numbers in this paper use the rescored values. The rescored greedy
baseline of Qwen3-8B on MATH-500 ($`75.0`$) is consistent with the
independently run sampling baseline ($`79.6{\pm}0.4`$ at temperature
0.6), validating the fix.

##### Degenerate-generation handling (Llama).

Llama-3.1/3.2 occasionally enter repetitive loops, filling the entire
generation budget with a short cycle. Beyond the standard exact-cycle
detector (10–30 token loops), we add an information-saturation detector:
once a generation exceeds 4,000 tokens, it is terminated early if the
novel-token rate over each of the last three 200-token windows falls
below $`0.10`$ relative to a 400-token baseline window. On a calibration
set the detector hits $`97\%`$ of degenerate chains with $`0\%`$ false
positives on normal chains and speeds up MATH-500 by $`{\sim}12\times`$.
Degenerate responses are almost always incorrect, so early termination
has negligible effect on accuracy.

##### Two-arm protocol.

The TextGrad (no-M-step) arm shares its baseline and iter-0 responses
with the EMO arm by construction (reuse-baseline/iter0-from), so both
arms start from an identical state and diverge only from iteration 1
onward. In the sampling experiments the no-M-step arm additionally
reuses the same sharding, guaranteeing a 1:1 question match across arms.

##### Compute.

Each optimization round generates one response using two
context-conditioned model streams. Including the initial generation,
EMO@$`k`$ uses $`N=2k+1`$ target-model generation equivalents and
produces $`k+1`$ candidates. Section 4.4 matches this budget to $`N`$
independent samples for SC/BoN; the accounting excludes judge/rewrite
calls, response-length differences, and latency. Judge throughput is not
a bottleneck: with CUDA graphs, the 122B FP8 judge sustains $`{\sim}22`$
concurrent scoring workers per 4-GPU instance.

##### Self-consistency and Best-of-$`N`$ baselines.

The SC/BoN comparison shares the sampling pool of the top-$`p`$
experiments: for each question we draw $`n{=}10`$ i.i.d. samples at
temperature $`0.6`$, top-$`p`$ $`0.95`$, with the same per-model
generation budget and the same seeds $`\{0,1,2\}`$ as the EMO sampling
runs. BoN@$`N`$ selects, among the first $`N`$ samples, the one with the
highest judge score (the same scoring engine and prompt as EMO’s
judge-select, ties broken toward the earlier sample) and reports its
rule-based correctness. SC@$`N`$ takes a majority vote over the
extracted final answers of the first $`N`$ samples (the same extraction
rules as the main scoring pipeline); a sample whose answer cannot be
extracted abstains, and if all $`N`$ abstain the first sample’s
rule-based verdict is used. On a vote tie, the answer with the highest
total judge score among the tied candidates is selected. pass@$`N`$ is
the oracle upper bound shared by SC and BoN (any of the first $`N`$
samples correct).

#### B.4.2 Accuracy vs. Iteration for All Models

Figures 3–8 plot the accuracy of the judge-selected response (solid

EMO iteration $`n`$, for all 8 models on each of the six datasets. All
curves are computed from the greedy main experiments, with the baseline

subsection, iteration $`n\in\{1,\dots,5\}`$ counts the baseline round as

elsewhere).

Three patterns emerge. (i) Early convergence. Across the 44 (model,
task) pairs with a non-saturated gain ($`\geq 2`$ points), iteration 2
already realizes on average about half of the final gain of EMO, and on
saturated tasks (GSM8K for the Qwen3 family) the curve flattens after a
single iteration. The main exceptions are the hardest tasks (AIME,
GPQA-D for weak models), where gains accrue through iteration
4—consistent with headroom being unlocked late when the base policy is
far from the target. (ii) Steering benefits depend on the task beyond
its answer format. On MMLU-Pro the pass@5 of EMO exceeds that of
TextGrad for all 8 models (mean gap $`+9.6`$ points, up to $`+17.9`$ for
Llama-3.1-8B: $`74.5`$ vs. $`56.6`$), and the selected accuracy likewise
wins $`8/8`$. On free-form mathematics (MATH-500, GSM8K), the mean
pass@5 gap is at most $`1.4`$ points, suggesting that context refinement
already provides most of the observed coverage. However, GPQA-D also
uses multiple-choice answers and exhibits mixed selected-accuracy
effects, so answer format alone cannot account for the MMLU-Pro pattern.
The results motivate testing whether the quality of context-induced
distribution shifts, rather than output format, predicts when steering
helps. (iii) Candidate coverage and selection can improve at different
rates. On Qwen3-14B/GPQA-D, EMO reaches $`79.8`$ pass@5 against $`65.2`$
selected accuracy. The gap shows that correct candidates are available
for additional questions but are not always selected; by itself, it does
not measure semantic diversity. Rising coverage across rounds
establishes that later attempts solve previously uncovered questions,
motivating stronger verification to realize these gains (Section 4.5).

Llama-3.1-8B GPQA-D, MATH-500, and MMLU-Pro; $`+6.5`$, $`+7.6`$, and
$`+12.7`$ on Llama-3.2-3B) and Qwen3-8B/MMLU-Pro ($`+2.5`$). SC and BoN
trajectories need not be monotone: for example, Qwen3-8B/GPQA-D BoN
accuracy decreases from $`60.6`$ at $`N=8`$ to $`59.8`$ at $`N=10`$.
Likewise, EMO’s mean selected accuracy increases with each round in
eight of nine pairs; the exception is Llama-3.1-8B/GPQA-D, which
decreases from $`37.5`$ at $`k=3`$ to $`37.4`$ at $`k=4`$.

##### Oracle coverage.

At $`N=9`$, EMO’s five-candidate coverage exceeds the nine-sample SC/BoN
coverage in seven of nine pairs. The largest gap is on
Llama-3.1-8B/GPQA-D ($`73.7`$ vs. $`60.3`$); the two exceptions are
Qwen3-8B/GPQA-D ($`79.1`$ vs. $`80.0`$) and Qwen3-8B/MATH-500 ($`90.4`$
vs. $`90.9`$), where independent sampling retains an edge. Coverage can
continue to improve while selected accuracy changes little, as on
Llama-3.1-8B/GPQA-D, indicating that candidate availability and judge
selection place different limits on the final result.

##### How coverage translates into selected accuracy.

Because a correct selected response must belong to a pool containing a
correct response, selected accuracy divided by oracle coverage measures
the fraction of covered questions on which selection succeeds. We
compute this descriptive ratio from the reported three-seed means; with
equal evaluation-set sizes, it corresponds to pooling question–seed
outcomes, rather than averaging per-seed ratios. At $`N=9`$ on
Llama-3.1-8B/GPQA-D, the ratio is $`37.4/73.7\approx 50.7\%`$ for EMO
and $`32.2/60.3\approx 53.4\%`$ for BoN. EMO’s accuracy advantage
therefore coexists with a lower fraction of available successes realized
by selection: its greater coverage is essential to the observed
advantage. On Llama-3.1-8B/MATH-500, both coverage ($`73.4`$ vs.
$`71.9`$) and this fraction ($`89.4\%`$ vs. $`84.6\%`$) favor EMO. The
same pattern appears on Llama-3.2-3B/MATH-500 (Figure 2(b)): EMO’s
coverage is $`70.6`$ versus BoN’s $`66.5`$, and its selected-to-covered
fraction is $`60.3/70.6\approx 85.4\%`$ versus
$`52.7/66.5\approx 79.2\%`$. These are descriptive relationships, not
isolated causal effects of ranking: candidate pools and the questions
they cover differ across methods. They show why a larger absolute
oracle–accuracy gap need not indicate less useful generation, and why
selection quality alone cannot explain all accuracy gains.

##### Repeated sampling and feedback address different limitations.

At $`N=9`$ on Llama-3.1-8B/MMLU-Pro, SC and BoN share $`76.4`$ oracle
coverage but achieve $`51.4`$ and $`62.5`$ accuracy, respectively. Thus
many available correct responses are not recovered by majority voting,
while judge selection realizes more of the same pool’s potential. EMO
further raises coverage to $`83.2`$ and accuracy to $`68.2`$ with only
five candidates, showing additional value from changing how subsequent
attempts are generated. For Qwen3-8B/MATH-500, by contrast, SC is
strongest ($`86.1`$), and EMO has no coverage advantage over independent
sampling ($`90.4`$ vs. $`90.9`$). This contrast suggests that
feedback-guided adaptation is most useful where independent sampling and
its selection rule leave recoverable errors; it need not dominate when
repeated sampling already supplies effective candidates and reliable
consensus.

The M-step steering has two hyperparameters: the total strength $`\mu`$

##### Strength is insensitive.

Across a $`2\times`$ range of the total strength ($`\mu\in[1.6,3.2]`$)
the gain stays within $`5.6`$–$`7.6`$ points with no monotone trend:
$`\mu=1.6`$ and the default $`\mu=2.4`$ tie for the highest observed
accuracy ($`56.6`$), and no setting collapses. This indicates that the
KL-gradient signal (Section 3.2.2) is a robust direction—once the update
points toward the optimized context, its magnitude is of secondary
importance.

##### The confidence constraint is what matters.

The picture changes qualitatively for $`\alpha`$. At $`\alpha{=}0.05`$
the gain collapses to $`+1.0`$: with too permissive a mask, the steering
vector acts on tokens where the optimized context expresses almost no
preference, injecting noise rather than signal. At
$`\alpha{=}0.1`$–$`0.4`$ the gain is stable ($`+7.6`$ to $`+9.6`$, with
the best value at $`\alpha{=}0.2`$), i.e., the constraint only needs to
separate confident tokens from noise, not to be tuned precisely. We
conclude that the confidence mask, not the step size, is the
load-bearing hyperparameter of the M-step, and keep the conservative
default $`\alpha{=}0.1`$ throughout the main experiments (where it
already attains the $`+7.6`$ gain reported above).

### B.6 Prompt Templates

This section documents the four prompts that drive the E-step
(Section 3): the judge that scores responses, the backward engine that
produces the textual gradient, the optimizer that rewrites the context,
and the answer-leakage filter that guards the whole loop. All prompts
are task-agnostic (the same templates are used for all six benchmarks);
the question stem and option block are the only task-specific inputs.

#### B.6.1 Judge Scoring Prompt

The judge prompt (\_EVAL_SYSTEM_PROMPT in trainer.py) instructs the
judge model to grade a response on a 0–10 scale while remaining blind to
the gold answer. Its key requirements are: (i) solve the problem
_independently_ before grading, never assuming the model’s answer is
correct; (ii) 10 means the final answer is correct, 0 means wrong or
missing, with partial credit (1–9) for substantive progress on incorrect
solutions; (iii) the feedback must locate the methodological error and
how to fix it _without ever revealing or hinting at the final answer_
(no numbers, letters, or expressions); (iv) no restating of the question
or response.

> Question: {question}\n\nResponse: {response}\n\nEvaluate this
> response. Provide detailed feedback and a score.

The judge returns its output in the format
\<FEEDBACK\>...\</FEEDBACK\>\<SCORE\>0-10\</SCORE\>; the score drives
judge-select (Appendix B.3) and the feedback becomes the input to the
backward pass below.

#### B.6.2 Backward (Text Gradient) Prompt

The backward prompt (BACKWARD_SYSTEM_PROMPT in llm_backward_prompts.py)
asks the engine to act as the “gradient” with respect to the context
variable: given the current system prompt, the question, the model’s
response, and the judge feedback, produce _criticism of the
context_—strategies, explanations, and methods to change—but never a
rewritten version of the variable (that is the optimizer’s job). It
carries the same no-leakage and conciseness clauses as the judge prompt.
The gradient is injected through a structured conversation template:

> \<LM_SYSTEM_PROMPT\>{system_prompt}\</LM_SYSTEM_PROMPT\>  
> \<LM_INPUT\>{prompt}\</LM_INPUT\>  
> \<LM_OUTPUT\>{response}\</LM_OUTPUT\>  
> \<FEEDBACK\>{feedback}\</FEEDBACK\>

Note that the full response is embedded in the backward prompt; this is
the main driver of the judge-context length budget ($`{\sim}14.5`$k
tokens at peak for our longest responses).

#### B.6.3 Optimizer (Context Rewrite) Prompt

The optimizer prompt (OPTIMIZER_SYSTEM_PROMPT in optimizer_prompts.py)
implements the TGD update: improve the context variable using the
feedback, returning the improved variable wrapped in
{new_variable_start_tag} {improved variable} {new_variable_end_tag} tags
so it can be extracted and replace the old context directly, with an
instruction to keep the improved variable concise. The update
instruction passes the variable’s role, its current value, and the
gradient from the backward pass. What shapes the rewrite most is the
_role description_ attached to the context variable:

> _“system prompt to guide the model’s behavior. A good system prompt
> establishes the model as a domain expert relevant to the task (e.g.
> ‘You are an expert chemist …’), states the key reasoning approach and
> pitfalls to avoid, and specifies the required output format. Avoid
> generic phrases; be specific to the question’s domain. Do NOT reveal
> or hint at the actual answer.”_

This steers the E-step away from generic encouragements (“think
carefully”) toward substantive, domain-specific guidance, which is what
the M-step steering vector can then convert into a decoding-time
correction. Optional momentum (past iterations), constraints, and
in-context-example sections exist in the template but are disabled in
all experiments reported in this paper.

#### B.6.4 Answer-Leakage Filter

Two complementary guards keep the optimization signal free of the
answer. First, the judge never receives the gold answer, and its prompt
explicitly forbids the feedback from revealing or hinting at the final
answer (Appendix B.6.1). Second, every context produced by the optimizer
is passed through a regex filter (\_ANSWER_LEAK_PATTERNS in
optimizer.py) that rejects rewrites containing concrete answers—e.g.
“the answer is X” patterns, contrastive “the answer is X, not Y”
constructions, or specific numbers/letters—in which case the previous
context is retained. The v2 filter no longer false-positives on the
output-format directive Answer: \$LETTER\$: a literal \$ is only treated
as numeric leakage when followed by a digit. Together these guards
ensure that neither the judge feedback nor the optimized context can
smuggle the gold answer into the generation path, so any accuracy gain
is attributable to the optimization itself rather than to answer
leakage.
