###### Abstract.

Recent advancements have highlighted that large language models (LLMs),
when given a small set of task-specific examples, demonstrate remarkable
proficiency, a capability that extends to complex reasoning tasks. In
particular, the combination of few-shot learning with the
chain-of-thought (CoT) approach has been pivotal in steering models
towards more logically consistent conclusions (Wei et al., 2022b). This
paper explores the optimization of example selection for designing
effective CoT pre-prompts and shows that the choice of the optimization
algorithm, typically in favor of comparison-based methods such as
evolutionary computation, significantly enhances efficacy and
feasibility. Specifically, thanks to a limited exploitative and
overfitted optimization, Evolutionary Pre-Prompt Optimization (EPPO)
brings an improvement over the naive few-shot approach, exceeding 10
absolute points in exact match scores on benchmark datasets such as
GSM8k and MathQA. These gains are consistent across various contexts and
are further amplified when integrated with self-consistency (SC).

###### Keywords: 

Evolutionary algorithms, large language model, pre-prompt optimization,
mathematical reasoning

## 1. Introduction

Large language models (LLMs) (Brown et al., 2020; Hoffmann et al., 2022;
Touvron et al., 2023; Anil et al., 2023; Team et al., 2023) have emerged
as a transformative force, demonstrating exceptional capabilities across
a spectrum of tasks. However, despite their size and complexity, these
models still face challenges in multi-step reasoning, particularly in
tasks that require arithmetic, logic, and/or mathematical
reasoning (Cobbe et al., 2021; Rae et al., 2021).

To address this limitation, recent works have focused on enhancing the
reasoning abilities of LLMs. A significant advancement in this direction
is the chain-of-thought (CoT) prompting method (Wei et al., 2022b). This
approach involves guiding LLMs to articulate intermediate reasoning
steps in a manner akin to human thought processes, leading to more
accurate and interpretable solutions. This method has shown substantial
improvements on complex tasks, including mathematics and commonsense
reasoning (Wei et al., 2022b; Suzgun et al., 2022; Lu et al., 2022).

The advancement of the CoT prompting has opened new pathways in the
design of effective CoT prompts (Zhou et al., 2022; Jiang et al., 2023;
Fu et al., 2022; Kojima et al., 2022). A crucial aspect of this research
is the strategic use of complex examples in prompts for a solution. Fu
et al. (2022) demonstrate that using such intricate examples in few-shot
prompts could improve LLM performance in reasoning tasks, highlighting
the importance of not only the content but also the structure of the
prompts in enhancing the reasoning process. In the context of LLMs,
few-shot pre-prompting based on CoT consists of adding several examples,
typically made of several steps as in standard CoT, as a pre-prompt
before each prompt under consideration.

Another effective technique in CoT prompting is the inclusion of
directive phrases, such as “let us think step by step,” which leads to
more organized reasoning generation (Kojima et al., 2022). Overall,
these developments highlight the need to carefully select and structure
prompts, as they directly affect LLM efficiency in tackling complex
reasoning tasks.

In line with previous works, we investigate the effect of few-shot
prompting through automated example selection. However, we use these
examples as a pre-prompt for the whole downstream task at hand. Once
constructed, these few examples remain fixed for the given task (here,
mathematical modeling (Feigenbaum et al., 1963; Bundy, 1983)). This
differs from various in-context learning methods (see Section 6) in
which prompt selection depends on each specific instance. Furthermore,
since most reasoning benchmarks provide a training set that is too small
for proper training (Ni et al., 2023; Liu et al., 2023), we focus on
evolutionary algorithms, which can be comparison-based and therefore
only need a few bits of feedback, paving the way for
information-theoretic generalization guarantees. We analyze
mathematically the risk of overfitting or exploitative behavior of such
evolutionary approaches, and prove generalization bounds that do not
exist for other approaches accessing fine-grained data with
gradient-based methods.

Such an _Evolutionary Pre-Prompt Optimization_ strategy (termed EPPO in
the following) effectively identifies a concise set of examples (called
for short few-shot in the following) that notably enhance performance
when used together as a pre-prompt. Additionally, considering the
current limited understanding of few-shot mechanisms (Min et al., 2022),
the insights gained from the selected examples enable us to identify new
ways to improve the performance of large language models, not only
helping in their practical use for complex tasks, but also contributing
to a better understanding of the nuances of few-shot prompting at large.

#### Contributions.

The main contributions of this work are as follows:

- •
  We introduce Evolutionary Pre-Prompt Optimization (EPPO), a
  comparison-based evolutionary framework for optimizing fixed few-shot
  chain-of-thought (CoT) pre-prompts at the task level, framing prompt
  design as a principled combinatorial optimization problem.
- •
  We empirically demonstrate that controlled, exploitative evolutionary
  search reduces overfitting in low-data regimes while outperforming
  brute-force example selection and classical fine-tuning.
- •
  We demonstrate strong and robust empirical gains on mathematical
  reasoning benchmarks, achieving improvements exceeding 10 absolute
  exact-match points on GSM8k and MathQA, with performance that
  generalizes across decoding strategies and combines additively with
  self-consistency.
- •
  We show that optimized pre-prompts induce more consistent and
  structured reasoning traces, revealing that prompt quality rather than
  prompt length is the key factor in effective and interpretable CoT
  reasoning.
- •
  We enforce these results by an information-theoretic analysis of
  evolutionary few-shot optimization, deriving generalization bounds
  that characterize the effects of data size, population parallelism,
  and optimization budget, and that are unavailable for gradient-based
  fine-tuning methods.

## 2. Methods

This work focuses on optimizing the performance of a given LLM on a
given complex downstream task (here, mathematical reasoning) through
few-shot CoT pre-prompting.

### 2.1. Datasets

Our primary goal is to identify the most impactful small pre-prompt that
can significantly boost the LLM performance on the task at hand. We will
be looking for a pre-prompt composed of several prompts chosen from a
much larger set, the demonstration set. We want this set of selected
prompts, when used together as a pre-prompt for all further queries, to
improve the efficiency and effectiveness of the LLM on the task at hand.

Downstream tasks for LLMs (e.g., GSM8k, SVAMP, MathQA, among others,
more in Section 3) are usually defined by two sets of examples, an
original training set, and a test set, both these sets being of course
disjoint. The test set remains hidden throughout the EPPO process and is
used only to evaluate the final solution.

To summarize, we will be dealing with three datasets made of examples of
the task at hand, i.e., prompts and the corresponding expected answer
from the LLM: the demonstration set is used as the source of prompts to
be included in the pre-prompt; the _training set_ is used to compute the
objective function of the optimization algorithm (see Section 2.4); and
the _test set_, unseen during the optimization process, is used to
evaluate the best pre-prompt found by EPPO.

### 2.2. Few-shot optimization

For a given training set $`\mathcal{T}`$ and its corresponding
demonstration set $`\mathcal{D}`$ made of CoT prompt examples, or simply
CoT prompts (see Section 2.3), we aim to find out the best performing
small subset, referred to as few-shot pre-prompt in the following, or
simply pre-prompt. To do so, we formulate this problem as a
combinatorial optimization problem, as follows (see Figure 1):

- •
  Representation of CoT prompts: Each example (prompt + solution) in the
  demonstration set $`\mathcal{D}`$ is assigned a unique index in
  $`\{1,\dots,|\mathcal{D}|\}`$. This transforms the demonstration set
  into a lookup table. Instead of searching in $`\mathcal{D}^{s}`$,
  where $`s`$ is the size of the few-shot prompts (number of prompts) we
  are looking for, we search in $`\{1,2,\dots,|\mathcal{D}|\}^{s}`$.
  Note that redundancies are possible.
- •
  Construction of the few-shot pre-prompt: A few-shot pre-prompt is
  hence represented by a (small) list of $`s`$ integers (typically
  $`2\leq s\leq 16`$), each representing a CoT prompt from the
  demonstration dataset $`\mathcal{D}`$.
- •
  Optimization: The objective is to identify the combination of integers
  (i.e., of CoT prompts) that, when used together as pre-prompt,
  maximizes the performance of the LLM for the task at hand on the
  training dataset. This involves varying the integers of the list (and
  possibly the order of the prompts and the size of the list). This is
  amenable to black-box optimization (see Section 2.4). The optimization
  loop is represented by the black arrows in Figure 1.
- •
  Evaluation: the solution (a few-shot pre-prompt) returned by the
  optimization algorithm is then evaluated on the test set, not seen
  during the optimization. This step is represented by the red arrows in
  Figure 1.

### 2.3. Datasets construction

In the current setup, we work with original datasets that typically
contain several thousand examples. However, optimizing various few-shot
pre-prompts across the entire dataset is cost-prohibitive due to the
high cost of running LLMs. To address this challenge, we use a
subsampling strategy to create the demonstration set, from which the
optimized pre-prompt will be extracted. When datasets are categorized
with various difficulty levels (i.e., MATH), we ensure a balanced
approach by layered sub-sampling, i.e., uniformly sub-sampling across
all categories and levels. This method ensures a diverse set of examples
in the reduced dataset, which is crucial for comprehensive model
evaluation.  
In scenarios where the dataset lacks such detailed categorization, we
pivot to a strategy based on the LLM’s response uncertainty,
specifically when using LLaMA2-70B (Touvron et al., 2023). We generate
$`n`$ different answers for each example, using a predefined temperature
setting $`\tau`$ (by default $`\tau=0.8`$), that influences the
diversity of the model responses. To gauge confidence in an answer, we
analyze the frequency of the correct answer across these multiple
responses. The idea is that the more frequently a correct answer
appears, the higher the confidence of the model in that answer. Based on
this confidence measure, we perform uniform subsampling across all
levels of uncertainty. In practice, in our experiments, we take $`n=10`$
and take the same number of examples for each frequency of correct
answers (in each interval between $`\frac{0}{10}`$,
$`\frac{1}{10},\ldots\frac{9}{10},\frac{10}{10}`$). This method allows
us to select a representative subset of examples that captures a wide
range of the model certainty levels as a proxy for the example
difficulty.  
We apply the same strategy to construct the demonstration dataset from
the remaining examples of the original training set. To further increase
diversity, we additionally include correct demonstrations generated by
the model using the baseline CoT. Consequently, the demonstration
dataset $`\mathcal{D}`$ comprises both the usual hand-annotated
demonstrations (from GSM8k-train) and automatically generated ones,
thereby reducing the need for human intervention. The proportion of
generated demonstrations ranges from 0% for Math and MathQA to 50% for
GSM8k. Table 6 reports the sizes of all datasets involved.

### 2.4. Evolutionary optimization

The goal is to optimize an array of integers (the list of indices of
prompt/answer pairs from the demonstration set). This is amenable to a
classical black-box optimization setting, allowing us to use standard
optimization libraries such as Nevergrad (Rapin and Teytaud, 2018). The
simplest optimization algorithms for doing so consist of mutating one or
a few variables at a time. More sophisticated methods include selecting
the variables or the number of variables to modify. This last option is
frequent in recent works and included in Nevergrad algorithms such as
“LogNormal” (Kruisselbrink et al., 2011) and “Lengler” (Doerr et al.,
2019; Einarsson et al., 2019). In a nutshell (see e.g., (Rapin and
Teytaud, 2018) for details), the following optimization algorithms are
used in our experiments in Section 3, assuming we are optimizing an
array of size $`d`$:

- •
  The simple Discrete $`(1+1)`$-ES maintains a current solution and
  mutates each of its variables with probability $`1/d`$ (repeat if no
  variable is mutated): the mutated point is used as a new current
  solution if its objective value is not worse than that of the previous
  one.
- •
  The Portfolio method (Dang and Lehre, 2016) replaces $`p=1/d`$ with a
  uniform random choice of the mutation probability $`p`$ in $`[0,1]`$.
  “Portfolio” is the name used in the implementation in Nevergrad.
- •
  The DoubleFastGA method is an adaptation in Nevergrad of the Fast GA
  (Doerr et al., 2017) to non-binary discrete variables.
- •
  In the Lengler method, the mutation probability $`p`$ decreases over
  time, with a schedule that is mathematically derived for optimal
  performance on artificial test functions (Einarsson et al., 2019).
  Variants include variations of the critical hyperparameters. We refer
  to (Rapin and Teytaud, 2018) for details.
- •
  The LogNormal method (Kruisselbrink et al., 2011) uses a
  self-adaptation mechanism that mutates the mutation probability $`p`$
  using a log-normal mutation before mutating the variables.
- •
  Some variants of these algorithms that use a crossover operator are
  also tried – their name contains “Recombining” or “Crossover”. The
  different crossover operators include

  (a) one-point crossover, (b) two-point crossover, and (c) uniform
  crossover. We do not check for duplicates in the list after the
  crossover, which is unlikely to occur given the large size of the
  demonstration set $`\mathcal{D}`$ relative to the few-shot size $`s`$.

- •
  We also compare EPPO with random search, in which all variables are
  repeatedly and uniformly sampled from their domains, retaining, in the
  end, the best-encountered solution.

The discrete algorithms discussed above are generally presented in the
context of binary variables, but adapting them to categorical variables
is straightforward: the mutation operator replaces the current value
with a randomly drawn value from the variable’s domain (excluding the
current value).

### 2.5. Evolutionary Pre-Prompt Optimization

2: State-transition functions
$`\mathrm{Init},\mathrm{Ask},\mathrm{Tell},\mathrm{Recommend}`$

3: Comparison procedure $`\mathrm{Compare}`$

4: $`(x_{0},\kappa)\leftarrow\mathrm{Init}(\omega,b,\dots)`$
$`\triangleright`$ $`x_{s}`$: optimizer state; $`\kappa`$: number of
candidates per iteration

5: $`A_{0}\leftarrow\emptyset`$ $`\triangleright`$ Archive of evaluated
pre-prompts

6: for $`s=1,\dots,b`$ do

7:   $`(P_{s},x^{\mathrm{ask}}_{s})\leftarrow\mathrm{Ask}(x_{s-1})`$
$`\triangleright`$ $`P_{s}=(p_{s,1},\dots,p_{s,\kappa})`$

8:   $`A_{s}\leftarrow A_{s-1}\cup\{p_{s,1},\dots,p_{s,\kappa}\}`$

9:   $`i_{s}\leftarrow\mathrm{Compare}(P_{s})`$ $`\triangleright`$
$`i_{s}\in\{1,\dots,\kappa\}`$ is the index of the best candidate

10:   $`x_{s}\leftarrow\mathrm{Tell}(x^{\mathrm{ask}}_{s},i_{s},P_{s})`$

11: end for

12: $`r\leftarrow\mathrm{Recommend}(x_{b},A_{b})`$

13: return $`r`$

Algorithm 1 Pseudo-code for EPPO.

A global overview of the EPPO algorithm is given in Figure 1, and its
pseudo-code is shown in Algorithm 1. The algorithm relies on a
comparison-based combinatorial optimization method. Such optimization
methods are often described using the so-called ask-and-tell paradigm.
In this paradigm, the optimizer maintains an internal state and
interacts with the evaluation process through two abstract operations.
First, an ask step proposes one or more candidate solutions to be
evaluated solely on the basis of the current internal state. Second, a
tell step updates this internal state using feedback obtained from
evaluating these candidates. Crucially, the optimizer never directly
observes the evaluation process itself, but only the information
returned by the tell step, which may be partial or noisy. This
separation allows the same optimization logic to be reused with
different evaluation mechanisms.

In EPPO, we adopt a comparison-based variant of this paradigm: at each
iteration, the ask step proposes $`\kappa`$ candidate pre-prompts; these
candidates are evaluated by running the LLM, and the tell step receives
only the index of the best-performing candidate, rather than its
absolute performance value.

The internal state of the optimizer at iteration $`s`$ is denoted by
$`x_{s}\in\mathcal{X}`$. The algorithm is initialized by a function

```math
(x_{0},\kappa)=\mathrm{Init}(\omega,b,\dots),
```

where $`\omega`$ is a random seed, $`b`$ is the evaluation budget, and
$`\kappa`$ is the number of candidate pre-prompts proposed and compared
at each iteration. Additional problem-specific information (e.g., number
of variables, domains, or constraints) may also be passed to
$`\mathrm{Init}`$.

An explicit archive $`A_{s}\subseteq\mathcal{P}`$ of all visited
pre-prompts is maintained. Initially, $`A_{0}=\emptyset`$. At each
iteration $`s=1,\dots,b`$, the optimizer proposes a $`\kappa`$-tuple of
candidate pre-prompts

```math
P_{s}=(p_{s,1},\dots,p_{s,\kappa})
```

via the function $`\mathrm{Ask}`$, which also returns an intermediate
optimizer state. All proposed candidates are added to the archive and
then evaluated and compared.

The comparison procedure

```math
i_{s}=\mathrm{Compare}(P_{s})
```

returns the index $`i_{s}\in\{1,\dots,\kappa\}`$ of the best-performing
pre-prompt among the $`\kappa`$ candidates. More precisely,
$`\mathrm{Compare}`$ first evaluates each pre-prompt $`p_{s,j}`$ by
running the LLM on the training set using $`p_{s,j}`$ (as illustrated in
the LLM block of Figure 1), computes a scalar performance measure for
each candidate, and finally returns the index of the candidate with the
highest estimated performance. The optimizer does not observe the
numerical scores; the comparison result $`i_{s}`$ is the only feedback
signal.

The optimizer state is then updated according to

```math
x_{s}=\mathrm{Tell}(x^{\mathrm{ask}}_{s},i_{s},P_{s}),
```

which emphasizes that the update depends solely on the outcome of the
comparison, not on absolute performance values.

After $`b`$ iterations, the algorithm returns a recommended pre-prompt

```math
r=\mathrm{Recommend}(x_{b},A_{b}),
```

typically chosen as the best-performing element of the archive. This
final pre-prompt is then evaluated on the test set (red arrows and text
in Figure 1).

The explicit parameter $`\kappa`$ departs from the classical
ask-and-tell formalism but naturally captures most evolutionary
algorithms. For example, the $`(1+\lambda)`$-ES corresponds to
$`\kappa=1+\lambda`$, while the $`(1,\lambda)`$-ES corresponds to
$`\kappa=\lambda`$. Typically, EPPO can be seen as a repeated A/B
pre-prompt testing scenario for an online LLM, but without ensuring that
the same questions are sent to both alternatives, nor that we have
access to a detailed ground-truth answer for each question.

### 2.6. Generalization risk under limited feedback.

EPPO selects a pre-prompt through an iterative procedure that relies on
coarse feedback (e.g., $`\kappa`$-ary comparisons aggregated over the
full evaluation dataset), rather than on fine-grained gradient
information. As a result, the returned pre-prompt is chosen from a
finite set of candidates whose size depends only on the optimization
budget.

Assume that for any fixed pre-prompt $`p`$ and any $`\varepsilon>0`$,
the probability that the empirical loss deviates from its expected loss
by more than $`\varepsilon`$ is bounded by $`\delta_{1,\varepsilon}`$.
After $`b`$ optimization iterations with feedback arity $`\kappa`$, the
number of pre-prompts that can possibly be returned by EPPO is at most
$`\kappa^{b}`$. A union-bound argument then yields

```math
\Pr\!\left(\big|\widehat{L}(r)-L(r)\big|>\varepsilon\right)\;\leq\;\kappa^{b}\,\delta_{1,\varepsilon}, \tag{1}
```

where $`r`$ denotes the pre-prompt returned by EPPO.

This bound isolates the _selection-induced_ generalization risk of the
procedure: it depends only on the feedback granularity and the
optimization budget, and not on the size of the ambient pre-prompt space
or on the internal complexity of the underlying language model. Detailed
statements with their proofs, as well as examples of such situations,
are provided in Appendix A.

### 3.1. Experimental settings

Datasets: EPPO is evaluated on four mathematical reasoning tasks. Each
task comes from a different dataset, and they have heterogeneous
complexity. The datasets used here are GSM8k (Cobbe et al., 2021),
SVAMP (Patel et al., 2021), MathQA (Amini et al., 2019), and
MATH (Hendrycks et al., 2021). GSM8k and SVAMP focus on real-world
mathematical problems. While GSM8k offers a comprehensive training set,
SVAMP does not have a corresponding training set. To tackle this issue,
we will test the transferability of EPPO by evaluating on SVAMP the same
few-shot pre-prompts found for GSM8k. MathQA offers a wide range of
mathematical problems of varying difficulties. In contrast, MATH poses
the greatest challenge, targeting advanced mathematics typically
encountered in late high school and beyond. Also, notice that MathQA has
very noisy annotations and, hence, provides a test for the resilience of
optimization strategies. For an in-depth understanding of each of these
datasets, including specifics about their Training, Demonstration, and
Test components, please refer to Table 6 in Appendix B.

Evaluation metrics: For each dataset, our primary metric is the exact
match (EM) with the ground truth. Specifically, we focus on the final
output generated by the model, which is typically a numerical value, and
we compare it directly with the ground truth. For the MathQA dataset,
which consists of multiple-choice questions, we use two distinct EM
scores. One for the numerical answer and another for the correct
multiple-choice option (identified simply by its letter). This
dual-scoring approach is particularly beneficial for evaluating
smaller/less capable models that do not effectively correlate with the
numerical answer.

P-values: One run of EPPO results in multiple evaluation loops (see
Algorithm 1). Thus, despite the low cost of EPPO compared to a full
backpropagation training procedure, such repeated experiments are still
costly, and in some (though not all) of our experimental contexts, a
rather small number of independent runs of EPPO itself could be
performed, making comparative statistically significant tests impossible
for unique contexts. Nevertheless, when considering several contexts
simultaneously, it becomes possible to test the statistical relevance of
our findings by computing the p-values through a binomial test (Fisher’s
exact test), with the null hypothesis being “the model improved by EPPO
does not outperform the baseline on a given context with probability
$`\geq\frac{1}{2}`$”. We refer to this measure simply by “p-value” in
the following, in the absence of any ambiguity.

Language models: We use the LLaMA family of models (i.e.,
LLaMA2 (Touvron et al., 2023)). A key advantage of these models is their
open-source nature, which significantly supports cost-effective,
reproducible research.

Baselines: The baselines used for comparison are:

(a) the original CoT prompt (Wei et al., 2022b), as detailed in the
publication and sourced from their appendix; (b) the Long CoT prompt (Fu
et al., 2022) that includes examples with more steps, targeting complex
reasoning tasks. These prompts were obtained from the official GitHub
repository; and (c) the Resprompt approach (Jiang et al., 2023) that
suggests following a reasoning graph and adding residual connections
between the nodes of the graph. This technique is designed to reduce
errors in multi-step reasoning tasks by LLMs. We sourced the Resprompt
prompts from the paper’s appendix.

We do not include in-context learning such as (SU et al., 2023; Wu et
al., 2022; Zhang et al., 2023a; Gupta et al., 2023; Ye et al., 2023;
Rubin et al., 2022), which optimize a pre-prompt on a per-example basis.
Likewise, they are usually tested on heterogeneous and older models
without considering mathematical reasoning tasks. Moreover, their source
codes are barely available.

For MATH tasks, which are not covered by any of the baselines, we took
the base CoT prompt from Minerva (Lewkowycz et al., 2022). For the Long
CoT, we created prompts using the most detailed and complex answers from
the training set. All these baseline prompts are 8-shot for GSM8k and
SVAMP, 4-shot for MathQA and MATH. To ensure a fair comparison, we run
all these baseline prompts in our own codebase. Our scores are
consistent with the original papers, except for ResPrompt, which yields
worse results on MathQA.

Hyperparameters: For each task, except for SVAMP, which lacks a training
set, we create a smaller training set containing roughly 500 samples by
downsampling the original one, and assemble a Demonstration dataset of
about 1000 samples, as described in Section 2.3. Precise details about
each dataset can be found in Table 6 in the Appendix. In Algorithm 1,
the optimization algorithm $`Optimiser`$ is chosen among those described
in Section 2.4, in their Nevergrad implementation (Rapin and Teytaud,
2018); The budget $`b`$ is set to 100 for both the LLaMA2-7B and
LLaMA2-70B models; The number $`s`$ of prompts from the demonstration
set $`\mathcal{D}`$ is taken in $`\{2,4,8,12,16\}`$, and such
pre-prompts are denoted for short “$`s`$-shot pre-prompts”.

the optimizer logs of the train error along optimization. Our findings
are illustrated in Figure 2, showing statistics gathered over the full

and consistent performance gap between the 2-shot prompts and those with

### 3.5. Combining with Self-Consistency

In the context of problem-solving with LLMs, multiple pathways often
lead to a solution. Recognizing this, Wang et al. (2022) introduced the
concept of self-consistency (SC) for LLMs. SC operates by generating a
range of potential solutions with the same LLM. Diverse prompts for the
problem at hand are generated by adding random parts and controlling the
level of randomness via a specific parameter, $`\tau`$, called the
temperature. Each of these generated pathways leads to diverse answers
that are then aggregated by a majority voting (Wang et al., 2022),
effectively using the collective output of the model to determine the
most likely correct answer.

We now examine the interaction between self-consistency, known to
improve LLM performance, and pre-prompt optimization. The aim is to
determine whether the enhancements observed in single-path decoding also
hold in a self-consistency framework. Indeed, results presented
in Table 4 show a clear benefit transferred from greedy decoding to
self-consistency, at least with a temperature of 0.6 (a robust,
reasonable value, though sensitivity analysis remains to be done).

What constitutes a good pre-prompt? We observe that chosen examples (see
“Best Few-Shot Prompt” in the appendix) frequently include:

(a) Long, multi-step examples with long and intricate questions. This is
consistent with some classical heuristics, such as Long CoT (Fu et al.,
2022). (b) Examples which link previous steps, especially the
quantities, and start by stating each element of the question very
literally. (c) Examples which are effective while being buggy in terms
of justification. We observe that the style of answer matters more than
its correctness. Examples of effective incorrect rationales can be found
in the MATH task few-shot.

However, these heuristics appear to be less effective as the difficulty
of the task increases, as for MATH.

## 5. Discussion

Computational cost. To show the effectiveness of EPPO and explore
further the space of examples selection in few-shot pre-prompting, we
use a relatively large Demonstration dataset of around 1000 samples.
Still, the computational cost of EPPO is reasonable (on the downsampled
GSM8k, $`\approx`$3h of 64 V100 GPU for LLaMA2-70B, less than one hour
for LLaMA2-7B with float16 precision and a budget of 50) compared to
that of training LLaMA2. Our method is tractable, in particular thanks
to a well-chosen optimization method. Moreover, it has a much smaller
memory footprint than approaches based on backprop (i.e., fine-tuning),
as there is no backward pass involved. Moreover, a few-shot pre-prompt
provided by humans can be used as an initial point for the optimization
algorithm.

Required data. EPPO also requires less data than fine-tuning: For
instance, GSM8k lacks sufficient data to do a classical fine-tuning (Ni
et al., 2023; Liu et al., 2023) even for moderate sized LLMs
($`\approx`$1B). Also, EPPO is less likely to overfit on a specific
task, as indicated by the information-theoretic generalization bounds
(Section A.2), independent of model size: we get positive results even
with a 70B model. Fine-grained token-level outputs are unnecessary here
and, indeed, increase the risk of overfitting. For instance, with GSM8K,
only the final result is used, unlike in fine-tuning methods, where the
detailed reasoning process is needed.

Versatility. We have demonstrated the efficiency of EPPO on multiple
datasets with various levels of difficulty and Demonstration datasets
setup. In particular, for MathQA, the demonstration dataset is noisy,
often containing false, poorly explained, or incomplete rationales. This
particular case demonstrates the robustness of EPPO, which nevertheless
found effective few-shot preprompts.

Finding the data for our approach is feasible: When using a dataset as
ground truth, our sources are the training data and the answers by
LLaMA2 itself, so human intervention is limited. Also, our approach
could be entirely integrated into an automatic A/B testing scenario.
Last, EPPO works when heuristic methods used by humans fail.

Combination with other methods. Finally, our improvement can be combined
with Self Consistency (Wang et al., 2022), and the improvements are
additive: The simple and cheap EPPO approach could also be added on top
of other methods such as fine-tuning using augmented data by the LLM (Yu
et al., 2023; Xu et al., 2023; Pang et al., 2024), bootstrapping from
output of the LLM (Bai et al., 2022; Huang et al., 2023; Pang et al.,
2024).

## 6. Related Works

In-context learning has emerged as a way to adapt LLMs to a task just
from a few-shot examples. This method is particularly effective as the
scale of the models increases. Essentially, it allows LLMs to adjust to
new tasks without modifying their underlying parameters (Brown et al.,
2020; Rae et al., 2021; Hoffmann et al., 2022)). As LLMs continue to
grow, they show improved performance across a range of tasks and exhibit
the development of new skills, a phenomenon referred to as “emergent
ability” (Wei et al., 2022a). At the forefront of this area is the
chain-of-thoughts (CoT) reasoning approach (Wei et al., 2022b). As
opposed to some forms of in-context learning, CoT modifies the prompt
statically for a specific downstream task (e.g., mathematical reasoning)
instead of on a per-example basis: Our work fits this “per task”
framework and differs from “per-example” works such as (SU et al., 2023;
Wu et al., 2022; Zhang et al., 2023a; Gupta et al., 2023; Ye et al.,
2023; Rubin et al., 2022; Fernando et al., 2024). This method, which
prompts LLMs to articulate intermediate reasoning steps, has been
refined through different techniques (Wang et al., 2022; Fu et al.,
2022; Lewkowycz et al., 2022; Jiang et al., 2023). Despite its
effectiveness, CoT prompting often faces challenges with complex
multi-step tasks (Fu et al., 2022; Zhou et al., 2022; Jiang et al.,
2023). Various approaches have been explored to handle this challenge.
For instance, Wang et al. (2022) introduced self-consistency, which
involves aggregating LLMs’ reasoning across multiple reasoning paths.
Zhou et al. (2022) proposed a “least to most” prompting strategy, which
breaks down problems into sub-problems and solves them sequentially.
Jiang et al. (2023) developed Residual prompting, which follows a
reasoning graph and incorporates residual connections into the prompt
design. Finally, (Gao et al., 2023) introduced PAL, a method that
prompts LLMs to generate programs that solve the problem, with the
solution obtained by executing these programs.  
There are various methods for selecting examples for the prompts. This
task can be very challenging due to the instability. Multiple works have
shown that the performance is sensitive to prompt, task, dataset, and
model changes (Lu et al., 2021; Zhao et al., 2021). This sensitivity
contributes to the complexity of prompt engineering, making it a
somewhat arduous task. In particular, prompt engineering for LLMs often
relies on community-wide collective trial-and-error efforts (there is
even a prompt marketplace named PromptBase). Despite these challenges, a
line of work addresses this issue by introducing retrieval into few-shot
learning (Zhang et al., 2023b; Wu et al., 2023; Liu et al., 2021). In
this context, each few-shot examples are retrieved dynamically at test
time for each presented example, meaning that a particular CoT is
retrieved following some similarity measure on the test example and the
retrieved few-shot CoT. Our work, however, shows that a single retrieval
for the task is effective, robust, and transferable.

EPPO focuses on pre-prompt optimization by example prompt selection for
a given task and on multi-step reasoning, searching in large datasets to
demonstrate diverse ways of answering the questions. Orthogonal to this
approach, several methods (Yu et al., 2023; Xu et al., 2023; Pang et
al., 2024; Sessa et al., 2024) involve sampling multiple generations
from the LLM and using bootstrapping techniques to iteratively refine
and improve the model by updating its weights. These approaches are
complementary to EPPO, and they could enhance one another. Specifically,
EPPO could support the sampling phase by incorporating the few-shot
examples it identifies, creating a more robust population of generations
to bootstrap and potentially guiding the model toward even
higher-quality outputs. Complementary to EPPO  Promptbreeder (Fernando
et al., 2024) leverages LLMs to evolve the structure and the content of
the prompts, whereas EPPO optimizes the selection of static few-shot
examples.

## 7. Conclusion

This work focused on a key component of LLMs, namely, few-shot
optimization. We showed that we can outperform brute-force optimization
of examples in spite of not working at a per-example level, thanks to
using relevant combinatorial optimization methods. Limiting the
generalization error is crucial for obtaining such results. Our
mathematical results based on information theory predict a
generalization error that

(i) decreases as the volume of data increases (unsurprisingly, but this
is not so clear in the case in case of training on fine grained
data/with next-token prediction); (ii) decreases when using more
parallel evolutionary methods (consistently with (Videau et al., 2024))
or random search, and (iii) increases with the budget (consistently with
early stopping methods). For typical orders of magnitude, consistent
with (Videau et al., 2024; Liu et al., 2023; Zheng et al., 2024), we
obtain better generalization bounds, i.e., reduced exploitative behavior
and reduced overfitting, when using our method compared to classical
fine-tuning.

We experimentally verify each of these predictions. Additionally, our
results are robust across different contexts, such as greedy decoding
LLMs versus voting mechanisms: the benefits of voting and those of
few-shot optimization effectively add up. We observe a positive transfer
to various contexts.

This cannot be reproduced by simply adding many random shots. In the
optimized case, we observe a decrease in performance as we add shots,
and the best prompts are not necessarily the longest. Also, a detailed
analysis shows that few-shot optimization few-shot optimization leads to
a more consistent generation of reasoning steps across examples.

## Appendix A Generalization risk bounds under limited feedback

This appendix provides a simple, _model-agnostic_ bound on the
probability that the empirical evaluation of the pre-prompt returned by
EPPO deviates from its expected performance. Our analysis does _not_
rely on gradient-based learning theory, nor on capacity measures of the
underlying LLM; instead, it isolates the _selection effect_ induced by
comparing multiple candidates. In particular, the bound depends only on

(i) the optimizer feedback arity $`\kappa`$ and (ii) the number of
optimization iterations $`b`$, through the number of candidates that can
be selected.

### A.1. Deviation event and base concentration assumption

Setup. Let $`\mathcal{D}`$ denote the demonstration pool and let a
_pre-prompt_ $`p`$ be an element of $`\mathcal{D}^{s}`$ (typically, an
array of $`s`$ indices pointing to demonstrations). Let $`L(p)`$ denote
the (unknown) expected loss of the LLM equipped with pre-prompt $`p`$ on
the population distribution, and let $`\widehat{L}_{\mathcal{T}}(p)`$
denote its empirical loss on the evaluation set $`\mathcal{T}`$ (in our
implementation, the full dataset used by the compare step to score or
compare candidates).

For $`\varepsilon>0`$, define the deviation event

```math
err_{\varepsilon}(p)\;=\;\Big\{\big|L(p)-\widehat{L}_{\mathcal{T}}(p)\big|>\varepsilon\Big\}. \tag{2}
```

Hypothesis H1 (single-candidate deviation bound). We assume that for any
fixed pre-prompt $`p`$ and any $`\varepsilon>0`$ there exists a quantity
$`\delta_{1,\varepsilon}\in(0,1]`$ such that

```math
\Pr\!\big(err_{\varepsilon}(p)\big)\;\leq\;\delta_{1,\varepsilon}. \tag{3}
```

This is a standard concentration assumption: it bounds the probability
that the empirical loss of a _fixed_ candidate deviates from its
expectation by more than $`\varepsilon`$.

Example (bounded losses, Hoeffding). Assume
$`\widehat{L}_{\mathcal{T}}(p)`$ is an average of $`T`$ independent
terms taking values in $`[0,1]`$. Then Hoeffding’s inequality yields

```math
\Pr\!\big(err_{\varepsilon}(p)\big)\;\leq\;2\exp(-2T\varepsilon^{2}), \tag{4}
```

so Hypothesis H1 holds with
$`\delta_{1,\varepsilon}=2\exp(-2T\varepsilon^{2})`$.

### A.2. Selection over candidates: union-bound control

The difficulty is that the pre-prompt returned by EPPO is
_data-dependent_: it is chosen after observing comparison feedback. A
direct application of Equation 3 is therefore not immediate. We control
this selection effect using the classical union bound.

###### Lemma A.1 (Union bound for selection over a finite candidate set).

Assume Hypothesis H1 holds. Let $`\mathcal{R}=\{r_{1},\dots,r_{M}\}`$ be
a finite set of pre-prompts and let $`r`$ be any (possibly randomized,
data-dependent) element taking values in $`\mathcal{R}`$. Then for any
$`\varepsilon>0`$,

```math
\Pr\!\big(err_{\varepsilon}(r)\big)\;\leq\;M\,\delta_{1,\varepsilon}. \tag{5}
```

###### Proof.

We write

```math
\Pr\!\big(err_{\varepsilon}(r)\big)\;\leq\;\Pr\!\Big(\exists\,c\in\mathcal{R}:\;err_{\varepsilon}(c)\Big)\;\leq\;\sum_{c\in\mathcal{R}}\Pr\!\big(err_{\varepsilon}(c)\big)\;\leq\;M\,\delta_{1,\varepsilon},
```

where the second inequality is the union bound, and the last inequality
uses Hypothesis H1. ∎

### A.3. Application to EPPO: bounds for the returned pre-prompt and the archive

We now instantiate Lemma A.1 for EPPO. Recall that EPPO runs for $`b`$
iterations. At each iteration, the routine Compare returns an index in
$`\{1,\dots,\kappa\}`$, i.e., the feedback is $`\kappa`$-ary.¹¹ 1 For
$`\kappa=2`$, the feedback is a single comparison bit. The algorithm
maintains an archive (archive) collecting the evaluated candidates and
returns one pre-prompt $`r`$ at the end.

###### Theorem A.2 (Generalization risk bounds for EPPO under limited feedback).

Assume Hypothesis H1 holds. Consider one run of Algorithm 1 with
feedback arity $`\kappa`$ and budget $`b`$, and let $`r`$ be the
returned pre-prompt (13) and archive be the set of candidates stored
along the run (8). Then for any $`\varepsilon>0`$,

```math
\displaystyle\Pr\!\big(err_{\varepsilon}(r)\big) \displaystyle\;\leq\;\delta_{\varepsilon}^{EPPO}\;\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{def}}}{{=}}\;\kappa^{b}\,\delta_{1,\varepsilon}, \tag{6}
```

```math
\displaystyle\Pr\!\Big(\exists\,p\in\text{Archive}:\;err_{\varepsilon}(p)\Big) \displaystyle\;\leq\;\delta_{\varepsilon}^{\mathrm{unif}}\;\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{def}}}{{=}}\;\Big(\kappa+\kappa^{2}+\cdots+\kappa^{b}\Big)\delta_{1,\varepsilon}\;=\;\frac{\kappa^{b+1}-\kappa}{\kappa-1}\,\delta_{1,\varepsilon}. \tag{7}
```

###### Proof.

Step 1: bounding the number of possible outputs. Fix all deterministic
inputs to Algorithm 1 (training/evaluation sets, the optimizer
implementation, and the budget $`b`$). If the algorithm is randomized,
additionally fix its random seed $`\omega`$. At each of the $`b`$
iterations, the compare step returns one outcome among $`\kappa`$
possibilities. Therefore, the entire run induces a sequence of
$`\kappa`$-ary feedback of length $`b`$, hence at most $`\kappa^{b}`$
distinct feedback transcripts. For each transcript, the run is
deterministic given $`\omega`$ and thus determines a unique returned
pre-prompt. Consequently, for fixed $`\omega`$, the returned pre-prompt
must belong to a set $`\mathcal{R}_{\omega}`$ of size at
most $`\kappa^{b}`$.

Step 2: applying Lemma A.1 (fixed seed). Conditioned on $`\omega`$, we
have $`r\in\mathcal{R}_{\omega}`$ with
$`|\mathcal{R}_{\omega}|\leq\kappa^{b}`$. Applying Lemma A.1 with
$`M=\kappa^{b}`$ yields

```math
\Pr\!\big(err_{\varepsilon}(r)\mid\omega\big)\;\leq\;\kappa^{b}\,\delta_{1,\varepsilon}.
```

Step 3: removing the conditioning (randomized runs). If $`\omega`$ is
random, taking expectations over $`\omega`$ gives

```math
\Pr\!\big(err_{\varepsilon}(r)\big)=\mathbb{E}_{\omega}\!\left[\Pr\!\big(err_{\varepsilon}(r)\mid\omega\big)\right]\;\leq\;\kappa^{b}\,\delta_{1,\varepsilon},
```

which proves Equation 6.

Step 4: uniform bound over the archive. Across the run, at iteration
$`t\in\{1,\dots,b\}`$, at most $`\kappa^{t}`$ distinct candidates can be
_considered_ as a function of the first $`t`$ feedback outcomes (again
fixing $`\omega`$). Summing over iterations yields at most
$`\kappa+\kappa^{2}+\cdots+\kappa^{b}`$ candidates overall. Applying
Lemma A.1 to the set of all candidates that can enter the archive
gives (7). ∎

Interpretation (selection effect). The bounds in Theorem A.2 depend on
the number of candidates that the procedure can effectively compare and
select, rather than on the size of the ambient pre-prompt space
$`|\mathcal{D}|^{s}`$. This isolates the _selection-induced_
generalization risk: as the budget $`b`$ increases, more candidates can
be adaptively explored, thereby increasing the probability that at least
one candidate appears spuriously good on the evaluation set.

### A.4. Instantiating H1 and scaling with dataset size

When Hypothesis H1 is instantiated via Hoeffding’s
inequality Equation 10, Theorem A.2 implies

```math
\delta^{EPPO}_{\varepsilon}\;\leq\;2\,\kappa^{b}\exp(-2T\varepsilon^{2}), \tag{8}
```

and equivalently, for a target risk level
$`\delta^{EPPO}_{\varepsilon}\in(0,1)`$,

```math
\varepsilon\;\leq\;\sqrt{\frac{\ln\!\big(\tfrac{2\kappa^{b}}{\delta^{EPPO}_{\varepsilon}}\big)}{2T}}. \tag{9}
```

These expressions quantify the trade-off between the evaluation set
size $`T`$ (which tightens $`\delta_{1,\varepsilon}`$) and the
optimization budget $`b`$ (which enlarges the effective candidate set
via selection).

### A.5. Discussion: generalization bounds vs. optimization accuracy

A small upper bound on the deviation probability does not imply that the
method finds _high-performing_ candidates; it only controls the risk
that empirical performance is overly optimistic. For instance, consider
a form of random search that samples candidates independently and
selects the best among $`b`$ evaluated pre-prompts. In that case, the
selection set has size $`b`$, yielding

```math
\Pr\!\big(err_{\varepsilon}(r)\big)\;\leq\;b\,\delta_{1,\varepsilon}, \tag{10}
```

which can be smaller than the $`\kappa^{b}\,\delta_{1,\varepsilon}`$
bound for evolutionary selection. However, random search is typically
far less effective at identifying empirically strong pre-prompts. This
highlights a basic tension between

(i) exploring many candidates to improve empirical performance and (ii)
controlling selection-induced generalization risk.

A natural direction is to study intermediate strategies that increase
population-level diversity (larger $`\kappa`$ with fewer iterations,
early stopping, or stronger validation protocols) to balance these
objectives.
