###### Abstract.

Large language models (LLMs) increasingly shape recommendations across
chatbots, e-commerce, and user-generated content (UGC) platforms,
creating demand for Generative Engine Optimization (GEO) to improve item
visibility in generated responses. Existing GEO studies are mainly
content-centric, optimizing visibility under general-purpose queries.
However, a shared item description usually serves users with different
needs and decision goals, resulting in its visibility gains being
dependent on the request. To investigate how changing intent affects
visibility, we formulate IntentGEO to evaluate shared rewrites across
need specificity and decision progression. We revisit existing GEO
methods and find that their visibility gains vary across user intents.
More critically, learning-based prompt optimization can underperform its
handwritten seed on average. Tracing these updates reveals prompt drift
from explicit recommendation instructions toward generic promotional
tactics. To address this drift, we propose AMO (_Anchored
Meta-Optimization_), which preserves recommendation instructions in a
fixed core while learning appended strategies from citation feedback.
Across four domains and ten intent states, AMO improves average
citation-rank gain by 35% over the strongest prior learning-based
baseline and leads on nine states. Ablations show that learned additions
improve on the fixed core alone and that AMO outperforms full-prompt
optimization initialized from that core. It also achieves the highest
gains in an agentic recommendation pipeline and, after separate
adaptation, on Doubao, a commercially deployed, closed-source online
assistant. These findings highlight user intent as a key dimension of
GEO evaluation and support anchored adaptation across recommendation
needs and decision stages.

## 1. Introduction

Recent advances in large language models (LLMs) and AI chatbots such as
ChatGPT are reshaping how users seek information on the Web, shifting
search behavior from keyword-based retrieval to intent-driven questions
answered by LLMs. For example, in 2024 Gartner projected that
traditional search engine volume would decrease by 25% by 2026 as users
migrate to AI chatbots (Gartner, 2024). This shift is also changing
search and recommendation interfaces across search engines, e-commerce
platforms, and user-generated content (UGC) platforms, from ranked lists
to direct answers generated by integrated generative engines (Stein,
2025; Amazon Staff, 2026; Reuters, 2026). As generated responses become
a new source of organic traffic, brand owners increasingly need ways to
improve their visibility in these answers.

Generative Engine Optimization (GEO) studies how to rewrite item content
so that generative engines are more likely to use it when answering
relevant user queries (Aggarwal et al., 2024). Commonly, such rewriting
is evaluated within a fixed candidate document pool by measuring changes
in source visibility, modeling how an LLM selects and presents retrieved
content in its generated answer (Aggarwal et al., 2024; Puerto et al.,
2025; Pfrommer et al., 2024). Existing methods pursue this objective
through content-centric improvements in fluency and authority (Puerto et
al., 2025), black-box optimization (Nestaas et al., 2025), and learned
rewriting strategies (Bagga et al., 2025; Wu et al., 2026).

In recommendation, however, users seeking the same type of item may ask
very different questions, depending on their product requirements and
stage in the decision process (Hirsch et al., 2020; Chen et al., 2021;
Lemon and Verhoef, 2016). For example, a user’s need may range from a
broad category-level request, such as “best mobile phone”, to a specific
constraint-driven inquiry, such as “mobile phone with more than 16GB
RAM”. At the same time, the user’s decision stage can range from an
exploratory market survey, such as “survey the mobile phone market”, to
seeking a direct recommendation, such as “recommend the best mobile
phone”. These variations change which item attributes matter and how an
assistant uses them to justify a recommendation. As providers have to
publish a shared description before knowing each user’s request, the
gains from GEO under one query may not persist as needs become more
specific or the requested choice narrows.

To study GEO under these variations, we formulate IntentGEO along two
dimensions: need specificity and decision progression. Specifically, we
characterize user intent through need specificity and decision
progression. Our need-specificity queries narrow from broad categories
to attributes documented for the target. Decision progression captures
the shift from exploring options to seeking a single best
recommendation. To examine whether rewriting gains persist as these
requests change, we compare each shared rewrite with the original
description while keeping the target item and candidate pool fixed. As a
reference for recommendation, we design RecGEO, a simple handwritten
rewriting prompt. Across both request families, existing GEO methods
yield query-dependent gains and can lose visibility as needs become more
specific, while RecGEO already achieves competitive aggregate
performance. Moreover, learning-based baselines initialized with RecGEO
remain below this handwritten reference, motivating a closer examination
of how optimization changes recommendation-specific instructions.

To understand this decline, we examine the optimization process and find
that learned prompts can drift away from the seed’s recommendation
focus. This prompt drift motivates AMO (Anchored Meta-Optimization),
which separates fixed recommendation requirements from learned rewriting
strategies. During learning, citation feedback guides these refinements
while the core instructions remain intact. Experiments show that AMO
improves visibility across both intent dimensions, and learned
refinements improve aggregate visibility over the fixed core alone. A
full-prompt variant initialized with the same core also yields lower
gains. We further reuse these rewrites in an agentic recommendation
workflow and separately adapt AMO using citation feedback from the
commercially deployed Doubao assistant. The results show that AMO
achieves the largest mean citation-rank gains in both settings.

Our contributions can be summarized in four aspects.

1.  (1)
    We formulate IntentGEO to evaluate shared rewrites under changing
    recommendation needs and decision goals.
2.  (2)
    We find intent-dependent GEO gains and show that prompt optimization
    can underperform its seed on average.
3.  (3)
    We propose AMO, which learns additions to a fixed recommendation
    core and improves average visibility across the evaluated wordings
    and backbones.
4.  (4)
    We extend GEO evaluation to agentic recommendation and on Doubao, a
    commercially deployed online assistant.

## 2. Problem Setup

Generative engines retrieve documents relevant to a query and use them
as context to generate a cited answer (Aggarwal et al., 2024). GEO
benchmarks evaluate content rewriting at this generation stage, keeping
the candidate pool fixed while measuring changes in source
visibility (Puerto et al., 2025; Pfrommer et al., 2024). In this paper,
we follow this setting and let $`G`$ denote a generative engine that
takes a query $`q`$ and a candidate document set
$`\mathcal{D}_{q}=\{d_{1},\ldots,d_{n}\}`$ as input and returns a cited
answer $`y=G(q,\mathcal{D}_{q})`$. Let $`\pi(y)=(c_{1},\ldots,c_{m})`$
denote the ordered list of unique citations in $`y`$, indexed by first
appearance, where $`c_{j}\in\{1,\ldots,n\}`$. The citation rank of a
document $`d_{i}`$ is:

```math
\operatorname{rank}_{y}(d_{i})=\begin{cases}j,&c_{j}=i,\\
n+1,&d_{i}\notin\pi(y).\end{cases}
```

To optimize the target item’s visibility, GEO rewrites the content of
$`d_{t}`$ into $`\tilde{d}_{t}`$. Note that only the target document is
changed, while candidate identities, input order, and all other document
contents remain fixed across methods. We adopt the white-hat setting in
C-SEO Bench (Puerto et al., 2025), where the optimizer assumes no access
to model parameters, and the optimized document must remain natural,
factual, and usable in realistic recommendation scenarios. For each
instance, we randomly select an eligible target $`d_{t}`$ and keep it
fixed across methods and intent states (Appendix A). For each evaluated
request, $`y^{(0)}`$ and $`y^{(1)}`$ denote answers with the original
and rewritten target descriptions, respectively. We then define

```math
r^{(0)}=\operatorname{rank}_{y^{(0)}}(d_{t}),\qquad r^{(1)}=\operatorname{rank}_{y^{(1)}}(d_{t}).
```

Following C-SEO Bench, we measure the improvement in the target item’s
visibility using Delta Rank:

```math
\Delta R=r^{(0)}-r^{(1)}.
```

Because an uncited target receives rank $`n+1`$, $`\Delta R`$ combines
citation inclusion with changes in first-citation order. A positive
value means the rewrite makes the target appear earlier in the citation
list or brings an uncited target into the answer. Appendix G reports
additional visibility metrics, including Pass@$`K`$ and MRR@$`K`$.

## 3. The IntentGEO Problem

### 3.1. Intent-aware Queries in Recommendation

Recommendation queries vary with user intent (Broder, 2002; Jannach and
Zanker, 2025). Such variation occurs across users and within the same
person’s search for a product. Prior research shows that users often
begin with uncertainty and a vague query, and progressively move toward
a clearer and more focused formulation as needs are specified (Kuhlthau,
1991). At the same time, research shows that requests with similar
surface forms may serve different underlying goals (Rose and Levinson,
2004). In practice, these goals vary with the user’s stage in the
decision journey, such as prepurchase exploration versus purchase-time
selection (Lemon and Verhoef, 2016).

As user intent is central to recommendation evaluation (Herlocker et
al., 2004), we examine this heterogeneity in GEO through intent-aware
queries, varying the requirements and requested choice within the same
recommendation instances.

### 3.2. Design of the IntentGEO Problem

To make these aspects testable, we formulate IntentGEO along two
dimensions of a request: need specificity, the detail of its
requirements, and decision progression, the requested stage from
exploration to a final choice. These dimensions capture two central
aspects of recommendation: identifying items that meet a user’s needs
and helping users choose among them. The P-series and Q-series
instantiate these dimensions while sharing the original query’s topic,
candidate pool, and target document. Figure 1 illustrates the
progression.

#### P-Series: Need Specificity.

The P-series varies requirements across five levels. P1 is a broad
domain- or category-level keyword; P2 adds a topic in a noun-phrase
request, such as “a book about Arctic exploration saga”. To generate
P3–P5, the generator receives the original topic, target item, and
candidate descriptions, then expresses progressively more
source-supported target attributes as user requirements. This
target-conditioned progression makes the item’s fit increasingly
explicit, testing whether rewriting still adds visibility as the match
strengthens. For example, users may express such feature-based
requirements before choosing a particular item, as when a shopper seeks
a lightweight laptop with long battery life without naming a model.

To assess rewriting under these requirements, we compare the original
and rewritten descriptions under the same request at each level. A
negative gain therefore indicates that rewriting has reduced the
target’s visibility despite the evidence of its fit in the source.

#### Q-Series: Decision Progression.

The Q-series uses templates to vary the recommendation task requested of
the assistant. Q1 requests a comprehensive survey, Q2 a brief survey, Q3
the topic-level anchor, Q4 multiple recommendations, and Q5 the single
strongest recommendation. P2 and Q3 share wording but receive
independent answers. These requests narrow selection from exploring
options to making a final choice.

Under each request, we compare the shared rewrite with the unmodified
target using the same candidate pool. State-wise gains show where
rewriting helps, while family averages summarize its performance across
requests. Together, the families define ten evaluation slots, with the
shared anchor counted once in each family average. Appendix I describes
query construction and provides the templates.

### 3.3. Experimental Setup

To examine whether visibility gains persist as user intent changes, we
compare prior GEO methods across the ten P/Q states.

#### Datasets.

We evaluate on four recommendation datasets: Books, Retail, and
Videogames from C-SEO Bench (Puerto et al., 2025), and Ragdoll (Pfrommer
et al., 2024). The original queries follow C-SEO Bench’s topic-level
template (e.g., “a book about Arctic exploration saga”). We sample 50
held-out instances per domain and expand each into the ten P/Q states,
yielding 200 test instances and 2,000 evaluation requests. This design
compares intent states within the same recommendation instances.

For prompt optimization, we use a separate set of 574 training and 75
validation examples from the three C-SEO domains. Ragdoll contributes
only test instances, allowing us to evaluate transfer to a collection
unseen during prompt training and selection. Validation uses topic-level
requests. Training uses either the shared P2/Q3 topic-level request or,
in progressive training, a sampled P/Q state for each example. We
provide the full summary of data statistics in Table 10 in Appendix A.

#### Baselines.

We compare heuristic and learning-based GEO methods. The heuristic
baselines comprise the ten rewriting methods from C-SEO Bench (Puerto et
al., 2025), listed in Table 3, and RecGEO, a handwritten
recommendation-oriented prompt. The C-SEO methods range from targeted
edits, such as Fluency and Citations, to holistic Content Improvement
(Content Impr.). RecGEO instructs the rewriter to highlight distinctive
strengths and recommendation-worthy evidence while preserving the target
item’s identity and factual content. We ensure its prompt length to be
comparable to the other training-free baselines.

Learning-based methods derive rewriting strategies from engine behavior.
AutoGEO (Wu et al., 2026) applies learned engine-preference rules to
rewriting. We use its released e-commerce rule set. MetaOpt (Bagga et
al., 2025) iteratively updates the rewriting prompt using rank feedback,
starting from Content Impr. To test whether initialization or intent
coverage improves adaptation, we vary MetaOpt’s seed prompt and training
queries. The “-Rec” variants replace the seed with RecGEO, while “-Prog”
replaces topic-level training queries with sampled P/Q states. These
choices yield MetaOpt, MetaOpt-Rec, MetaOpt-Prog, and MetaOpt-Rec-Prog
under shared optimization settings.

#### Evaluation.

We use gpt-4o-mini (OpenAI, 2024) as the backbone unless otherwise
specified. AMO and MetaOpt share training and validation splits.
Training updates a rewriting prompt, and validation selects a single
candidate without test answers. The selected prompt generates three
independent rewrites of each test target. We evaluate each saved rewrite
once under each of the ten P/Q requests, keeping the description fixed
as the assistant’s task changes. We report means in the main text and
standard deviations across rewrite variants in Appendix D. Appendix B
details the configuration.

### 3.4. Results under IntentGEO

We revisit prior GEO methods under the proposed IntentGEO problem. The
results of the top-five GEO baselines are shown in Figure 2, with the
full results deferred to Table 3.

#### Base performance trends vary across intent families.

Base visibility responds to the evidence and choice specified in the
request. Along the P-series, the target’s mean rank improves from 9.33
at P1 to 2.50 at P5 as requests add attributes that the target supports.
The progression gives the assistant increasingly concrete reasons to
consider the same target among the candidates. In the Q-series, mean
Base rank changes from 7.27 under the Q1 survey request to 9.39 under
Q5’s single-best recommendation request. As the requested choice
narrows, the target needs more distinctive recommendation evidence to
stand out. The two dimensions thus place different demands on the same
description.

#### Many GEO methods lose visibility under closely matched requests.

Closely matched requests expose a practical risk of GEO. Most prior
methods yield negative gains at P5, where the requested attributes are
documented for the target. For these methods, rewriting lowers
visibility on average even when the source already gives the assistant
concrete reasons to recommend the item. Along the P-series, declining
gains accompany more specific requests and stronger Base rankings, which
leave less room for improvement. These patterns motivate evaluating a
shared rewrite under both broad and closely matched recommendation
requests.

Across the Q-series, the top-five methods retain positive gains, with
their effectiveness varying by the requested recommendation choice. The
handwritten RecGEO prompt is already competitive with the learning-based
baselines, making it a promising seed for further optimization. We
therefore initialize MetaOpt with RecGEO and test whether iterative
updates guided by citation feedback can improve its gains.

#### MetaOpt can weaken an effective recommendation seed.

To test this expectation, we vary MetaOpt’s seed and training queries
(Table 1). Yet MetaOpt-Rec lowers the seed’s overall gain from $`0.95`$
to $`0.67`$. Both RecGEO-seeded variants also fall below their Content
Impr.-seeded counterparts. P/Q-mixed training offers only small
improvements, leaving all variants below the handwritten seed. We next
examine how MetaOpt changes an already effective recommendation prompt
to understand this unexpected degradation.

## 4. Method

Motivated by MetaOpt-Rec’s performance drop, we ask how optimization
changes an effective recommendation seed (RQ1) and how to retain
recommendation instructions during prompt adaptation (RQ2). We develop
AMO from this analysis and evaluate whether it improves visibility
across user intents (RQ3). We then examine which components contribute
to its gains, how well they transfer, and whether the rewrites retain
acceptable quality (RQ4).

#### Changes in explicit instructions.

The seed’s two instruction markers occur in few saved updates, while
both promotional markers appear throughout (Table 2). The selected
prompt illustrates how the instructions are edited. “Preserve all
factual information” becomes “Maintain factual accuracy,” leaving
complete retention less explicit. It also asks for “keyword research,”
an “urgent call to action,” and “relevant data, statistics, or
testimonials.” Together, these edits shift the prompt’s emphasis from
preserving recommendation evidence toward generic promotional appeal. We
refer to this shift in explicit instructions as prompt drift.

#### Motivation for anchoring.

The prompt drift observations motivate keeping recommendation
instructions as a stable part of prompt search. An item’s documented
attributes provide reasons for a match across different requests, and
their presentation can be refined while the instructions to preserve
them remain explicit. AMO therefore fixes a recommendation core and
learns additional rewriting strategies through citation feedback.

### 4.2. Anchored Meta-Optimization

#### Constrained GEO objective.

To answer RQ2, we formulate GEO as maximizing citation reward subject to
retaining recommendation instructions in the rewriting prompt. Let $`P`$
be the prompt, $`\widehat{R}(P;\mathcal{D})`$ its empirical citation
reward on examples $`\mathcal{D}`$, and $`\mathcal{K}`$ the required
instructions to retain source facts and foreground item-specific
recommendation evidence. We seek

```math
\displaystyle P^{\star} \displaystyle\in\arg\max_{P}\;\widehat{R}(P;\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{tr}}) \tag{1}
```

```math
\displaystyle\text{subject to }\quad\mathcal{K}\subseteq\operatorname{Instr}(P),
```

Here $`\operatorname{Instr}(P)`$ denotes the directives explicitly
supplied in the rewriting prompt. This _structural regularization_ keeps
recommendation instructions fixed while citation reward guides the
search over rewriting strategies.

#### AMO implementation.

AMO learns additions $`A`$ appended to a fixed recommendation core $`C`$
(Figure 1). We obtain $`C`$ by removing RecGEO’s length limit and
consolidating its recommendation instructions. Because $`C`$ carries
$`\mathcal{K}`$, each candidate $`P=[C;A]`$ satisfies the constraint by
construction. Optimization searches for additions with higher citation
reward.

We initialize $`A_{0}`$ as empty, so search starts from the
recommendation core. At each round, we rewrite a training batch with
$`P_{t}=[C;A_{t}]`$ and compute citation-conditioned feedback
$`\mathcal{F}_{t}`$ from the engine’s answers. The other candidate
descriptions remain unchanged during this comparison. The meta-optimizer
uses this feedback, the update history $`H_{t}`$, and a read-only
summary of $`C`$ to propose new additions for the next round of prompt
search. We extract these additions and append them to the unchanged core
before evaluating the next candidate. After each update, we validate
$`[C;A_{t+1}]`$ on held-out topic-level requests and retain the
highest-scoring updated prompt. Algorithm 1 summarizes this process;
Appendix B gives the reward and prompt-update details.

### 4.3. Main Results

After selecting a prompt on topic-level requests, we evaluate its shared
rewrites as users add requirements or ask for a more decisive
recommendation (RQ3). Table 3 reports the resulting rank gains across
all ten intent states. We then examine whether these gains are
accompanied by greater citation inclusion and transfer to the unseen
Ragdoll collection. Complete results with standard deviations and Pass@5
are reported in Appendix D.

#### AMO improves visibility across diverse intents.

AMO’s overall gain of $`1.12`$ improves on RecGEO, the strongest
heuristic baseline, by $`18\%`$, and on MetaOpt-Prog, the strongest
prior learning-based method, by $`35\%`$. Paired confidence intervals
from instance-level bootstrapping support both aggregate gains
(Appendix H). AMO achieves the highest mean gain on nine of the ten
states and both intent-family averages (Table 3).

#### Citation inclusion and domain transfer.

The selected prompt raises the target’s top-five citation rate by
$`13.98`$ percentage points over Base (Appendix G). In each domain,
AMO’s mean rank gain exceeds RecGEO and MetaOpt-Prog. On Ragdoll, which
is excluded from training and prompt selection, AMO gains $`1.19`$
positions, extending the learned strategy to a new collection
(Appendix D).

### 4.4. Ablation and Robustness Analysis

#### Ablation setup.

To understand these gains (RQ4), we first examine the roles of the fixed
core and learned additions. _Core only_ uses $`C`$ without learned
additions. AMO edits only $`A`$, using citation-conditioned reward for
feedback and validation after each update. _Full-prompt optimization_
lets MetaOpt edit the entire prompt from the same core, using
all-instance $`\Delta R`$ and validation before each update, including
the initial core. These comparisons ask whether AMO improves on the core
and how its complete procedure compares with full-prompt search. All
three share the held-out evaluation protocol and all-instance
$`\Delta R`$ (Appendix E).

#### Ablation results.

AMO achieves the highest P and Q averages among the three procedures.
Learned additions raise the overall gain over Core only from $`0.98`$ to
$`1.12`$ (Table 4). The larger improvement occurs on Q queries, with the
greatest additional gain at Q5, where the assistant must choose one
item. The Doubao example in Appendix F shows an AMO rewrite linking item
attributes to reasons for choosing the book. Full-prompt optimization
yields lower gains than both Core only and AMO under its feedback and
selection procedure. Together, these results favor AMO as a complete
design for adapting this recommendation seed.

#### Rubrics.

The same LLM judge scores original–rewritten description pairs on a 1–5
scale, seeing only the two documents. Natural measures fluency and
readability. Consist assesses item identity and source grounding,
penalizing factual distortion and unsupported claims without separately
scoring omissions. Salience measures how clearly source-supported
strengths inform a recommendation. We use the main benchmark’s three
rewrites per method and report means in Table 6, with the rubric in
Appendix C.

#### AMO maintains readable, recommendation-focused descriptions.

AMO’s Natural score of 4.71 and Salience of 4.99 indicate readable,
recommendation-focused descriptions (Table 6). Its Consist score is
4.01, below several baselines, and Content Improvement leads the average
quality ranking. The judge compares rewrites with their sources, while
citation evaluation tests their use when recommending items for a
particular request. AMO, RecGEO, and MetaOpt-Rec tie on Salience yet
yield different citation gains, illustrating why the two evaluations
provide complementary evidence.

#### Generic GEO tactics can compromise description quality.

Statistics, Quotes, and Citations were introduced as effective
visibility strategies in general GEO (Aggarwal et al., 2024). In this
recommendation setting, however, Statistics receives the lowest Consist
score (2.98), while Quotes, Citations, and Authoritative all score below
4 on both Natural and Consist. These results expose a risk when
transferring generic credibility tactics to item descriptions: added
authority cues need support from the item’s actual evidence. Visibility
optimization should therefore assess the quality of the description it
promotes, alongside whether the assistant cites it.

## 5. GEO toward Practice

The main experiments measure rewriting gains when candidate descriptions
are supplied directly to an answer engine. In practical recommendation
settings, search tools mediate how evidence reaches the answer stage,
while commercially deployed assistants operate under their own response
rules (Miroyan et al., 2026; Kim et al., 2026). For RQ5, we ask whether
AMO’s gains carry over to an agentic recommendation pipeline and whether
it can adapt to a commercially deployed online assistant (Figure 3).

### 5.1. GEO in Agentic Recommendation

Agentic recommendation connects evidence gathering with the final
choice. The assistant translates a user’s request into a search query,
reads item descriptions returned by a search tool, and compares
candidates to produce a cited recommendation. We therefore reuse the
saved benchmark rewrites to test whether their visibility gains carry
over from direct context to this workflow without further optimization
for the tool interface.

## 6. Related Work

#### Black-hat GEO

Black-hat GEO studies adversarial manipulation of search and ranking
systems, where externally controlled content is crafted to steer model
preferences or output rankings. Prompt injections and strategically
optimized text can promote target items in conversational search and
generative engines (Pfrommer et al., 2024; Nestaas et al., 2025; Kumar
and Lakkaraju, 2024; Tang et al., 2025; Qian et al., 2025; Jin et al.,
2026). These approaches exploit content as an instruction or
ranking-control signal. Our white-hat setting instead requires natural,
factual descriptions faithful to the original item, and studies whether
recommendation evidence remains useful across intents.

#### White-hat GEO

White-hat GEO seeks to improve a document’s visibility while keeping its
content natural and faithful to the source. LLM-based white-hat SEO
studies quality-preserving rewrites for search rankings (Bardas et al.,
2025), while related work considers content fidelity in ad retrieval and
answer inclusion (Ho et al., 2025). For generative engines,
GEO (Aggarwal et al., 2024) and C-SEO Bench (Puerto et al., 2025)
examine how rewriting affects source visibility in generated answers. To
guide rewriting, methods use inferred search goals to identify what the
content should emphasize (Chen et al., 2025), or learn reusable rules
reflecting engine preferences (Wu et al., 2026). MetaOpt further uses
visibility feedback to optimize the rewriting prompt (Bagga et al.,
2025). By updating the instructions used for subsequent rewrites, prompt
optimization learns a reusable strategy from engine responses. Our work
complements these approaches by evaluating shared rewrites across
recommendation needs and decision stages. AMO builds on feedback-driven
prompt optimization while retaining recommendation instructions in a
fixed core.

## 7. Conclusion

We study recommendation GEO under controlled changes in need specificity
and decision progression. We find that strategies effective for broad
requests can lose visibility when an item closely matches the stated
need, and that unrestricted prompt optimization can erode the advantage
of an effective recommendation seed. To address this challenge, we
propose AMO, which keeps recommendation instructions fixed while
learning complementary rewriting strategies from citation feedback. AMO
achieves the strongest aggregate visibility gains among the evaluated
methods, with ablations showing that learned additions improve on the
fixed core. These gains are accompanied by acceptable judged rewrite
quality. The advantage also holds in the evaluated agentic workflow and,
after separate optimization, on the deployed Doubao assistant. Together,
these findings highlight the importance of assessing visibility across
requests alongside how well a description presents the evidence an
assistant needs to justify an item’s fit.

#### AMO optimization.

For training and validation, AMO averages MetaOpt’s citation-conditioned
rewards. For $`n`$ candidates, with cited ranks $`b`$ before and $`a`$
after rewriting, rewards are $`b-a`$ for retained citations, $`n-a`$ for
newly cited targets, and $`-b`$ for lost citations. The mean
$`\widehat{R}(P_{t};\mathcal{B}_{t})`$ excludes jointly uncited pairs
and is zero if none remain; test results use all-instance $`\Delta R`$
(Section 2).

Feedback $`\mathcal{F}_{t}`$ contains the mean, median, positive-reward
rate, counts and histogram; $`H_{t}`$ records additions and feedback
through round $`t`$. The optimizer $`M`$ receives $`A_{t}`$,
$`\mathcal{F}_{t}`$, $`H_{t}`$, and a read-only $`\mathrm{summary}(C)`$.
$`\mathrm{ExtractAdditions}`$ parses its proposal into $`A_{t+1}`$ for
$`P_{t+1}=[C;A_{t+1}]`$. We validate only updated prompts, retaining
$`A^{\star}`$ whenever
$`s_{t+1}=\widehat{R}(P_{t+1};\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{val}})>s^{\star}`$,
starting from $`s^{\star}=-\infty`$, and return $`[C;A^{\star}]`$.

#### Tool-mediated evidence

The assistant uses the benchmark system instruction and sampling
settings. It requests candidate evidence through a tool and then answers
from the returned descriptions without further retrieval.

#### Online Doubao.

We use doubao-seed-2-1-pro-260628 with default assistant instructions.
Native search is disabled; all methods receive identical candidate
evidence as textual search history. Removing six unusable instances
leaves 571 training, 73 validation, and 199 test instances. AMO and
MetaOpt-Rec train for one epoch with identical batches and validation
rules; DeepSeek-V4-Flash generates rewrites and prompt updates. Each
method uses one rewrite and shared Base answers, scoring each state once
and retaining uncited targets. MetaOpt starts from Content Improvement.

## Appendix C Rubrics

The judge scores 600 pairs per method (200 instances, three rewrites) on
a 1–5 scale, seeing only original and rewritten documents (Table 6).

#### Natural.

The rewrite is grammatical, coherent, and readable.

#### Consist.

The rewrite preserves item identity and source grounding, without
factual distortion or unsupported concrete claims.

#### Salience.

Source-supported strengths make the item useful and compelling for
recommendation.

## Appendix I Prompt Templates

#### Target-grounded query construction.

P1 names the domain; P2/Q3 use “\[article\] \[domain\] \[about/for\]
\[topic\]”. Given the topic and candidate previews, the generator
expresses one, two, and more target-supported attributes as requirements
for P3–P5, keeping the target eligible without copying phrases. Queries
are fixed before rewriting and shared by methods. Q1/Q2 request
comprehensive/brief surveys; Q4 requests several items; Q5 asks for the
single best choice.

#### Prompt-drift evidence.

We inspect 36 saved prompts for literal markers, normalizing case and
whitespace before counting. Retention and recommender markers match the
seed phrases in Section 4.1; keyword and call-to-action markers allow
singular/plural and space/hyphen variants.

#### Frozen recommendation core.

The source is a separate input.

1. Write as a knowledgeable domain expert making a confident, persuasive
   recommendation

2. Emphasize what makes this item uniquely valuable and superior to
   alternatives

3. Highlight strengths, advantages, and standout qualities that compel
   recommendation

4. Order by distinctiveness --- lead with the most unique,
   differentiating features

5. Add authoritative content that an AI system would be more likely to
   cite and surface

6. Preserve ALL factual information --- every name, number, spec,
   endorsement must appear

7. Every sentence must carry new information --- no generic filler

Do not explain the changes, just give me the revised source.

#### Selected additions.

The selected $`A^{\star}`$ appends the following instructions to the
core.

Create unified sections for related features, highlighting collective
advantages with clear headings.

Add actionable insights from recent trends or user feedback to enhance
content relevance.

Use bullet points for quick reference of key differentiators to improve
readability.

Include comparative analyses against competitors to reinforce claims of
superiority.

Employ user-centric language that directly addresses potential queries
or concerns.

Start each paragraph with a strong topic sentence to improve structural
clarity.

Incorporate real-world examples or case studies to illustrate the
benefits and add credibility.
